## The Ground of Aim

There are two opposite ways to evade responsibility under uncertainty. One trusts numerical dashboards to tame the future; the other uses uncertainty as an excuse to betray agreements once conditions shift. Both confuse measuring what sits finished before our eyes with taking careful aim before certainty arrives.

The Greeks called aiming at a mark **stókhos** (target or conjecture) and the conjectural art itself *stokhastikē*. Plato's *Philebus* (55e–56c) ranked conjectural crafts below exact counting, measuring, and weighing (Plato c. 360 BCE), yet real action runs inside **kairós**—the fleeting, decisive moment (Hippocrates c. 400 BCE)—that closes within irreversible time before exact measurement arrives (Georgescu-Roegen 1971).

Although Greek gives us these technical names, the predicament itself is planetary. Ptahhotep's *Instruction* (preserved in Egypt's Middle Kingdom Prisse Papyrus) warned that no craft ever reaches the end of its skill (Lichtheim 1973). Nahuatl *Huehuetlahtolli* taught that **tlalticpac**—our existence upon the earth—is a slippery ridge that demands balanced footing (Anderson & Dibble 1969; Burkhart 1989). Sunzi insisted on securing one's own ground against defeat before waiting for an opening (Griffith 1963). In the Andes, Aymara temporal grammar places the visible past (*nayra*, front or eye) before one's eyes and the unseen future (*qhipa*, behind or back) behind one's back (Núñez & Sweetser 2006), condensed in the compound maxim **Qhipnayra**—walking forward while keeping the past in view (Rivera Cusicanqui 2012). Along the same insight, Māori *Whakataukī* pictures honest foresight as walking backwards into the future with eyes fixed on the past (Rameka 2016). Every word spoken to another person and every choice made for a group enters that irreversible interval and changes the world we will have to read tomorrow.

No one takes aim outside history. The bow, the records, and the reserves at our disposal grow out of material life, the social division of labor, and everyday practices of mutual aid from below (Marx & Engels 1845/1932; Kropotkin 1902). We make our own choices, yet always under inherited conditions we did not freely select (Marx 1852). Every present stance carries in the body, in institutions, and in language the traces and conflicts of past decisions. As Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* insist, the truth of what we say is settled in practice rather than in abstract verbal disputes, tested by the real effects it produces in common life (Marx & Engels 1845/1932).

## The Mirror and the Measure

We often remain trapped in the mirror of other people's gaze and try to shape both who we are and the image we return to the world. Demanding that the portrait formed in someone else's mind match our exact wish is a quiet punishment we inflict upon ourselves. Being understood is different from controlling how others understand us.

The ancient Stoic image of the archer separates two things anxiety blurs: the internal duty to aim well—grounded in Antipater's procedural end (**télos**) of doing everything in one's power continuously, preserved alongside the Stoic distinction between **télos** and target (**skopós**) in Stobaeus (1884, *Ecl.* II.76–77, distinct from the earlier Peripatetic *teûxis* definition at II.47.7–11), and sharply drawn as internal aiming against hitting an external mark in Cicero's archer simile and Plutarch's critique (Cicero 45 BCE, *De Finibus* III.22; Plutarch c. 100 CE, *De comm. not.* 1070f–1071e)—and hitting that external mark, which in life as in flight faces crosswinds after release. Parallel disciplines arose far from Athens. The *Bhagavad Gītā* separates the agent's custody over disciplined action from any claim over its external fruits (Radhakrishnan 1948), while Sunzi separates self-secured readiness from the contingency that lies outside one's hand (Griffith 1963). In dialogue, clarity, presence, and candor lie within our care; the impression left in the listener belongs to the arrow's flight. Once spoken, a word passes through another person's history, social position, and limits. Trying to command reception asks the arrow to keep obeying the hand after release.

When we forget that boundary, shared life turns into surveillance. In institutions just as in intimate life, whenever a measure turns into a target of control, it corrupts the living reality it was meant to report (Goodhart 1975; Campbell 1979; Strathern 1997). Relations among people harden into autonomous scoreboards that come to govern their own makers (Marx 1867), while administrative grids built for legibility from above strip away practical local know-how (Scott 1998). Spontaneous gesture, unmeasured care, and candid listening give way to impression management. Demanding that another person mirror our will without noise erases the distinctness of the one who listens (Adorno 1966) and flattens into averages everything that escapes our schemes (Georgescu-Roegen 1971).

Meeting this encounter on firm ground avoids two symmetrical mistakes. The first reduces the other person to an inert tool—denying each mind's inalienable freedom to think and judge alongside each person's natural right to exist and act without mutual harm (Spinoza 2007 [1670])—and treats another's margin of existence as a mere means to buy our comfort (Kant 1996 [1785]). The second mistakes every disagreement for enmity. Ordinary tensions among equals belong to the movement of shared life—a distinction between non-antagonistic differences among peers and open enmity (Mao 1965–1977 [1957])—and call for patience and open argument rather than unilateral power to brand dissenters as enemies and turn disagreement into a purge. The health of an organism does not lie in matching a rigid average; it lives in the leeway kept to withstand a shifting environment (Canguilhem 1943/1966). In life with others, that leeway means accepting the gap between who we are and how we are read, without despair and without giving up honest speech. Even when two claims clash directly, encountering an unresolved contradiction need not collapse honest reasoning into anything-goes cynicism—a boundary Latin American logic formalized by separating localized contradiction from the trivialization of thought (da Costa 1980).

Giving up forced unanimity preserves clarity instead of weakening it. Genuine understanding grows out of an encounter between two freedoms, far from the anxious custody of a guarded portrait. When we return to others the right to see from where they stand, we recover the lightness of existing without staging the spectacle of our own reception.

## Fouled Air

Respecting the freedom of another's gaze ceases to suffice once shared life is overtaken by bad faith, factional monopoly over who counts as an enemy, and predation. Sometimes the gap between what we do and how we are read comes from deliberate tampering: filtering records at the gate, hiding inconvenient facts, and steering group perception toward public scapegoating or self-sabotage.

In an honest shot, wind deflects the arrow in flight. In manipulation, the air is fouled before release. Coercive competition and enclosure strip shared reserves into private gain while leaving society only collective debt (Marx 1867). Centralized state and bureaucratic command can likewise expropriate mutual-aid buffers from above and entrench a ruling caste of its own (Bakunin 1873/1990; Kropotkin 1902). In both forms, rulers pocket calm-weather gains, unload crisis losses onto the common ledger, and silence whoever guards reserves before storms arrive. As soon as dishonesty threatens to surface, the manufacture of a scapegoat begins (Girard 1986 [1982]). Someone at the most exposed edge absorbs collective anger until the group believes it is doing justice while shielding the fraudster or marching toward its own ruin.

When speech stops mediating encounter and serves as an ambush, the floor of common life gives way. In intimacy and along chains of work, contradictory signals corner the listener: loyalty is feigned in words while landmarks of reality are twisted in practice (Bateson 1972/2000). Worse, the lower-status voice that points out the fault meets instant dismissal (Fricker 2007)—forgetting Ptahhotep's ancient reminder that discerning speech hides like greenstone yet is still found among maids at the grindstones (Lichtheim 1973). Little by little, the person who raised the alarm begins to doubt personal judgment and falls ill from exhaustion while offices above assemble a paper alibi. In public life, breaking sworn promises when blame seeks an owner and judging conduct by sheer outcome luck (Baron & Hershey 1988; Nagel 1979; Williams 1981)—rather than grading epistemic aptness against lucky accuracy (Sosa 2007)—destroy the few shelters human promise can build against an uncertain future (Arendt 1958).

Without protection against falsehood, the faculty of judgment itself decays (Weil 1952 [1949]). Against organized fraud, good intentions achieve little without material safeguards. Commitments and criteria must be sealed before outcomes appear, lest anyone rewrite tomorrow what was promised yesterday (Arendt 1958). Every organization also needs a direct **algedonic** channel—a distress-and-pain bypass—carrying urgent warnings straight from the base to oversight without rank censorship (Beer 1972), guarded against both alarm floods and permanent emergency rule. Answering proven betrayal bears no resemblance to a lynching in reverse (Girard 1986 [1982]). It means restoring the facts, holding accountable those who held real decision-making power, and stripping imposture of silence and false symmetry (Weil 1952 [1949]).

Relinquishing control over another's reception never licenses silent witness to deceit. We must separate the honest reading of a listener in good faith from the cowardice of those who burn the innocent to cover their own tracks. Before orders built on false or cruel premises, refusal stands higher than technical obedience. That does not mean silent exit abandoning the injured; it means breaking complicity, returning the stolen bow as Neoptolemus does in *Philoctetes* (Sophocles 409 BCE), and contesting the corrupt rule openly.

## The Seat of Direction

There remains a domain in which capture dispenses with open lying because it is already built into the design of questions and the control of time. Anyone occupying a seat of direction never speaks from neutral ground. Priorities, metrics, and deadlines shape the mental horizon of everyone nearby, deciding which harms enter the ledger and which vanish outside the table. Every manager is a cognitive influencer by duty, yet becomes a cognitive manipulator by choice.

Within organizations, authority acts first upon decision premises, setting what the group notices and how problems may be framed (Simon 1997 [1947]). Yet the bureaucratic cost borne by an official at the top rarely matches the harm suffered downstream. Honest coordination must place both stakes on the table, admit the scale of what remains unknown, and account for collateral damage a narrow target hides. Otherwise, one solves the wrong question with impeccable precision (Kimball 1957). Self-enclosed offices fail right here: without close investigation of lived reality, no one holds a legitimate right to speak on a question (Mao 1965–1977 [1930]). Honest synthesis gathers scattered frontline experience without convenient omissions and returns it to the group for the test of practice (Mao 1965–1977 [1943]). When historical ground shifts, repeating yesterday's formulas is blindness—as *"Left-Wing" Communism* and the *Philosophical Notebooks* insist (Lenin 1975 [1920]; Lenin 1976 [1914–1916])—whereas inventing permanent emergencies to govern by stampede is usurpation. Anarchist critique adds a firm boundary no vanguard or specialist body may cross: technical skill earns freely tested counsel rather than coercive command, because crowning any academy or directorate over living people corrupts both knowledge and governors through privilege (Bakunin 1882/1970 [1871]).

Once that boundary is crossed, coordination turns into the engineering of consent. Open understanding sought through shared reasons gives way to hidden strategic calculation (Habermas 1984 [1981]). When leaders withhold or skew information, workers are made to reason inside a maze built to lead to a single exit, creating an illusion of free agreement where capture has already occurred. Clear compacts dissolve into convenient ambiguity, silent rule changes, and risk shifted onto people denied both voice and consent (Scanlon 1998). Steering people through mental traps reduces another's intelligence to a disposable part in a machine sustained by their own wear and tear, without permission to understand or contest it (Kant 1996 [1785]).

Directing common life serves less to restrain bodies through fear than to secure the free exercise of reason (Spinoza 2007 [1670]). Its purpose is to let each person act with vital power free of deceit, through horizontal federation, mutual aid, and direct stewardship of shared goods (Proudhon 1863/1979; Kropotkin 1902; Ostrom 1990).

Accepting the duty to guide rules out the calculation to manipulate. As anarchism long insisted against authoritarian shortcuts, means cannot be severed from ends: methods built on coercion or hidden tutelage reproduce domination rather than free cooperation (Malatesta 1891/2014). Legitimate coordination rests on revocable compacts and mandated delegates accountable to those who live with the results (Proudhon 1863/1979; Bakunin 1873/1990). When coordinators refuse invisible tutelage, an organization stops acting as a shadow theater and returns to cooperation among whole minds.

## The Weakest Link and the Crossing

From dialogue to institutional governance, whenever choices are irreversible, threaten ruin, or shift harm onto neighbors, a material rule holds: what sustains common life cannot be averaged away (Leontief 1941). A water treatment plant with spotless paperwork and an empty chlorine tank stands exposed to disaster. Technical polish cannot offset an exhausted reserve once irreversible time runs down (Georgescu-Roegen 1971); abundance cannot cleanse a betrayed word, nor internal efficiency excuse pushing ruin onto someone else. At the decisive hour, when delay is fatal, wisdom lies in grasping the critical link before the chain snaps (Lenin 1975 [1902/1918]).

Beneath every serious choice lies a prior ethical refusal: never treat whoever bears the impact of action as a disposable remainder (Kant 1996 [1785]; Scanlon 1998). Three non-tradeable safeguards rest on that ground:

- **Investigate the real world honestly**, without hiding who went uncounted or faking certainty where doubt remains.
- **Keep real slack** in time, health, and reserves against sudden shocks and slow wear that averages erase.
- **Bind power to its consequences**, sealing commitments before the wind rises and keeping listening open to the base.

Whenever that ethical refusal is abandoned or any one of those three safeguards gives way, the others lose their footing—through the sacrifice of another's life, blind aim, exhaustion without reserve, or unpunished plunder.

Even safeguards sicken when turned into hollow ritual or a mere defense of things as they stand. Living on uninterrupted guard drains human strength before physical stocks run dry and locks society into yesterday's molds. Knowledge advances by acting, colliding with reality, and acting again on wider ground, since only those willing to transform the world truly come to know it (Lenin 1976 [1914–1916]; Mao 1965–1977 [1937]). Managing an unjust order with accounting caution achieves little while that order keeps maiming people every day rather than changing it (Marx & Engels 1845/1932). Yet change cannot postpone freedom to a future seizure of command; it must practice free association and mutual aid in the very forms it builds today (Malatesta 1891/2014; Kropotkin 1902). At certain junctures, prevailing normality chokes life (Canguilhem 1943/1966), and standing still already sides with harm. When real impact shatters outworn maps, honesty demands admitting what failed and recovering overlooked counsel—in the spirit of the Akan **Sankofa** maxim (Twi oral tradition; Gyekye 1996; Wiredu 1996), going back to fetch what was forgotten rather than burying it under taboo. It means setting new landmarks forward in the public record without erasing prior sworn promises, and owning responsibility for what is built.

Integrity in speech asks for little artifice and substantial ballast. It asks us to aim well from lived ground, free from the vanity of the mirror and the fetish of targets. It asks us to separate loyal disagreement from ambush, defend human labor, common reserves, and sworn agreements against scapegoating, and refuse every form of direction built on deceit. When those commitments walk together in practice, uncertainty ceases to serve as a pretext for arbitrariness, and human speech regains its weight in history.
