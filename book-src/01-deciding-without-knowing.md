> Opening chapter of Part I, and the entry point for a reader with no mathematics beyond school
> arithmetic. Nothing here requires a formula. The formal version is
> [What Judging Is, and What It Is Not](27-what-judging-is.md).

## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Tell a question about *what is the case* from a question about *what to do*, and say why the second one cannot be answered by waiting for the first.
2. Explain, with an ordinary example, why a decision costs something on both sides, acting and not acting, and compute the threshold $p^\ast = C / (\eta L)$ that sets the bar.
3. Say what this book is about in one sentence, without using the word *uncertainty*.

## 1. Two kinds of question

Some questions ask what is true. *How tall is that building? How many students are in the room?*
These have answers. The answers can be checked, and if two people disagree at least one of them is wrong.

Other questions ask what to do. *Should I take the umbrella? Should I answer this message now or
tomorrow? Should the school cancel the trip on Thursday?* These have answers too, but of a stranger
kind. Two people can look at the same sky, disagree about the umbrella, and both be right, because they
are not only asking about the sky. One of them has a long walk home. The other has a lift.

A flood director or an emergency physician at 07:50 faces the second kind, and what she lacks is not data in general but three
particular things, one for each plane of the circuit this book keeps returning to. In the **World**, the
storm has not finished. In the **Archive**, the records are late and full of holes: Keynes, separating
probability from the weight of evidence (Keynes 1921), put the general point, that an archive can be
enormous and still hold nothing to compare against. The standard case is an evaluation sample with no
treated worker in it at all; more rows will not put one there, because the arm is missing rather than
small. In the **Institution**, the official who pulls the lever is rarely the household downstream that
takes the loss (Rudner 1953; Douglas 2000).

The second kind of question is the subject of this book. It is not the first kind done badly. It is a
different question, with habits of its own.

## 2. The umbrella problem

You are leaving the house at 07:50. The sky is grey, the bus goes at 08:00, and the radar will not say
whether the squall hits your street until 09:00. Do you take the umbrella?

Think about what the two mistakes cost. Take it and it stays dry, and you have carried something
useless for a few hours: a cost of action $C$. Leave it and it pours, and you spend the afternoon wet
and cold: a loss of unbuffered inaction $L$. An umbrella of protective efficacy $\eta \in (0, 1]$, where $\eta = 1$
is complete cover and lower values mean windblown rain down your neck, leaves you exposed to $(1 - \eta) L$.

Those two costs are seldom the same size, and that is the whole of the decision. Write $p$ for your own
assessment of the chance of rain. Then

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Loss}(\text{Act})] = C + (1 - \eta) p L, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Loss}(\text{Wait})] = p L$$

and you act when acting costs less in expectation than waiting:

$$C + (1 - \eta) p L < p L \iff C < \eta p L \iff p > p^\ast = \frac{C}{\eta L}$$

Read the fraction once and it explains itself. $C$ is upstairs because you pay it $100\%$ of the time you act, rain or
shine. $L$ and $\eta$ are downstairs because the umbrella only removes part of the soaking: what you
still absorb is $(1 - \eta) p L$, so the harm actually avoided is $p L - (1 - \eta) p L = \eta p L$.

Both ends of that formula are worth having in your head. Send the cost of carrying toward zero and the
threshold follows it down: $C \to 0^+ \implies p^\ast \to 0^+$, so any chance of rain at all justifies
the umbrella, and the expected value of waiting for perfect information collapses to nothing
($\mathrm{EVPI} \to 0$). The boundary is held just inside the interval rather than at it, which is
Cromwell's rule: a probability of exactly zero, or exactly one, is a commitment no finite archive can
license.

The other end is sharper than it looks. Push the mitigated harm up to twice the cost,
$\eta L \to 2C \implies p^\ast \to 1/2$, and waiting until rain is more likely than not, $p > 0.50$,
becomes rational, but at that point and no earlier: $p^\ast = \frac{C}{\eta L} = \frac{1}{2} \iff \eta L = 2C$,
where the harm avoided, $\eta L$, is exactly twice the cost paid. A shield that merely saves more than it costs is not enough. Where
$\eta L > C$ the trigger still sits above $50\%$, and where $\eta L \le C$ it passes one altogether:
$\eta L \le C \implies p^\ast \ge 1$, so preventive action never pays, not even under certainty
($p = 1$). The boundary sits at $p^\ast \ge 1$ exactly, and nothing below it buys cover.

**You act when the chance of the bad thing exceeds the cost of acting divided by the loss you would
suffer by not acting, scaled by efficacy.** With $C = 1$, $L = 20$ and $\eta = 1$ the threshold is
$p^\ast = 1 / 20 = 0.05$, so at a 5% chance of rain you take the umbrella and are right to. Someone with a
car and an underground garage faces the same sky and the opposite arithmetic, with $C$ as annoyance
and $L \approx 0$, and is equally right to ignore it.

The threshold has a name and a near neighbour. As a cost–loss ratio, $p^\ast = C / (\eta L)$, it is
Murphy's (1973). The clinical
version is Pauker and Kassirer's (1975), $p^\ast\_{\mathrm{PK}} = \frac{C\_{\mathrm{FP}}}{C\_{\mathrm{FP}} + L\_{\mathrm{FN}}}$,
and the two are not the same animal: the first prices an upfront intervention of fixed cost $C$ paid
across all states, the second prices a false-positive treatment cost $C\_{\mathrm{FP}}$ paid only when the
threat is absent ($1 - p$), against a false-negative harm $L\_{\mathrm{FN}}$ paid when it is present ($p$).
Mixing them is an arithmetic error, and a later chapter takes some trouble to keep them apart.

Two things about this are uncomfortable. There is no correct answer to the umbrella question in general,
only a correct answer *given* what each mistake costs the person deciding (Harding 1991). Two people
arguing about the umbrella without saying what getting wet costs them are not having the same argument,
however alike their sky. And the number the decision turns on is one nobody has: $p$, the true chance of
rain. You never find out whether you had it right. You find out whether it rained, and the habit of
confusing those two is the most common mistake in this book.

## 3. Why you cannot wait

You can wait until you know, you say. The bus goes at eight. The moment for deciding turns up before the
information does.

That is the shape of nearly every decision worth the name: the exam is marked before you learn whether
the method worked, and what you chose to study was chosen years before you found out what the world
wanted. A septic patient treated only after the culture comes back turns a treatable infection into
irreversible organ failure (Wong et al. 2021). The Hippocratic writers had a word for the window,
**καιρός** (*kairós*), the moment that does not wait for certainty, and an agent that waits for point
certainty before provisioning, rather than buffering, is ruined by the first shock it declined to wait
for. The ruin is absorbing. The later shifts in which its estimate would have improved never arrive.

The opposite error teaches as much. A staged, reversible choice can be taken for an irreversible
lock-in, and a narrow proxy can be solved while the spillover across its boundary goes unmodelled: a
**Type III error** (Kimball 1957), where the focal gain is real and measurable, the spillover is larger,
and the sum is a net loss that the narrow scoreboard books as a win. Where an action can be reversed, or
salvaged for recovery value $r \in [0, C)$ when the threat does not materialise, the false-positive cost
falls from $C$ to $C - r$ and the threshold moves to

$$p^\ast\_{\mathrm{rev}}(r) = \frac{C - r}{\eta L - r} \qquad (0 \le r < C < \eta L)$$

That formula has two ends as well. Let $r \to 0^+$, strict irreversibility, and
$p^\ast\_{\mathrm{rev}}(0) = C / (\eta L)$, which is where we started. Let $r \to C^-$, reversal nearly
free (staging an initial probe before committing the fleet, say), and $p^\ast\_{\mathrm{rev}}(r) \to 0^+$:
the fractional probe beats passive waiting and irreversible lock-in both, and it beats them for a reason
that has nothing to do with the forecast.

The stance has an old name, and it comes from archery. Antipater of Tarsus held that the archer's
internal duty (**τέλος**, *télos*: standing correctly, drawing the string, measuring the wind, aiming
with care) is complete the moment the arrow leaves the bow, while the hit itself, **σκοπός**, *skopós*,
belongs to the crosswinds after release. What you did and what happened to it are two things, judged by
two measures, and they do not convert into one another. The same line runs through conversation
([Chapter 27](27-what-judging-is.md)), where speaking honestly is the *télos* and managing how the
listener receives it is the *skopós*: the margin of tolerance in a conversation shrinks as surveillance
of the other's reception grows. That substitution of a proxy for the thing itself is the one pattern
this book keeps finding in institutions.

## 4. What this book is about

This book is about getting better at the second kind of question: deciding before you can know, and being
honest afterwards about what you decided and why.

It is a real skill. It can be learned, and it has more structure than it looks: some of it arithmetic,
which the later chapters supply. The opening chapters need only that you notice three ordinary
difficulties: you cannot see the future, the records you rely on are incomplete, and the people who
measure you can be changed by the measuring.

Those three are not a list I chose. They are the three planes of the mediation circuit this book runs on,
**Archive**, **World**, **Institution**, and each carries a safeguard that cannot be bought with the
other two:

1. **Archive and Epistemic Margin:** Records are finite, clustered, and filtered before anyone decides on them. An honest decision maker therefore refuses point dogmatism and tracks both the threshold $p^\ast = C / (\eta L)$ and the width of what the archive leaves unidentified. A million rows collected through one biased door carry the information of a few hundred draws. The design effect and the effective sample size make that exact later on.
2. **World and Vital Margin:** Physical time runs while we deliberate (**kairós**) and losses compound nonlinearly. An honest system keeps slack and recovery speed rather than pricing survival on a static average.
3. **Institution and Governance:** Budgets routinely separate the official who pays $C$ from the citizen who suffers $L$ (write the two thresholds of a single forecast side by side and the divergence is visible), and hindsight routinely confuses outcome luck (**skopós**) with ex-ante competence (**télos**). Governance therefore has to align liability with exposure, and protect the warning while the window is still open.

### The same forecast, two different losers

The forecaster and the resident of the floodplain read the same number and act differently. Both are
right. They face the same cost of acting $C$ and the same efficacy $\eta$; what separates them is the
loss $L$ inside the threshold,

$$
p^\ast = \frac{C}{\eta\mkern3mu L},
$$

Let $L\_{\mathrm{inst}}$ be the loss the city bears and $L\_{\mathrm{cid}}$ the loss the household bears,
with $L\_{\mathrm{inst}} < L\_{\mathrm{cid}}$. Then $p^\ast\_{\mathrm{inst}} = C/(\eta L\_{\mathrm{inst}})$
and $p^\ast\_{\mathrm{cid}} = C/(\eta L\_{\mathrm{cid}})$, and because the household's denominator is
larger its threshold is lower. Any forecast probability inside
$(p^\ast\_{\mathrm{cid}},\mkern3mu p^\ast\_{\mathrm{inst}})$ has the household protecting itself and the
city not.

The width of that interval measures nothing about the weather. It measures who bears the loss, and it
exists only between the two thresholds: below both, both wait; above both, both act; inside it the same
number means two different things. Raise the institutional loss and $p^\ast\_{\mathrm{inst}}$ falls toward
$p^\ast\_{\mathrm{cid}}$, the window narrows, and nothing about the weather has changed. Pauker and
Kassirer formalized the therapeutic threshold as a cost ratio in 1975 (Pauker & Kassirer 1975). What this
chapter adds is that the ratio contains the loss of whoever pays it. The same arithmetic explains a file
that looks complete and is not: a comparison sample with no treated unit in it cannot answer the causal
question at all, which is the distance between the two kinds of question the chapter opened with.

### When the thesis stops holding

"One cannot wait" is a claim about irreversible decisions, and it fails where the decision is reversible.
If reversing the protective action costs less than the expected loss it avoids, waiting dominates acting,
not indefinitely but as a fractional probe: buy part of the protection, read the outcome, keep the
option to complete or abandon. A reversible decision is priced by its reversal cost, not by its estimated
probability, and this chapter's claim holds only for decisions that carry no such option.

## Exercises

1. Compute $p^\ast$ for $C = 50$, $\eta = 0.5$ and $L = 400$ (answer: $50/(0.5\times 400) = 0.25$).
2. Keeping $C$ and $\eta$ symbolic, find the loss $L$ at which $p^\ast$ falls below a stated level $p\_{\mathrm{bar}}$, and say why the required loss grows without bound as $\eta \to 0$ (answer: $L > C/(\eta\mkern3mu p\_{\mathrm{bar}})$; a shield that protects almost nothing must be nearly free to be worth carrying at any low probability).
3. Construct a case in which waiting is rational and name the premise of section 3 that it overturns (answer: a reversible decision whose reversal cost is below $pL$; what falls is "the decision cannot be reversed").
4. State the invalidation condition of this chapter's thesis and the procedure once it is violated (answer: when the action is reversible, replace the fixed threshold by the comparison with the reversal cost and act as a fractional probe).

## Bridge

The rest of Part I takes the ideas in this chapter apart and puts them back together with names.
[What Judging Is, and What It Is Not](27-what-judging-is.md) gives the historical vocabulary and the
three reasons the difficulty is permanent rather than temporary. The decision threshold itself (*act when the chance exceeds the cost of acting divided by the loss of not acting, scaled by efficacy*) is formalized in
[Decision, Loss, Threshold](36-decision-loss-threshold.md), which separates the cost-loss ratio $C/(\eta L)$ from the decision-theoretic boundary $C\_{\mathrm{FP}}/(C\_{\mathrm{FP}}+L\_{\mathrm{FN}})$ of Pauker and Kassirer: two forms, two arguments, and mixing them is the arithmetic error that chapter warns about.

[The Average Is Not the Story](02-the-average-is-not-the-story.md) starts with the first of the three
difficulties (a single summary number standing in for a hundred different cases) and shows what an
average leaves out of a plan.
