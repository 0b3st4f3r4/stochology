## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Separate the quality of a decision before the outcome from the quality of the outcome afterward, using the fourfold competence–luck matrix.
2. Explain why a room full of winners can hold almost no skill, and how survivorship bias manufactures methods that were never there.
3. Apply the archer's distinction between aim (*télos*) and hit (*skopós*) to design accountability that rewards careful preparation instead of luck.

## 1. Two things that are not the same

A hospital chief of staff, or a municipal infrastructure board, meets on the Monday after a stormy weekend. Two district managers sit across the table. Manager A inspected the drainage pumps on Friday, verified the backup diesel stock and stationed a repair crew on standby. A localized lightning strike hit her substation anyway, and her district still lost three hours of power. Manager B skipped the Friday inspection, left the backup tank empty to trim quarterly operating expenses, and went home early. The storm track veered four miles north of his district, so his lights stayed on all weekend.

A decision is made beforehand (at time $t\_0$) with what is known then. The result arrives afterward (at time $t\_1 > t\_0$), in irreversible time, shaped by everything else in the **World**. When the board votes on promotions, the one thing a simple outcome spreadsheet in the **Archive** leaves out is what each manager actually prepared before the storm arrived.

Because the decision precedes the outcome, four combinations are possible in any uncertain world:

| Before-Outcome Decision Quality | Desirable Result Afterward | Adverse Result Afterward |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Competent / Well-Calibrated** | **Apt Competence:** Careful inquiry producing a good result through skill rather than an intervening stroke of luck. | **Noble Misfortune:** Prudent precaution tested by an unpreventable extreme shock. |
| **Reckless / Incompetent** | **Toxic Luck:** Wild gamble rewarded by random chance (the most corrupting case). | **Deserved Catastrophe:** Neglect followed by predictable collapse. |

Which cases look alike to an inattentive observer?

The two successes—apt competence and toxic luck—look identical on a short-term spreadsheet. Both hit the target, passed the audit, won the match. The two failures—noble misfortune and deserved catastrophe—both show red ink or dark streets.

Judging decisions by their later results has a name, **outcome bias** (Baron & Hershey 1988), and so does the philosophical puzzle underneath it, **moral luck** (Nagel 1979; Williams 1981). Untangling the two takes two distinct timestamps. At decision time $t\_0$ in the **Institution**, the outcome at $t\_1$ does not exist yet: the manager governs only the inquiry, the margin of doubt, and the backup tank. At audit time $t\_1$, the outcome sits in the **Archive**. Unless the reasoning and precautions were sealed before $t\_1$, hindsight collapses all four cells into two columns: success or failure. The distortion is systematic: on the very same day, it promotes a reckless gambler who got lucky while punishing a prudent steward hit by a freak bolt.

## 2. A room full of winners

No advanced mathematics is needed for this one.

A thousand people each flip a fair coin and are paid for heads. After one flip, about five hundred remain. They flip again; about two hundred and fifty remain. Continue for ten rounds, and only about one person in a thousand finishes with ten heads in a row. Bring that winner onto a stage and ask for the secret.

The room reacts one of two ways. Either the audience thinks there is a magic trick (a special flick of the wrist, a winning mindset, a proprietary algorithm), or it notices that a thousand coin-flippers is enough to produce one lucky streak no matter what anyone does.

Put simple counts on a promotion room to see why luck dominates whenever reckless players outnumber careful ones. Suppose $1{,}000$ new managers start out: $100$ of them ($10\unicode{x25}$) are genuinely skilled, while $900$ ($90\unicode{x25}$) are reckless gamblers. Now two kinds of year can happen:

- **A stormy filter:** Suppose $80\unicode{x25}$ of skilled managers survive ($80$ winners) and only $20\unicode{x25}$ of gamblers survive ($180$ winners). Look at the winners' stage: $80 + 180 = 260$ managers survived, yet $180 / 260 \approx 69\unicode{x25}$—more than two-thirds—are lucky gamblers, purely because gamblers outnumbered skilled hands nine to one at the start.
- **A calm year:** Suppose calm weather lets $90\unicode{x25}$ of *both* groups pass ($90$ skilled and $810$ gamblers, $900$ winners total). Now $810 / 900 = 90\unicode{x25}$ of the stage are gamblers, the exact same $10\unicode{x25}$ skill rate you started with. A quiet season leaves zero trace of skill.

Now write that exact counting rule in symbols. Write $\pi \in (0, 1)$ for the starting share of genuinely competent ("Adroit") agents, $s\_h \in (0, 1]$ for the survival rate of adroit agents, and $s\_l \in [0, 1]$ for the survival rate of reckless ("Inapt") gamblers. The chance that a winner on stage is genuinely skilled is:

$$\mathbb{P}(\text{Adroit}\mid\text{Success}) = \frac{\pi\mkern3mu s\_h}{\pi\mkern3mu s\_h + (1 - \pi)\mkern3mu s\_l} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}\frac{s\_l}{s\_h}}$$

Read that fraction aloud: put the skilled survivors upstairs ($\pi s\_h$), and divide by all survivors—skilled plus lucky gamblers ($\pi s\_h + (1 - \pi)s\_l$). Every promotion room sits between two limits:

1. **A quiet year ($s\_l = s\_h$):** When no storm arrives to test anyone, gamblers pass at the same rate as prudent stewards. The ratio $s\_l / s\_h = 1$ cancels out, leaving $\mathbb{P}(\text{Adroit}\mid\text{Success}) = \pi$—outcomes add **zero information** about skill.
2. **A test luck cannot pass ($s\_l \to 0^+$ with $s\_h > 0$):** As the chance of surviving by blind luck drops toward zero, the right-hand denominator shrinks to $1$ and $\mathbb{P}(\text{Adroit}\mid\text{Success}) \to 1$. Only when luck cannot pass the bar does winning signal real competence.

**Survivorship bias** enters institutional life right here ([Who Is Missing From the Table](06-who-is-missing-from-the-table.md)). Averaging only the active firms or surviving funds left standing while erasing collapsed peers makes reckless methods look brilliant on paper.

## 3. The shot, the hit, and apt agency

The vocabulary comes from archery, and its central distinction recurs across traditions ([the Prologue](00-preface.md)): classical Stoic sources separate the archer's continuous craft (*télos*) from the external mark exposed to crosswinds (*skopós*; developed canonically in [Chapter 27](27-what-judging-is.md) and [Chapter 29](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md)). Following Ernest Sosa's performance test (Sosa 2007; deepened in [What Knowledge Is](16-what-knowledge-is.md)), hitting a mark counts as **apt** only when accuracy flows *from* genuine skill—not in a Gettier-style wind-blown arrow case where a lucky gust pushes an off-target shot back onto the bullseye (Sosa 2007; cf. Gettier 1963).

Evaluating people strictly on hits destroys institutional accountability. Bengt Holmström's (1979) **Informativeness Principle** explains why: any scorecard that throws away information about external winds forces pure noise onto the judgment of the operator. Whenever executives at the top set thin budgets or fragile staffing and frontline crews absorb the storms at the bottom, an outcome-only scoreboard dumps structural blame onto frontline operators. Someone who spots unreadable crosswinds and lowers the bow has acted wisely. A bonus system that rewards only hits trains that wisdom right out of the building.

## 4. What this does to schools, teams, and institutions

An institution that measures only outcomes teaches the following, without anyone intending it:

That luck is skill. That someone who took a reckless gamble and got away with it deserves promotion. That someone who kept a careful **Vital Margin** and got hit by an unpreventable extreme shock deserves punishment. Remove accountability from an institution while keeping everything else intact, and the slide accelerates: high-powered chasing of short-term numbers and dismissal of frontline warnings drain common reserves until the system hits ruin.

A similar distortion appears even without reckless gambling, simply through imperfect scoring. Take an imperfect yes-or-no screening tool—one that makes both false alarms and missed detections—applied to groups with different baseline rates, the case Alexandra Chouldechova (2017) studied for risk scores. If the score demands equal accuracy among flagged individuals across groups with different underlying rates, it becomes mathematically impossible to equalize false-alarm rates and missed-case rates at the same time—so retrospective scorecards manufacture unequal blame out of unequal baselines.

The remedy is not to ignore outcomes, but to bind an institutional promise *before* the outcome arrives (Arendt 1958): write down at $t\_0$ what was known, which risks were flagged, and which reserves were held. For consequential choices affecting third parties—without burying routine work in defensive paperwork—sealing that pre-outcome log in a tamper-evident two-clock record ($t\_1 > t\_0$) lets auditors tell apt skill from lucky hits, and noble misfortune from neglect. Still, sealing promises at $t\_0$ only stops *backward* rewriting; it leaves blind spots open to outside witnesses and must allow attested *forward* reopening—verified independently and logged as a new entry rather than an erasure—whenever real conditions shatter the original map (Canguilhem 1943/1966).

### How much of the room was lucky

Look again at our room of promoted managers and ask what share is truly competent. In Section 2, observers never directly see the base rate $\pi$, the skilled survival rate $s\_h$, or the gambler survival rate $s\_l$—they only see the total winners left in the room. Remember what outcome selection actually does: it multiplies the starting *odds* of skill $\pi/(1 - \pi)$ by the survival ratio $s\_h/s\_l$, rather than lifting competence directly. When genuine skill is rare at the start and calm years let luck pass almost as easily as care ($s\_l$ close to $s\_h$), the winner's circle remains overwhelmingly lucky.

### When judging by outcome is nearly correct

Judging strictly by outcomes works safely only when the process is nearly deterministic on *both* sides: reckless shortcuts almost never succeed ($s\_l \to 0^+$) *and* careful work almost never loses to freak crosswinds ($s\_h \to 1^-$), with no misleading luck, proxy gaming, or shifting of unpaid harm onto outsiders. Even when reckless luck never wins ($s\_l \to 0^+$), if careful crews still lose to rare storms ($s\_h < 1$), outcome-only grading still punishes *Noble Misfortune*. Outside that narrow limit, apply the pre-outcome seals, outside witness checks, and attested forward reopening of Section 4.
