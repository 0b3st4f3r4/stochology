## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Separate the quality of an ex-ante decision from the quality of its ex-post outcome, using the fourfold competence–luck matrix.
2. Explain why a room full of winners can hold almost no skill, and how survivorship bias manufactures methods that were never there.
3. Apply the archer's distinction between aim (*télos*) and hit (*skopós*), and use it to design institutional governance that rewards apt agency instead of luck.

## 1. Two things that are not the same

An hospital chief of staff, or a municipal infrastructure board, meets on the Monday after a stormy weekend. Two district managers sit across the table. Manager A inspected the drainage pumps on Friday, verified the backup diesel stock and stationed a repair crew on standby. A localized lightning strike hit her substation anyway, and her district still lost three hours of power. Manager B skipped the Friday inspection, left the backup tank empty to trim quarterly operating expenses, and went home early. The storm track veered four miles north of his district, so his lights stayed on all weekend.

A decision is made at one point in time ($t\_0$) with what is known then. An outcome arrives afterwards ($t\_1 > t\_0$), in irreversible time, with the cooperation of everything else in the **World**. The moment the board votes on promotions, the one thing a simple outcome spreadsheet in the **Archive** cannot show is the **counterfactual state of the agent's ex-ante preparation**.

Because the decision precedes the outcome, four combinations are possible in any uncertain world:

| Ex-Ante Decision Quality | Ex-Post Desirable Outcome | Ex-Post Adverse Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Competent / Well-Calibrated** | **Apt Competence:** Careful inquiry rewarded by favorable conditions. | **Noble Misfortune:** Prudent precaution tested by an unpreventable extreme shock. |
| **Reckless / Incompetent** | **Toxic Luck:** Wild gamble rewarded by random chance (the most corrupting case). | **Deserved Catastrophe:** Neglect followed by predictable collapse. |

Which cases look alike to an inattentive observer?

The two successes, apt competence and toxic luck, look identical on a short-term spreadsheet. Both hit the revenue target, passed the audit, won the match. The two failures, noble misfortune and deserved catastrophe, both show red ink or broken pipes.

Judging decisions by their ex-post outcomes has a name, **outcome bias** (Baron & Hershey 1988), and so does the philosophical problem underneath it, **moral luck** (Nagel 1979; Williams 1981). Untangling the two takes two distinct timestamps. At $t\_0$ in the **Institution** the realization of the **World** at $t\_1$ does not exist yet, and the
decision-maker governs only the inquiry, the epistemic margin and the reserve buffer. At audit time
$t\_1$ the outcome sits in the **Archive**, and unless the premises were locked before it arrived,
hindsight collapses the four cells into two columns: the result, and nothing else. The distortion is
neither small nor random. It moves an incompetent gamble upward across a passing bar when the gamble
wins, and moves a careful steward downward across the same bar when an exogenous shock arrives, in
the same audit, on the same day. Averaging the three safeguards of the triad and reading the average
against a threshold makes the error worse, because a single ruinous axis still leaves the average near
its other two terms: at the boundary, the average pronounces ruinous states safe and the minimum does
not. An **Institution** that cannot tell competence from fortune promotes reckless gamblers and
punishes prudent stewards, and it does so systematically.

## 2. A room full of winners

No advanced mathematics is needed for this one.

A thousand people each flip a fair coin and are paid for heads. After one flip, about five hundred
remain. They flip again; about two hundred and fifty remain. Continue for ten rounds, and the expected
number of unbroken winners is a thousand multiplied by one half raised to the tenth power — under one
person left with ten heads in a row. Bring that person to a stage and ask for the secret.

The room will react one of two ways. Either the audience believes there is a secret (a special flick of
the wrist, a positive mindset, a proprietary algorithm), or it notices that a thousand people is enough to
produce such a person no matter what anyone does. The second reaction is correct and remarkably hard to
hold on to. Write down the posterior that a promoted survivor is genuinely adroit rather than merely
lucky:

$$\mathbb{P}(\text{Adroit}\mid\text{Success}) = \frac{\pi\mkern3mus\_h}{\pi\mkern3mus\_h + (1 - \pi)\mkern3mus\_l} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}\frac{s\_l}{s\_h}}$$

where $\pi \in (0, 1)$ is the base rate of genuine competence in the population, $s\_h = \mathbb{P}(\text{Success}\mid\text{Adroit})$ is the survival rate of competent agents, and $s\_l = \mathbb{P}(\text{Success}\mid\text{Inapt})$ is the survival rate of reckless gamblers. Every promotion room sits between two limits:

1. **A quiet year ($s\_l \to s\_h$).** No tail shock arrives, so reckless gamblers survive at the same rate as prudent stewards ($s\_l = s\_h$). The likelihood ratio $s\_l / s\_h = 1$ cancels, and $\mathbb{P}(\text{Adroit}\mid\text{Success}) = \pi$: the outcome carries **zero information** about skill, and the stage fills with the base-rate share of the competent and nothing else. Selection by outcome has raised nobody's competence; it has merely removed everyone who failed.
2. **A test luck cannot pass ($s\_l \to 0^+$ with $s\_h > 0$).** As the chance of succeeding by pure luck vanishes ($s\_l \to 0^+$), the ratio $(1 - \pi)s\_l / (\pi s\_h) \to 0$ and $\mathbb{P}(\text{Adroit}\mid\text{Success}) \to 1$. The survivor is competent almost surely, and the outcome carries nearly all the evidence it is capable of carrying.

**Survivorship bias** enters institutional life here (Wald 1943). Take a randomized evaluation
of a training programme: the average gain across all participants is one number, and the average taken
only over those who ended up earning a positive wage is a different and systematically flattering
number, because the participants whose earnings stayed at zero are precisely the ones whose absence
raises the average of the rest. Conditioning on the survivors does not trim a noisy tail; it deletes
the arm of the comparison that carries the finding. Look only at the winners on stage and the
nine hundred and ninety-nine who used the same method and failed are invisible.

## 3. The shot, the hit, and apt agency

The vocabulary comes from archery, and it is old. The Stoics contrasted the internal duty of the archer (**τέλος, *télos***), everything within the agent's control, such as testing the bowstring, measuring the target and accounting for the crosswind (Cicero 45 BCE), with the external strike (**σκοπός, *skopós***), which stays subject to crosswinds after the arrow has left the bow.

Ernest Sosa formalized the distinction for modern epistemology as the **AAA criteria** of performance (Sosa 2007):

1. **Accurate:** did the arrow hit the target?
2. **Adroit:** was the shot executed with skill, care and calibration?
3. **Apt:** was the shot accurate *because* it was adroit, and not an errant gust that blew a badly aimed arrow into the bullseye?

In an environment that is **reactive** and dynamic, evaluating only accuracy destroys the **Institutional
Governance** axis ($S\_{\mathrm{gov}}$) of the **Nuclear Triad**. The failure is worth stating precisely,
because it is a misattribution and not a mere blind spot. In a hierarchy, the decision that sizes the
exposure is taken high up, in the mandate, the budget and the staffing model, while the operational
noise visible in the record is generated at the bottom. A scoreboard that reads the record attributes the
variance it can see to the people it can see, which is the operator rather than the executive
(Holmström 1979). The blame inversion is systematic, not occasional. An agent who knows the wind is
unreadable and lowers the bow has exercised adroit competence. A scoreboard that rewards only hits
trains that prudence out of the institution.

## 4. What this does to schools, teams, and institutions

An institution that measures only outcomes teaches the following, without anyone intending it:

That luck is skill. That a person who took an irresponsible risk and got away with it should be promoted. That a person who maintained a prudent **Vital Margin** ($S\_{\mathrm{vital}}$) and then suffered an unpreventable heavy-tailed shock should be penalized. Remove institutional governance from an otherwise intact arrangement and the mechanism runs to its conclusion: high-powered proxy chasing and the discounting of frontline testimony consume the common reserve until the run ends at the barrier, and it ends earlier than it would have with no governance failure at all. The same mechanism works through measurement. Take an imperfect binary screening rule deployed across groups
with unequal structural base rates, the case Chouldechova (2017) analyzes for recidivism instruments. A
threshold held to predictive parity (equal positive predictive value across groups) cannot simultaneously equalize both false-positive and false-negative rates, and retrospective tallies
fabricate differential blame out of structural exposure.

The remedy is not to ignore outcomes. It is to record the decision premises *before* the outcome is
revealed (Arendt 1958): what was known, what risks identified, what buffers reserved, what expected.
Seal the ex-ante decision state at $t\_0$ in an append-only, tamper-evident record, a bitemporal
commitment that can be opened at $t\_1 > t\_0$ and checked against what was actually written, and the
institution can audit whether the result was an apt hit, an adroit miss, or a toxic piece of luck. The
seal is not a formality. Without it there is nothing to compare, because the ex-ante record is
rewritten by whoever writes the report after the fact.

### How much of the room was lucky

Take a room of promoted managers and ask what share of them is competent. The posterior of section 2
answers with three quantities, and only one of them is visible from the room. Nobody reports the base
rate $\pi$. The survival rates $s\_h$ and $s\_l$ are only ever observed pooled, as the promotion
rate of the room. Selection by outcome raises the competent share above $\pi$ exactly by the likelihood
ratio $s\_h/s\_l$ in favour of competence, and how far it raises it depends on the base rate it is
applied to: the same selection regime lifts a high base rate substantially and a low one hardly at all.
That is why a profession with a small pool of genuine skill cannot be rescued by better selection. The
posterior depends on a base rate the room never reports. And the four cells of section 1 name what a
promotion decision cannot separate from the outside: apt and lucky, apt and unlucky, inept and lucky,
inept and unlucky. Outcome-based dismissal punishes the second cell, the one that did everything right.

### When judging by outcome is nearly correct

The thesis stops holding where the process is nearly deterministic, that is when the probability of
success given luck tends to zero. Then the survivor is almost surely competent and the outcome carries
nearly all the evidence. For the general case: record before judging, seal the ex-ante state, compare
the sealed state with the realised outcome. Without a sealed record the ex-ante is rewritten by whoever
writes the report, and there is nothing left to compare.

## Exercises

1. Using the posterior of section 2 with survival rates $s\_h = 0.9$ and $s\_l = 0.5$, compute the competent share among survivors for base rates $\pi = 0.10$ and $\pi = 0.20$, and say what the comparison proves about selection by outcome (answer: the posterior rises in both cases but stays below one half; selection cannot manufacture competence out of a low base rate).
2. Name the four cells that section 1 separates, and say which one dismissal by outcome punishes (answer: apt and lucky, apt and unlucky, inept and lucky, inept and unlucky; the punished cell is apt and unlucky).
3. Construct the case in which judging by outcome is almost optimal, and name the premise of section 1 that it overturns (answer: a nearly deterministic process, where success given luck tends to zero; what falls is "the outcome never informs").
4. Describe the minimal record that makes ex-post judgment auditable, and why the thesis does not apply without it (answer: a sealed ex-ante state plus a hash chain; without it the ex-ante is rewritten and there is nothing to compare).
