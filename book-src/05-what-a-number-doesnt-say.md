## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Tell an instrument's resolution (precision) apart from its calibration against reality (accuracy).
2. Compute why a diagnostic test with 95% sensitivity produces over 80% false alarms in low-prevalence regimes (base rate neglect).
3. Identify the dimensions a reported metric must carry with it: evidential weight, sample wobble, operational alert fatigue.

## 1. Precise and correct

A bathroom scale shows 63.42 kilograms. It is precise to the hundredth of a kilogram. It was also never calibrated, so it is wrong by two kilograms every single time.

The second fact is the one that matters. The first is the one that gets read. Decimals look like
knowledge and are only resolution: the scale tells 63.41 from 63.42, which says nothing about your mass.

**Precision** and **accuracy** are different things. Precision is how finely the instrument distinguishes in the **Archive**. Accuracy is whether the answer lands close to the truth in the **World**. A measurement can be precise and wrong, accurate and vague, or — when things go well — both.

Bare summary numbers in institutional life routinely hide two further things: distribution skewness
and retrospective revision. A mean reported to two decimal places can sit above a median that describes
the typical case, because a long quiet stretch pulls the average down and a few heavy periods pull it
up. Given only the mean, a reader cannot tell which of the two he is looking at. And many numbers that
steer policy are provisional the moment they are used. First releases of aggregate statistics get
revised after the decision they informed has been taken, sometimes reversing the sign of the change
that justified it, and the revision is rarely printed next to the figure that was quoted in the
meeting. Reports confuse precision with accuracy all the time, because decimals persuade even when they
come out of uncalibrated or provisional guessing.

## 2. Percentages of what: Natural frequencies and alert fatigue

A night-shift resident watches a central telemetry console. Over twelve hours the sepsis monitor
chimes a dozen times, and nearly every one of those patients is stable — the alert triggered by a
transient heart-rate spike after routine movement. By the third week her hand silences the alarm before
her eyes reach the banner. The stream taught her that most sirens are false.

The arithmetic behind that ward surprises almost everyone the first time they meet it.

A medical diagnostic test is remarkably good. It catches 95 out of every 100 people who have a condition (a sensitivity or true-positive rate of $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.95$). It also raises a false alarm for 5 out of every 100 people who are completely healthy (a false-positive rate of $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.05$). In a general population where 1 person in 100 has the condition (a base rate or prevalence of $\pi = 0.01$), your test comes back positive.

What is the chance you actually have the condition?

Most people guess something near 95%. The answer is about 16.1%.

Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage showed in 1995 (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995) that the clearest way to see why is to translate conditional percentages into a **natural frequency tree** across ten thousand people:

1. **Prevalence:** Out of $10{,}000$ people, exactly $100$ have the condition, and $9{,}900$ are healthy.
2. **True Positives:** Among the $100$ who are sick, the test catches $95\unicode{x25}$: $100 \times 0.95 = 95$ people.
3. **False Positives:** Among the $9{,}900$ who are healthy, the test sounds a false alarm on $5\unicode{x25}$: $9{,}900 \times 0.05 = 495$ people.
4. **Total Positives:** The test fires for $95 + 495 = 590$ people in total.

Now take the perspective of someone holding a positive test slip. Out of the $590$ people who tested positive, how many are actually sick? Bayes' rule, written in canonical form:

$$\mathrm{PPV}(\pi, \mathrm{TPR}, \mathrm{FPR}) = \frac{\pi\mkern3mu\mathrm{TPR}}{\pi\mkern3mu\mathrm{TPR} + (1 - \pi)\mkern3mu\mathrm{FPR}} = \frac{95}{95 + 495} = \frac{95}{590} \approx 0.161 \implies 16.1\unicode{x25}$$

Of $590$ positive alerts, $495$ are false alarms, a false discovery rate of $\mathrm{FDR} = 1 - \mathrm{PPV} = 83.9\unicode{x25}$. Two features of the fraction explain the ward, and both are structural rather than accidental. The numerator carries the base rate as a factor, so the fraction falls with prevalence. The denominator carries the false-alarm term multiplied by the healthy majority, so the fraction is governed by the size of the population that does *not* have the condition. Move either quantity and the positive predictive value moves with it. Doubling the false-alarm rate at fixed prevalence doubles the false-positive count while the true positives stay where they were; doubling prevalence doubles the true positives while leaving the healthy majority nearly unchanged. The denominator governs the room, and two limits show it:

- **A rare disease ($\pi \to 0^+$ at fixed $\mathrm{FPR} > 0$).** As prevalence $\pi \to 0^+$, $\mathrm{PPV} \sim \pi(\mathrm{TPR}/\mathrm{FPR}) \to 0$ no matter how close sensitivity $\mathrm{TPR}$ is to $1.0$. Even a test with perfect sensitivity finds that almost every one of its positives is a false alarm, because the healthy majority entering the denominator swamps the handful of true cases in the numerator.
- **A nearly silent alarm ($\mathrm{FPR} \to 0^+$ with $\pi\mkern3mu\mathrm{TPR} > 0$).** Only by driving the false-alarm rate $\mathrm{FPR} \to 0^+$ does $\mathrm{PPV} \to 1$. In a low-prevalence regime, specificity is the parameter that decides, and improving sensitivity buys almost nothing.

Institutional catastrophe begins here. In a landmark 2021 study of the widely deployed Epic Sepsis Model across hospital electronic health records, Wong et al. (2021) report that at the deployed threshold the alert carried a positive predictive value of $12\unicode{x25}$. The complementary false-alarm share, $1 - \mathrm{PPV} = 0.88$, is arithmetic on that reported value and not a separate figure the study states. Human conscious behavioral throughput is biologically bounded near $10\text{ bits/s}$ (Zheng & Meister 2025), so flooding nurses and physicians with uncalibrated alarms creates severe **alert fatigue**: when a genuinely septic patient deteriorates, the warning is dismissed as just another spurious ping.

## 3. A number with no room

The last thing a bare number hides is how much it would have moved, and how much evidence stands behind it.

A school reports that the average test score rose from 68 to 71. Is that an improvement? It depends on how many independent students were involved and how much scores normally fluctuate. With five effective students, a three-point move is what happens when two students had a good breakfast. With five thousand effective students, it represents a substantial educational shift.

John Maynard Keynes, in *A Treatise on Probability* (1921), distinguished the balance of probability $p$ from the **weight of evidence** $V$ (Keynes 1921). Two arguments can give the same probability and differ in how much evidence stands behind it; the argument built on more independent evidence is the one whose probability would move less if a further observation arrived. The weight is a harmonic ratio,

$$V(n\_{\mathrm{eff}}) = \frac{n\_{\mathrm{eff}}}{n\_{\mathrm{eff}} + n\_0}, \qquad n\_0 := n\_{\mathrm{req}}(\alpha, \beta, p^\star) = \left(\frac{z\_{1-\alpha} + z\_{1-\beta}}{\varepsilon\_{\mathrm{rel}}}\right)^2 \frac{1 - p^\star}{p^\star},$$

against a reference scale $n\_0$ fixed in advance by the decision's error tolerances ($\alpha + \beta < 1$), relative resolution $\varepsilon\_{\mathrm{rel}} > 0$, and cost–loss threshold $p^\star = C/(\eta L) \in (0, 1)$, where $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective independent sample size after accounting for clustering — the design effect of the survey literature, which converts a table of rows into the number of independent observations it actually carries. Two limits of $V$ are worth checking:

- **A small sample ($n\_{\mathrm{eff}} \ll n\_0$).** The weight is small: a table whose effective sample is a fraction of the reference scale cannot carry a strong claim, and $V \to 0$ as $n\_{\mathrm{eff}} \to 0$. At the reference scale itself, $V = 1/2$, half the weight a claim of the same probability would carry on evidence of unlimited independence.
- **A sample past the reference scale ($n\_{\mathrm{eff}} \gg n\_0$).** The weight approaches one from below and never arrives: multiplying the effective sample by ten moves $V$ from $1/2$ to $10/11$, a gain of well under half a unit of weight for an order of magnitude of data. The curve is concave by construction, and the concavity is Keynes's point: evidence saturates, and a table that doubles its rows through the same biased door barely moves.

A number that arrives without its evidential weight $V$ and uncertainty interval is a claim at infinite confidence, eroding the **Epistemic Margin** ($S\_{\mathrm{epi}}$) of the **Nuclear Triad**.

## 4. What to ask

Three questions catch most of this distortion across the **World**, **Archive**, and **Institution**, and they cost nothing:

1. *Precise or correct?* Where did this number come from, when was the instrument last calibrated against an independent ground truth, and is it subject to revision after first release?
2. *Out of what?* What is the base rate $\pi$ and the false-positive rate $\mathrm{FPR}$ in the denominator, and which way does the conditional probability point?
3. *How much would it move?* What is the effective sample size $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and its evidential weight $V(n\_{\mathrm{eff}})$, and could sample wobble cross the decision threshold? The question is comparative and has a clean answer. When the distance between the sample mean and the action threshold is small relative to the standard deviation of the sample mean, wobble can flip the decision and the margin has to be measured. When that distance dwarfs the entire support of the observed distribution, wobble cannot flip anything, and the bare point estimate is already sufficient.

### The denominator nobody quotes

A filter that catches $95$ in $100$ events can still be wrong eight times in ten. The operator who
learns to walk past the screen is not negligent: nobody asked the number for its denominator.

Ask the denominator first, the resolution second, and what would overturn the conclusion third. The
three questions are not rhetorical. Each has an answer that can be demanded of the person who produced
the figure, and a report that supplies all three is auditable in a way a two-decimal percentage is
not. A test is severe when it had a real chance of failing had the claim been false (Mayo 2018). A
screen whose false alarms swamp its hits was never asked that question: it was built to fire, and
firing is what it does.

### When the margin stops being worth its price

The thesis fails when the cost of measuring the margin exceeds the value the margin would change, and
the boundary is economic rather than epistemic. Write $m$ for the cost of measuring the gap, $p$ for
the probability of the event and $\Delta L$ for how much the gap would change the loss: the conclusion
survives while $m \le p\mkern3mu\Delta L$. Where it does not, decide at the operating point and record
the unmeasured margin as a declared debt. Do not buy precision that cannot pay for itself.
