## 1. The survey that only asks those present

A principal stands at the bus loop at 7:45 on a Tuesday, handing out a one-page questionnaire on whether
students feel safe commuting after dark. First-period homeroom collects the forms. Almost every
student present checks the box marked "Safe," and by noon the district office publishes a reassuring
safety score.

Who is missing from that spreadsheet? The students who never stepped off the morning bus. Some are ill,
some work an overnight shift to pay household rent, some were kept home because the walk to the bus
stop after dusk felt unsafe, and some have already dropped out. The students who did not cross the
schoolhouse threshold on Tuesday are a different sample of how the commute feels than the students at
their desks.

When the school board votes to cut evening crossing-guard patrols, decision-makers are missing the **unrecorded denominator**: how many students walked the dark route in the **World** yet were filtered out before reaching the **Archive**. An honest register separates the observed count from the missing count so unrecorded need is bounded across its full range rather than treated as zero. Hiding the intake door behind a big spreadsheet lets the **Institution** mistake its own filter for a fact about the neighborhood, eroding its **epistemic margin** and inviting **Collapse Proposition 1 (Blind Undersizing)**.

Collecting data is not the mistake. The habit to build before trusting any table is asking: *who would not be in this table, and why?*

## 2. Recorded is not the same as happened: Wald's missing armor

Every institution sees only what walks through its door. A hospital counts patients who reach triage; it misses those who stayed home sick, died before the ambulance arrived, or lacked a billing code. Absent from the list is not absent from the world. Roy Bhaskar called confusing the two the *epistemic fallacy*—treating a claim about what we happen to observe as a claim about all that exists, even though real absences shape what happens (Bhaskar 1975; Bhaskar 1993). Language reinforces that filter: Reinhart Koselleck (2006) showed that administrative categories often define the included group positively while marking outsiders only by negation—leaving the excluded hardest to see and defend in an official table.

During World War II, statistician Abraham Wald at the Statistical Research Group worked on estimating aircraft vulnerability solely from damage observed on returning bombers (Wald 1943). Because aircraft downed by fatal hits never enter the hangar ledger—if returning planes cluster bullet holes on wings and fuselage while showing clean engines, that pattern can reflect lethal engine hits rather than immunity—Wald proved that survivor counts cannot identify zone lethality without independent hit-dispersion assumptions or test shots.

To see the survivorship filter with an idealized numerical sketch, suppose out of every $10$ bombers hit in the wing ($A$), $9$ limp home and $1$ goes down—so each surviving wing-hit stands for $1/9$ of a missing plane. Meanwhile, suppose out of every $10$ bombers hit in the engine ($B$), only $2$ limp home and $8$ go down—so each surviving engine-hit stands for $8/2 = 4$ missing planes. Armor belongs where hits are lethal rather than where survivor counts pile up.

An idealized two-wave algebraic sketch shows how missing counts invert when hit-dispersion mixes differ across combat waves, illustrating the survivorship logic behind Wald (1943). Suppose each aircraft takes at most one hit in two disjoint zones $z \in \lbrace{}A, B\rbrace$ with constant unknown kill probabilities $q\_z \in (0, 1)$ across waves $1$ and $2$, so in expectation each surviving hit stands for $\theta\_z = q\_z / (1 - q\_z) > 0$ missing aircraft. Writing $\bar{S}\_{1,A}, \bar{S}\_{1,B}, \bar{S}\_{2,A}, \bar{S}\_{2,B}$ for expected returning survivors hit in each zone (approximated by observed counts $S\_{j,z}$ in large samples) and $M\_1, M\_2$ for expected missing planes:

$$\bar{S}\_{1,A}\mkern3mu\theta\_A + \bar{S}\_{1,B}\mkern3mu\theta\_B = M\_1, \qquad \bar{S}\_{2,A}\mkern3mu\theta\_A + \bar{S}\_{2,B}\mkern3mu\theta\_B = M\_2$$

Read those two equations aloud: **expected returning survivors in zone $A$ times zone $A$'s missing-per-survivor ratio, plus expected returning survivors in zone $B$ times zone $B$'s ratio, equals the expected missing planes in each wave.** As long as the two waves had different hit mixes ($\bar{S}\_{1,A} \bar{S}\_{2,B} - \bar{S}\_{2,A} \bar{S}\_{1,B} \ne 0$) and yield positive ratios $\theta\_A, \theta\_B > 0$, high-school elimination solves directly for both $\theta\_A$ and $\theta\_B$ (noting that plugging noisy finite-sample counts $S\_{j,z}$ straight into the matrix can push raw estimates $\hat{\theta}\_z$ negative unless constrained to physical bounds). That puts armor where lethality ($q\_B > q\_A$), not visible hangar clutter, is highest.

## 3. More rows do not fix a filter: The Big Data Paradox

This is the part that surprises planners. Surely if you gather enough data, errors wash out?

No—and plain everyday reasoning shows why. Two distinct problems shrink how much information a spreadsheet really holds before you even run a formula:

1. **Clustered rows (repeated copies of the same group):** Suppose you want to estimate average height across a city of $10{,}000$ people, and you measure $100$ people by picking $10$ extended families of $10$ relatives each. Your table lists $100$ rows, yet relatives share genes and meals—so each extra cousin adds far less fresh news than an unrelated stranger. Leslie Kish (1965) named this variance inflation the **design effect** ($\mathrm{DEFF} \ge 1$, capturing clustering here while unequal survey weights combine additively in [Chapter 34](34-sample-population-archive.md)), and Brent Moulton (1986) showed how clustering across classrooms, wards, or neighborhoods shrinks the **variance-effective** headcount governing random standard-error bars down to $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}} = n / \mathrm{DEFF}$.
2. **Skewed intake doors (selection bias):** Now suppose your recording door lets in mostly one side—like an app survey answered by confident users, where getting recorded ($R$) correlates with the outcome ($Y$) with population correlation $\rho\_{R,Y} \in [-1, 1]$. Xiao-Li Meng (2018) proved the **Big Data Paradox**: whenever a biased door captures a **fixed fraction** $f = n/N \in (0, 1)$ as archive and population grow together (or holds a fixed intake bias $\bar{Y}\_n - \bar{Y}\_N \ne 0$), piling up more biased rows does *not* wash out systematic bias—it just shrinks random error bars around the wrong answer.

Because $\rho\_{R,Y}$ depends on the unseen population outside the table (and becomes $0/0$ undefined at a full census $f = 1$), analysts evaluate effective information across a declared domain bound $\bar{\rho} \in [0, 1]$ ($\bar{\rho} \ge |\rho\_{R,Y}|$). Both penalties then combine in the **MSE-equivalent** effective sample size law derived in [Sample, Population, Archive](34-sample-population-archive.md) against an infinite-population unbiased random draw:

$$\frac{1}{n\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bar{\rho})} = \frac{1}{n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}} + \left(\frac{1 - f}{f}\right)\bar{\rho}^2 = \frac{\mathrm{DEFF}}{n} + \left(\frac{1 - f}{f}\right)\bar{\rho}^2, \qquad f = \frac{n}{N} \in (0, 1], \quad \bar{\rho} \in [0, 1]$$

Read that sum aloud: **one over the MSE-equivalent effective sample $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bar{\rho})$ equals the clustering variance penalty ($\mathrm{DEFF}/n$) plus the worst-case selection-bias penalty ($\frac{1-f}{f}\bar{\rho}^2$).** Remember that these two terms play different roles: $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}} = n / \mathrm{DEFF}$ sets random standard-error widths, whereas selection bias shifts the estimate itself by a systematic location band ($\pm\Delta\_{\mathrm{sel}}(\bar{\rho})$ in [Chapter 45](45-world-to-archive-big-data-paradox.md)). When there is no clustering ($\mathrm{DEFF} = 1$) and $f \in (0, 1)$, $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bar{\rho}) = n / \bigl(1 + (N - n)\bar{\rho}^2\bigr)$. Check a hand-sized surprise: in a city of $N = 1{,}000{,}000$ people, a biased survey of $n = 100{,}000$ rows ($f = 0.1$) with even a tiny declared selection bound $\bar{\rho} = 0.05$ carries a worst-case MSE-equivalent sample of only $100{,}000 / (1 + 900{,}000 \times 0.0025) \approx 44$ unbiased random draws from a large population—while its naive error bars shrink as if $100{,}000$ independent voices spoke.

Notice two clean extremes:

- **Unbiased door and unclustered draws ($\bar{\rho} = 0$, $\mathrm{DEFF} = 1$):** Both penalties vanish, so $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}(0) = n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}} = n$ and standard confidence intervals work as advertised.
- **Complete census within a frame ($f = n/N = 1$):** Sampling every member inside the defined roster drives $(1 - f)/f$ to zero, reducing $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bar{\rho})$ smoothly to the variance-effective count $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}$ (avoiding the undefined empirical correlation $\rho\_{R,Y} = 0/0$ at $f = 1$)—though groups left off the roster altogether still stay hidden.

Unlike clustering, $\bar{\rho}$ bounds the unseen population outside the table, so it must be set by domain judgment rather than read off the spreadsheet itself ([Sample, Population, Archive](34-sample-population-archive.md); [Inference and Evidence](35-inference-and-evidence.md)). Decades before big data logs, Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis (1946) demonstrated this on the ground in India using interpenetrating sub-samples (*IPNS*) to expose field-enumerator drift and non-sampling bias that raw headcounts hide.

## 4. Three ways to be empty

An empty or zero-looking cell in a ledger does not mean one thing. Separating a real recorded zero from blank fields yields **four distinct intake states** (formalized in [Measuring and Erring](33-measuring-and-erring.md))—a **Measured Zero** alongside **three ways a cell can lack a measurement**:

1. **Measured Zero (Observed as Absent):** Someone actually checked and recorded zero. A patient examined and found fever-free is genuine negative evidence, never an empty form.
2. **Unmeasured (Applicable Missingness, *A-mark*):** The event happened and the column applies, yet the clipboard missed it. When missingness depends only on observed traits, statistical reweighting helps; when it depends on the hidden outcome itself—like sick patients staying home—filling in blanks just copies survivor illusions, so only wide honest bounds hold (Rubin 1976).
3. **Schema-Inapplicable (*I-mark*):** A column exists on the form yet cannot apply to that row (like residential flood claims on a city park), marking structural inapplicability rather than zero or missing harm.
4. **Unrepresentable (*Not askable*, Left Outside the Form):** The reality had no column, dropdown, or category at all—invisible before a single row was written.

Database designer E. F. Codd (1979, 1986) fought to keep applicable unmeasured nulls (*A-marks*), inapplicable fields (*I-marks*), and measured zeros strictly separate (formalized in [Measuring and Erring](33-measuring-and-erring.md)). Yet even explicit null tags cannot record the fourth state, where a group lacks any box on the form—converting any of those three silences into zero manufactures false reassurance.

To estimate how large an unseen eligible population is when distinct registries overlap only partly, ecologists and statisticians compare individuals spotted on one list against those spotted on multiple lists. If two independent surveys catch very few people in common, a large uncounted crowd likely sits outside both doors—a lower bound under symmetric list detection heterogeneity (Model $M\_h$; Chao 1987, 1989; Chao & Colwell 2017; formalized alongside asymmetric or dependent lists in [Sample, Population, Archive](34-sample-population-archive.md)).

## 5. The mediation circuit and its guard sites

Numbers do not sit in a vacuum. They travel around a closed **three-arena loop** linking the **World** (where events happen), the **Archive** (where events are written down), and the **Institution** (where people make decisions and act):

- **Three forward steps carry information into action:** observing the **World** into the **Archive** (catching selection bias $\rho\_{R,Y}$ and uncounted gaps); turning **Archive** figures into rules inside the **Institution**; and acting from the **Institution** back onto the **World** (watching how published rules change behavior).
- **Three reverse checks protect against blind spots:** keeping the **Institution**'s framing and the **Archive**'s categories open to attested forward revision without altering past entries; testing **Archive** claims against real shocks in the **World**; and checking whether **World** survival time outlasts **Institution** delays.
- **One loop-wide closure locks accountability (*télos* and *skopós*):** sealing commitments and thresholds before outcomes land, judging craft separately from outcome luck, protecting frontline warnings without status discounting, and answering for displaced harm dumped onto outside communities and habitats—what the rest of this book calls **external reception perimeters** (shifting ruin onto others is barred in [the Prologue](00-preface.md)).

[Chapter 29](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md) (*Aim and the Three Planes*) and [Chapter 41](41-the-triad-and-the-minimum.md) (*The Triad and the Minimum*) map the complete seven-guard-site architecture along this loop. Because running every cross-list audit during a live emergency would freeze responders, multi-list reconciliation runs outside emergency shifts and feeds corroborated warnings cleanly to teams on duty ([Decision, Loss, Threshold](36-decision-loss-threshold.md)).

### Effective sample, not sample

Rows are not information. A clustered or skewed spreadsheet carries only its effective sample $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bar{\rho})$ (or $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}$ for random sampling variance). Keeping append-only logs that separate when an event happened from when it was reported preserves honest history without erasing earlier frames. Following Kish (1965), report effective sample size beside raw headcounts, state clearly who the frame excluded, declare framing revision rules up front, and never let missing fields collapse into false zeros.

### Public mirrors versus primary custody

Confidential medical or income files cannot be published raw, requiring a **two-layer archival rule** ([From World to Archive](45-world-to-archive-big-data-paradox.md); [Chapter 74](74-layer-critical-archival-transitional-justice-order.md)). Privacy-preserving public copies help protect people's identities, yet adding privacy noise blurs selection correlation ($\rho\_{R,Y}$), distorts extreme shocks, and hides rare overlaps needed for multi-list audits. Public copies must therefore stay strictly supplementary—never replacing immutable primary logs or concealing spillover displaced onto external reception perimeters.

### When there is no outside

Selection bias stops biting only when you want a purely **descriptive** tally of a completely enumerated closed roster ($f = 1$ with equal weights), where the ledger defines the entire group by rule and nobody in that roster was skipped. Even then, an internal $100\unicode{x25}$ roster never generalizes outside its walls, covers people excluded from the form's categories, or excuses hidden harm dumped onto bystanders. Whenever the real universe exceeds the table, name your sources, estimate the unseen complement honestly ([Chapter 34](34-sample-population-archive.md)), and warn readers when overlapping lists fall short.
