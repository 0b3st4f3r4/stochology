> First chapter of the philosophical block in Part II. It asks the question the rest of the book
> keeps presupposing: what is it for a claim to be known, rather than merely believed or merely
> useful?

## By the end of this chapter you can

1. State the classical analysis of knowledge and the objection that broke it.
2. Distinguish knowledge from true belief, from justified belief, and from consensus.
3. Say why an institution that needs knowledge cannot be satisfied with any of the substitutes.

## 1. The Classical Tripartite Analysis

Consider a municipal credit committee convening on a Monday morning to sign off on a small-business lending
facility. Its launch memorandum declares that the institution "knows" twelve-month portfolio default rates will
remain below a stated tolerance. The signatures dry at $t\_0$. Three distinct conditions meet in the room. The actual
twelve-month default rate lies in the unlived future: whether the proposition $P$ ("defaults stay below the
tolerance") is true cannot be inspected until next year's ledger closes. The committee's endorsement of $P$ is
already complete, since institutional belief is cheap to manufacture by vote or directive. The stress model and the
historical default series attached to the memorandum are the committee's warrant, the only element an independent
auditor can inspect before capital leaves the vault.

Western epistemology spent over two millennia on the question inside that committee room, formulated in Plato's
*Meno* (98a) and *Theaetetus* (201c–210a) (Plato c. 360 BCE): what distinguishes mere true opinion (*orthē dóxa*)
from knowledge (*epistēmē*)? Plato observed that true opinions, like the unmoored statues of Daedalus, run away
until tied down by an explanatory calculation of the reason (*aitías logismós*). The twentieth century crystallised
the metaphor into the tripartite analysis of knowledge as **Justified True Belief (JTB)**:

$$\mathrm{Knows}(S, P) \iff P \land B\_S(P) \land J\_S(P),$$

where $S$ is a cognitive or institutional agent and $P$ is a proposition:

1. **Truth ($P$)**: $P$ is true. If $P$ is false ($P = 0$), it cannot be known, regardless of the conviction of the believer.
2. **Belief ($B\_S(P)$)**: $S$ believes that $P$. An unendorsed claim is not knowledge held by an agent.
3. **Justification ($J\_S(P)$)**: $S$ is justified in believing that $P$. A wild guess or a lucky roll of the dice that happens to land on the truth does not constitute knowledge.

Believing is answerable to truth by its own constitution rather than by an external rule, so a belief held without adequate evidence is defective as a belief and not merely as a claim to knowledge (Adler 2002).

Two analytical limit cases bound the conjunction. In the first, coherent internal reasons attach to a false
proposition ($P = 0, B\_S(P) = 1, J\_S(P) = 1$), and the conjunction fails because "false knowledge" is a
contradiction. In the second, belief matches reality by chance with no explanatory tether
($P = 1, B\_S(P) = 1, J\_S(P) = 0$). In institutional judgment under structural uncertainty the truth condition is
inaccessible at the moment an action must be launched: action precedes the outcome. Belief is inexpensive to
manufacture. Justification is where epistemological integrity resides, and where institutional design routinely
founders.

## 2. The Gettier Problem and Institutional Epistemic Luck

An automated clinical triage system flags Patient X for immediate sepsis therapy, the therapy is administered, and
the patient recovers. An audit reveals that the model placed most of its weight on an artifactual portable-scanner
header ("Scanner 04") rather than on vital signs, and that the patient's fever broke because of an unrecorded
ambulance dose. The chart is "justified" and "true". The diagnostic process was blind.

In 1963 Edmund Gettier shattered the sufficiency of the tripartite analysis in a three-page paper (Gettier 1963).
His counterexamples show an agent holding a belief that is justified and true and still not knowledge, because the
truth of the belief is connected to reality only by chance. The canonical structure: an agent has strong empirical
justification for a false claim $A$, logically deduces $B$ from $A$, and $B$ happens to be true because of an
independent state of affairs $C$ that the agent never observed. The belief in $B$ is justified and true. It is also
a product of epistemic luck.

Quantifying the exposure takes two counts. Suppose an institution forms $N$ justified beliefs. $p > 0$ of them are
true *because* the process functioned competently, and $c \ge 0$ of the remaining $N - p$ process misses turn out
true by independent coincidence. The JTB ledger counts $p + c$ items as knowledge. Two ratios follow, the **Gettier
contamination fraction** and the **credit inflation ratio**:

$$G(p, c) = \frac{c}{p + c} \in [0, 1], \qquad I(p, c) = \frac{p + c}{p} = 1 + \frac{c}{p}.$$

In the **no-coincidence case** ($c \to 0, p > 0$), $G(p, 0) = 0$ and every true belief is earned by method. In the
**pure-coincidence case** ($p \to 0^+, c > 0$), $G(p, c) \to 1$ and $I(p, c) \to \infty$: every apparent piece of
"knowledge" is environmental charity. In institutional life **institutional Gettier cases** are ubiquitous and
pernicious:

- A financial risk model justifies extreme leverage by concluding that market default correlations are low; a catastrophic liquidity freeze is averted solely because an unmodeled central bank emergency swap line is deployed.
- A clinical diagnostic system recommends an effective treatment, but does so based on an artifactual hospital identifier in the training data rather than biological etiology.

In both instances the decision was "justified" and the outcome was "favorable", and the institution learned
nothing. The credit ratchet runs the wrong way. When benign tailwinds multiply coincidences, contamination $G$ rises
and the overcrediting ratio $I$ rises with it, so the process looks most reliable exactly where the world is being
most generous. It mistakes luck for operational competence, reinforcing fragile procedures that collapse on the first
unshielded encounter with reality.

## 3. Epistemic Substitutes: Consensus, Confidence, and Pragmatism

Picture a quarterly risk review. The committee votes unanimously to trim reserves because the presenter is fluent
and no breach has occurred in three calm years, unaware that the model tracks a narrow proxy while tail risk
accumulates outside its frame. Three counterfeit currencies stand in for epistemic warrant. **Consensus** reflects
sociology, shared training or incentives rather than truth, and unanimous concurrence manufactured by silencing
dissent is an agnological blindfold. **Subjective confidence** is driven by fluency, narrative coherence and
bureaucratic rank; no agent can introspect its own calibration, which only severe out-of-sample testing measures.
**Practical success** can occur for reasons divorced from a procedure's stated theory, and pragmatic success without
structural understanding fails at the first regime shift.

An inquiry can achieve full severe power on a narrow proxy while omitting a larger spillover harm
([Inquiry Openness, Severity, and the Multiverse](43-inquiry-openness-multiverse.md)), producing a Kimball Type III
error (Kimball 1957): the narrow focal gain is positive, the unmeasured spillover harm is negative and larger, and
the global net utility is negative although every test passed. The cost–loss threshold $p^\ast = C / (\eta L)$
splits in the same way. An institution prices its own loss at $L\_{\mathrm{inst}}$, affected citizens face
$L\_{\mathrm{affected}} > L\_{\mathrm{inst}}$. Because $p^\ast$ is decreasing in $L$, the citizens' threshold sits
strictly below the institution's, and the gap between the two thresholds is exactly the interval in which the
institution declines to act on a risk it would want acted on if the loss were its own.

## 4. Fallibilism, Severity, and Virtue Epistemology

Charles Sanders Peirce established **fallibilism** (Peirce 1899/1931): all empirical knowledge is provisional,
subject to revision upon further inquiry. Scientific warrant for Peirce does not require infallible Cartesian
certainty. It requires that hypotheses be exposed to energetic attempts at empirical refutation.

Imre Lakatos and Deborah Mayo formalised this into **error-statistical severity** (Lakatos 1970; Mayo 2018):
evidence warrants a claim only to the extent that the claim has survived severe tests that had a high probability of
detecting its errors had it been false. A claim clears the minimum relevant effect $\delta\_{\min}$ only when the
lower Imbens–Manski partial-identification bound (Imbens & Manski 2004) satisfies
$L\_{\mathrm{IM}}(\alpha) = L\_{\mathrm{Manski}} - c\_\alpha\mkern3mu\mathrm{SE}\_L > \delta\_{\min}$, with
critical thresholds and pre-registered falsifiers derived in [Philosophy of Science](18-philosophy-of-science.md) §4.
Epistemic warrant resides in an audit trail of survived tests, not in a static property of a model.

Ernest Sosa resolved the Gettier dilemma through **virtue epistemology** (Sosa 2007; Sosa 2015), evaluating
performance across three hierarchical tiers. **Accuracy** asks whether the shot hit the target ($P$ is true).
**Adroitness** asks whether the shot was taken with technical skill, which is competent execution of method.
**Aptness** asks whether the shot was accurate *because* it was adroit: the success was produced by the competence
and not by luck.

Aptness connects to the Stoic archer of Antipater of Tarsus (Cicero 45 BCE). The archer's internal aim (*télos*)
embodies adroitness, while hitting the external target (*skopós*) is exposed to luck. Knowledge is **apt belief**,
performance that succeeds because of manifest competence. Auditing aptness means recording the pair (ex-ante
adroitness, ex-post accuracy) and reading off four states: fully apt performance, adroit ex-ante with an unlucky
outcome, lucky accuracy alone, and inapt-and-inaccurate. The read counteracts the Baron–Hershey outcome bias
(Baron & Hershey 1988) in [Moral Luck, Apt Agency, and Institutional Design](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md).

## 5. What Follows for Institutions

An institution cannot possess infallible truth when deciding in the dark. It can build governance architectures
that enforce aptness:

1. **Pre-registration**: Hypotheses and decision rules must be recorded before observing outcomes, preventing post-hoc story-fitting.
2. **Severe falsification**: Models must be confronted with stress regimes designed to expose failure modes.
3. **Auditable justification**: The justification trail must decouple competence from lucky outcomes.

### How much of knowledge is luck

Take a thousand beliefs produced by a process that is right most of the time, and let a share of its misses turn
out true anyway. The classical analysis counts every one of them as knowledge; only the ones attributable to the
process are earned. The luck share $G(p, c) = c/(p + c)$ grows with the generosity of the world rather than with the
rigour of the justification, and a better justification cannot repair it.

The audit can be repaired. A claim that passes a severe test with its power declared has its failure mode named in
advance, and the naming separates it from a true belief that happened to survive. The archive cannot supply the
counterfactual: the comparison sample for the classic training programme carries thousands of controls and not one
treated unit, whereas the randomised trial that supplies the treatment effect contains both arms. Conversely, when a
low-stakes error costs little and a process audit costs a great deal, a small Gettier contamination carries an
expected loss smaller than the audit's own cost, so auditing costs more than the harm it prevents. That is the
counterexample regime where justified true belief suffices.

### When the truth cannot be checked in time

The thesis fails when the process is the only observable thing and the truth is not verifiable before the
decision. Audit the process instead of declaring knowledge. Replace "is true" by "passes this severe test, with this
power declared" and report the substitution (Gettier 1963), noting the price: every claim carries one declared test,
one declared power and one declared failure mode, so an institution that audits a thousand claims a year owes a
thousand such declarations.
