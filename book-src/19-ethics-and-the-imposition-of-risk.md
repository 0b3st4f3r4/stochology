> Fourth chapter of the philosophical block in Part II. The subject is not what is good, but who may
> impose which risk on whom, and on what authority.

## By the end of this chapter you can

1. State the three classical families of ethical theory and the question each one asks first.
2. Explain why imposing risk on others is a different act from imposing it on yourself.
3. Apply the contractualist test to a decision rule and say what fails it.

## 1. Three Ethical Lineages and the Aggregation Fallacy

Consider a transit authority celebrating a spreadsheet that reroutes a hazardous chemical freight corridor through a
dense residential district. Shaving minutes off regional delivery times yields a certain aggregate gain $G$ across
commercial shippers. Residents along the corridor absorb an uncompensated expected spill loss $D$ with $D > G$.

Normative ethics offers three distinct frameworks for evaluating actions under uncertainty:

- **Consequentialism** (Bentham, Mill): Evaluates an act exclusively by the value of its aggregated outcomes ($W = \sum\_i u\_i$). Its operational danger is the **aggregation fallacy**: maximizing total expected utility licenses the imposition of catastrophic, uncompensated ruin on a vulnerable minority if the aggregate sum of minor conveniences across the majority is sufficiently large.
- **Deontology** (Kant 1996 [1785]): Evaluates an act by whether it complies with universal moral duties. Kant's formula of humanity mandates treating persons always as ends in themselves, never merely as instruments. It forbids sacrificing unconsenting individuals for collective optimization, but struggles to provide operational thresholds when all available choices carry non-zero statistical risks.
- **Virtue Ethics** (Aristotle *c.* 335 BCE): Evaluates the character, practical wisdom (*phrónēsis*), and internal aim (*télos*) of the acting agent. It captures the nuance of situated judgment, but resists codification into formal administrative rules that an external auditor can inspect.

Stochology does not treat the three lineages as mutually exclusive options. Any sustainable decision governance has
to be constrained from all three directions at once: it must account for physical consequences, respect
non-instrumental deontic rights, and evaluate the internal virtue and calibration of the decision-maker.

Interpersonal aggregation is blocked by pricing a mandatory **solvency buffer surcharge** for any third party exposed
to irreversible harm $E\_{\mathrm{harm}} > 0$:

$$B\_{\mathrm{solvency}} = E\_{\mathrm{harm}} \cdot \gamma\_{\mathrm{consent}} \cdot \gamma\_{\mathrm{voice}},$$

where $\gamma\_{\mathrm{consent}}$ is below one for an exposed party who consented and above one for a party who did
not, and $\gamma\_{\mathrm{voice}}$ equals one when an effective voice is available to the exposed party and above
one when that voice is silenced.

With consent and voice both present, the consensual weight is a small fraction of the unconsented silenced weight,
because both factors sit at or below unity. With neither present, the institution must post the product of the two
surcharges as a multiple of the irreversible harm exposure in liquid reserves before acting. That is the ledger's way
of saying that the party who cannot refuse is the party the balance sheet has to protect.

## 2. The Asymmetry of Non-Reciprocal Risk Imposition

Picture a planner who redraws a night-bus route or a pre-trial custody threshold from an office miles from the
affected precinct. The planner never waits at the unlit stop and never faces the custody desk. Traditional decision
theory treats risk symmetrically: an agent faces lottery $L = (p, x; 1-p, y)$ and maximises expected utility
$\mathbb{E}[u(L)]$. Sven Ove Hansson (2013) showed what that framing obscures: **deciding to take a
risk for oneself is morally distinct from imposing that risk on someone else**.

Non-reciprocal risk imposition involves three structural asymmetries:

1. **Absence of consent**: The individuals who bear the physical or financial downside did not choose the risk; they were exposed to it by an external authority.
2. **Epistemic opacity**: The exposed individuals cannot audit the mathematical model or risk assumptions that justified their exposure; the model is proprietary or administratively remote.
3. **Decoupled incentives (Moral hazard)**: The upside of the risk (profits, executive bonuses, political expediency) accrues to the decision-maker, while the catastrophic downside (displacement, bankruptcy, injury, mortality) is externalized onto vulnerable third parties.

Stochology measures the asymmetry through Alexandra Chouldechova's (2017) **fairness trilemma**
([Moral Luck, Apt Agency, and Institutional Design](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md)). Take any imperfect binary risk
classification satisfying predictive parity — equal positive predictive value ($\mathrm{PPV}\_A = \mathrm{PPV}\_B \in (0, 1)$) — across groups $g \in \{A, B\}$ with
base-rate prevalences $p\_A \ne p\_B$ in $(0, 1)$ and $\mathrm{FNR}\_g < 1$. The group false-positive rate then satisfies an exact algebraic identity:

$$\mathrm{FPR}\_g = \frac{p\_g}{1 - p\_g}\mkern3mu\frac{1 - \mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}\mkern3mu(1 - \mathrm{FNR}\_g), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{FPR}\_A}{\mathrm{FPR}\_B} = \frac{p\_A / (1 - p\_A)}{p\_B / (1 - p\_B)} \quad (\text{when }\mathrm{PPV}\_A=\mathrm{PPV}\_B \in (0, 1),\mkern5mu\mathrm{FNR}\_A=\mathrm{FNR}\_B < 1).$$

The identity has two ends. When $p\_A = p\_B$, a classifier with equal positive predictive value and equal false-negative rates achieves equal false-positive rates
($\mathrm{FPR}\_A / \mathrm{FPR}\_B = 1$). Because $\mathrm{FPR}\_g = \mathrm{FP}\_g / \mathrm{Neg}\_g$ is
conditioned on true negatives, doubling a group's prevalence shifts $\mathrm{PPV}$ and absolute flag counts while
leaving its empirical $\mathrm{FPR}$ unchanged.

On any real custody or screening rule the identity shows up as a gap. When two groups with different base rates are held to
predictive parity at one decision rule, equalising one error rate forces disparity on the other, and under equal false-negative rates the ratio
of their false-positive rates is fixed by the ratio of their base-rate odds rather than by anything anyone chose at
the threshold. Sizing a vital margin is the moral duty to prevent externalising fatal risk onto non-beneficiary
populations.

## 3. Scanlonian Contractualism and the Reasonability Test

T. M. Scanlon (1998) formulated **contractualism**: an act or policy is wrong if its performance
could only be justified by principles that affected persons could **reasonably reject**. Contractualism evaluates
policies from the standpoint of individual affected parties, and it forbids utilitarian interpersonal aggregation
outright. When auditing an automated or institutional decision rule, the contractualist test asks:

- What is the worst-case burden imposed on the most vulnerable affected individual?
- Could that individual reasonably reject a principle that permits this exposure, given the alternative policies available?
- Does the decision rule provide meaningful justification in terms that the exposed person could recognize as valid?

Under contractualism, a decision rule that sacrifices an innocent or vulnerable party to optimise average
institutional efficiency is categorically rejected. An imposition is contractually justified only when three joint
conditions hold: **reciprocity**, **presumed consent**, and **reversibility**.

A dated historical failure illustrates all three being violated at once. In August 2020 the UK qualification regulator
Ofqual replaced teacher-assessed A-level grades with a historical school-ranking algorithm to prevent aggregate grade
inflation. It downgraded a large share of student entries without individual consent, with benefits accruing to
university admissions offices and irreversible loss of that cycle's university places. The algorithm was revoked
completely on 17 August 2020 (Office of Qualifications and Examinations Regulation (Ofqual) 2020).

## 4. Moral Luck and the Residue of Agent-Regret

Bernard Williams (1981) and Thomas Nagel (1979) identified the paradox of **moral luck**: judgments of agents depend heavily on factors outside their control. **Resultant luck** governs how
actions turn out under noise, so two drivers take an identical reckless turn and one meets an empty road.
**Circumstantial luck** governs the crises an agent is thrust into. **Constitutive luck** governs the traits and
capacities an agent is endowed with.

Williams showed that an adroit agent whose competent, well-calibrated action nevertheless causes catastrophic harm
experiences a profound psychological residue: **agent-regret**. In corporate hierarchies (Holmström 1979; Innes 1990),
executives routinely invert moral luck by starving frontline units of buffers and blaming operators when a shock hits.

Decomposing total deficit variance into structural, operational, and exogenous shares makes the inversion measurable.
In the case that motivates the decomposition, structural under-buffering accounts for the overwhelming majority of
deficit variance and frontline operations for a small fraction, yet management assigns most of the blame to the
frontline. The **blame inversion ratio** — assigned frontline blame divided by the operational share — runs to more
than an order of magnitude. Moral luck therefore requires two structural safeguards: an ex-ante bitemporal seal judging the operator strictly on the evidence and capacity available at decision time, and a variance-attribution audit separating executive under-buffering from frontline operational error before blame is assigned.

## 5. Performative Prediction and Donald MacKenzie's Performativity

In social and financial systems, models are not passive observers of an invariant reality. Donald MacKenzie (2006)
demonstrated in *An Engine, Not a Camera* that financial models exhibit **Barnesian performativity**:
using the model alters market behaviour, forcing reality into closer alignment with the model's theoretical
assumptions until systemic limits trigger liquidity crises.

Juan Perdomo et al. (2020) formalised the dynamic as **performative prediction**. In a static world, data are drawn from a fixed distribution:
$(X, Y) \sim \mathcal{D}$. In a reactive world, deploying decision rule $\theta$ changes the underlying distribution
itself:

$$(X, Y) \sim \mathcal{D}(\theta).$$

Predicting crime risk changes police deployment, which changes where arrests occur; credit scoring changes borrower
behaviour; automated grading changes student study strategies. Suppose a credit authority deploys a restrictive
scoring cutoff $\theta\_1$ in a neighbourhood based on historical defaults drawn from $\mathcal{D}(\theta\_0)$. By
denying working-capital rollovers to solvent small businesses, the deployment of $\theta\_1$ induces a wave of
liquidity defaults in $\mathcal{D}(\theta\_1)$ that would never have occurred under $\theta\_0$. On the retrospective
balance sheet, the model appears "confirmed" by the very defaults its own intervention manufactured.

The ex-ante gate guards against this performative trap by combining calibration, multi-environment invariance, and
out-of-sample discrepancy through a non-compensatory fold over those three instrument axes, so that no single
flattering axis can carry the verdict. That fold is the gate's own: it is taken over the validity of an instrument
and not over the margins of the Nuclear Triad, and the two are never the same object. The gate's fold is fixed before
the outcome exists and admits or refuses one candidate model; the triad's minimum is folded from a finished
trajectory and refuses nothing. An institution that ignores performative feedback optimises its models against a
ghost distribution, and the system induces the very failures it claimed to forecast.

### The numbers nobody in the room chose

A custody rule that flags repeated prior checks — or a single check, for arrestees who are unemployed and
non-citizens — produces false positives at very different rates in two groups. The gap is a property of the rule,
not of anyone's intent. One group receives a large share of wrongful flags per thousand negatives and the other a
much smaller share, and the base-rate odds fix the ratio of the two rates, not the threshold. Nobody in the room chose
those rates. That is the shape of a non-reciprocal risk imposition: an aggregate that nets to a gain can still be an
injustice.

The asymmetry survives aggregation. A programme with a certain gain and a larger expected loss nets to a deficit,
and the net says nothing about who received the gain and who carried the loss, which is the only question the third
party asks. The philosophical name for that case is testimonial injustice: the person bearing the risk is heard and
discounted in the same move (Fricker 2007).

A rate is a property of a rule. The wrong is done one arrest at a time. Three conditions make an imposition
justified rather than merely permitted, and all three are conditions on the bearer's position: reciprocity, presumed
consent and reversibility.

### When the third party cannot be identified

The thesis fails when the risk is diffuse and no bearer can be named, because there is then no one to reciprocate
with and no case-by-case consent to seek. Convert the decision into an ex-ante rule with periodic review. A rule can
be justified to an anonymous public in a way that no single finding can (Frick 2015).

## Exercises

1. From two group false-positive rates, compute the wrongful flags per thousand negatives in each group and their difference, and say which quantity the ratio fixes (answer: each rate times one thousand gives the count, the difference is the excess burden, and the ratio is fixed by the base-rate odds).
2. Compute the aggregate balance of a programme whose certain gain is smaller than its expected loss, and say what the net hides (answer: a deficit; who received the gain and who carried the loss).
3. Construct a case of justified risk imposition and name the three conditions that justify it (answer: reciprocity, presumed consent and reversibility).
4. State what to do when the third party cannot be identified (answer: an ex-ante rule with periodic review, not case-by-case judgment).
