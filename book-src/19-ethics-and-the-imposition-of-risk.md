## 1. Three Ethical Lineages and the Aggregation Fallacy

Imagine choosing to ride a motorcycle across town in the rain. You accept the danger yourself in exchange for speed. Now imagine a logistics firm venting toxic solvent fumes over a sleeping neighborhood to save delivery time. The firm takes the gain while unconsenting neighbors absorb the hazard. Deciding how to weigh those two risks is the core problem of risk ethics.

Consider a transit authority whose spreadsheet reroutes hazardous chemical freight through a dense residential corridor. Shippers gain certain delivery savings $G$ (distinct from [What Knowledge Is](16-what-knowledge-is.md)'s Gettier fraction $G(p, c)$) that exceed corridor residents' expected spill harm $D$, so total net surplus $W = G - D$ looks positive ($G > D$). Worse still, leaving external harm off the internal ledger lets an office approve routes even when $D > G$—a split-ledger failure examined in [Chapter 36](36-decision-loss-threshold.md).

Moral philosophy offers three classic traditions for judging decisions under uncertainty:

- **Classical Aggregative Utilitarianism** (Bentham 1789 [1780]; Mill 1863): Ranks choices by summed welfare ($W = \sum\_i u\_i$). Its built-in trap is the **aggregation fallacy**: maximizing a total average can endorse crushing harm on a minority whenever small gains across a large majority sum higher.
- **Deontology** (Kant 1996 [1785]): Tests choices against universal moral duties. Kant's humanity principle forbids treating unconsenting people merely as tools for others' gain, though strict bans struggle to pick thresholds when every feasible choice carries some statistical risk.
- **Virtue Ethics** (Aristotle *c.* 335 BCE): Judges practical wisdom (*phrónēsis*) and soundness of aim (*télos*). It illuminates character and judgment, yet resists simple translation into auditable public rules.

Plural global traditions reinforce that anti-aggregative boundary. Sub-Saharan relational ethics built around *Ubuntu* and Akan communal personhood locate moral wrongness in breaking community solidarity and dumping unshared harm onto neighbors (Wiredu 1996; Metz 2007). Nahuatl *Huehuetlahtolli* taught moral action as walking carefully on slippery earth (*tlalticpac*), where excess tips ruin onto the whole household and town (Burkhart 1989). Amartya Sen's capability approach likewise refuses to trade away basic human freedoms for an aggregate income sum (Sen 1999). Stochology combines these insights into joint constraints: legitimate judgment weighs consequences, enforces ethical limits against instrumentalizing people, shields external reception perimeters from dumped spillover, and audits the decider's own skill.

Consent, cash reserves, and caution are not interchangeable. Whenever an act threatens **irreversible physical harm** or breaches a non-negotiable right, cost–benefit pricing stops applying ([Chapter 36](36-decision-loss-threshold.md)). No bank reserve can purchase unconsented ruin. Where prior contestation is impossible during an acute emergency, legitimacy requires a pre-declared ceiling, an automatic sunset, and bounded post-event repair ([Chapter 20](20-legitimacy-and-institutions.md)).

Schemes that silence affected voices fail contractualist standards immediately. Solvency accounting specifies a **solvency buffer** $B\_{\mathrm{solvency}}$ only when a risk is **reversible**, carries positive monetizable expected harm $E\_{\mathrm{harm}} > 0$, and passes prior contestability (§3; Hansson 2004). Define $\gamma\_{\mathrm{consent}} \ge 1$ as $1$ under explicit informed consent and above $1$ when polling individual consent is impractical. Similarly, set $\gamma\_{\mathrm{voice}} \ge 1$ to $1$ under direct prior voice and above $1$ when contestation channels remain indirect:

$$B\_{\mathrm{solvency}} = E\_{\mathrm{harm}} \cdot \gamma\_{\mathrm{consent}} \cdot \gamma\_{\mathrm{voice}}.$$

Read aloud, this illustrative buffer scales expected reversible harm by consent and indirect-voice surcharges ($\gamma\_{\mathrm{consent}}, \gamma\_{\mathrm{voice}} \ge 1$), keeping $B\_{\mathrm{solvency}} \ge E\_{\mathrm{harm}}$. Silenced voice rejects an action outright with no finite $\gamma\_{\mathrm{voice}}$, and no surcharge monetizes irreversible ruin or limited-liability self-certification priced only off internal ledgers while ignoring external perimeters ([Chapter 40](40-institution-incentive-metric.md); [Chapter 49](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md)).

## 2. The Asymmetry of Non-Reciprocal Risk Imposition

Far from an unlit bus stop or a custody intake desk, an administrator redraws a late-night route or a pre-trial bail rule. Standard decision theory treats a gamble $L = (p, x; 1-p, y)$ symmetrically, asking only whether one agent maximizes $\mathbb{E}[u(L)]$. Sven Ove Hansson (2013) exposed the flaw: **taking a gamble with your own life or money differs morally from imposing that gamble on someone else**.

Non-reciprocal risk imposition splits benefit from harm along three lines:

1. **Absence of consent**: Bearers of physical or financial ruin never chose the gamble designed by a distant office.
2. **Epistemic opacity**: Exposed residents cannot inspect the proprietary assumptions justifying their exposure.
3. **Decoupled incentives (Moral hazard)**: Upside flows to the decider (fees, speed, budget savings), while catastrophic downside (injury, eviction, insolvency) lands on third parties.

Automated scoring sharpens this asymmetry through Alexandra Chouldechova's (2017) **fairness trilemma**. Whenever two populations differ in underlying base rates, any imperfect binary classifier faces a hard trade-off: matching predictive value across groups forces unequal false-positive or false-negative error rates, whereas equalizing error rates alters predictive values. Cash reserves cannot resolve who absorbs false alarms or missed dangers; blocking non-reciprocal error dumping belongs to governance and causal oversight ([Chapter 39](39-cause-intervention-counterfactual.md); [Moral Luck, Apt Agency, and Institutional Design](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md)).

## 3. Scanlonian Contractualism and the Reasonability Test

T. M. Scanlon (1998) grounded wrongness in **contractualism**: an act is wrong if governed by rules affected people could **reasonably reject**. Unlike utilitarianism, contractualism checks justification individually from each standpoint:

- How heavy a burden does this rule place on each person compared with alternative rules?
- Could an exposed person reasonably reject a rule permitting this risk given safer alternatives?
- Does the policy offer reasons the exposed person can recognize as fair?

Contractualism bars sacrificing an innocent bystander for average efficiency without collapsing into bare aggregate maximin: each person's burden is weighed pairwise against alternatives, so neither group totals nor trivial side complaints dictate policy unilaterally (Scanlon 1998; Steuwer 2021). Demanding literal unanimity would freeze cooperation behind single vetoes, whereas vague hypothetical consent invites abuse. Risk-taking must belong to an equitable system that genuinely works to the exposed person's advantage (Hansson 2004), pairing **reciprocity** with **contestable prior justification** ([Chapter 20](20-legitimacy-and-institutions.md)) and **rule revisability with bounded post-event repair** when harm cannot be undone.

The August 2020 UK A-level grading crisis illustrates what happens when those conditions fail. Aiming for year-to-year comparability while asking universities for flexibility, regulator Ofqual replaced teacher-estimated centre grades with a standardization model. Across 718,276 A-level entries in its interim report, 35.6% were marked down by one grade, 3.3% by two, and 0.2% by three or more, while 2.2% rose by one grade and 58.7% stayed unchanged. Without prior contestability, public backlash forced an emergency reversal on 17 August 2020 granting students the higher of their centre-estimated or moderated grade and conceding *"real anguish and damaged public confidence"* (Office of Qualifications and Examinations Regulation (Ofqual) 2020).

## 4. Moral Luck and the Residue of Agent-Regret

Two equally reckless drivers run a red light at midnight. One clears an empty intersection with a traffic ticket; the other strikes a pedestrian stepping off the curb. Both made the identical negligent choice, yet the world punishes them worlds apart. Conversely, a conscientious bridge inspector following every sound test may still witness an unprecedented quake topple a span.

Bernard Williams (1981) and Thomas Nagel (1979) termed this entanglement **moral luck**: judgment bending to events outside an agent's control—**resultant luck** in how noise falls, **circumstantial luck** in which crisis strikes, and **constitutive luck** in native endowment. Williams showed that a careful agent whose sound decision ends in tragedy still feels **agent-regret**, distinct from guilt over negligence. Inside organizations, limited liability creates convex, heads-I-win-tails-you-lose payoffs (Innes 1990; Holmström 2016), so Holmström's (1979) **Informativeness Principle** judges managers using external shock signals and reserve capacity—exposing blame inversion when leaders strip buffers in calm times and scapegoat frontline staff during crashes ([Chapter 40](40-institution-incentive-metric.md); [Chapter 49](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md)).

An orthogonal variance breakdown separates accountability from scapegoating. After assigning any operator error induced by starved staffing or structural shortcuts upstream to structural failure, split total accident variance into three non-negative orthogonal shares summing to one—structural reserve cuts ($v\_{\mathrm{struct}} \ge 0$), residual frontline operational error ($v\_{\mathrm{op}} \ge 0$), and pure exogenous shock ($v\_{\mathrm{exog}} \ge 0$). Among controllable human causes ($v\_{\mathrm{op}} + v\_{\mathrm{struct}} > 0$), operational execution carries share $v\_{\mathrm{ctrl}} := v\_{\mathrm{op}} / (v\_{\mathrm{op}} + v\_{\mathrm{struct}})$. If frontline workers receive blame share $b\_{\mathrm{fl}} \in [0, 1]$ ($b\_{\mathrm{fl}} + b\_{\mathrm{struct}} = 1$), the **blame inversion ratio** $b\_{\mathrm{fl}} / v\_{\mathrm{ctrl}}$ ($v\_{\mathrm{op}} > 0$) tracks fairness:

- When $b\_{\mathrm{fl}} / v\_{\mathrm{ctrl}} = 1$, blame matches controllable causation even alongside outside shocks ($v\_{\mathrm{exog}} > 0$); when $b\_{\mathrm{fl}} / v\_{\mathrm{ctrl}} < 1$ (including $b\_{\mathrm{fl}} = 0$ with $v\_{\mathrm{op}} > 0$), systemic claims shield genuine execution negligence.
- When structural failure dominates ($v\_{\mathrm{struct}} > 0$) and $b\_{\mathrm{fl}}$ stays positive as $v\_{\mathrm{op}} \to 0^+$, the ratio diverges; at $v\_{\mathrm{op}} = 0$ with $b\_{\mathrm{fl}} > 0$, workers take pure scapegoat blame (and blame when $v\_{\mathrm{op}} + v\_{\mathrm{struct}} = 0$ punishes exogenous luck).

Which share actually dominated is an empirical question, not a slogan: when operational negligence dominates, execution bears blame ([Chapter 40](40-institution-incentive-metric.md); [Chapter 49](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md)). Defeating hindsight moral luck requires locking decision standards before outcomes arrive while keeping revision open when conditions shift ([Aim and the Three Planes](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md)).

## 5. Performative Prediction and Donald MacKenzie's Performativity

Models in markets and public policy do not passively film an unchanging landscape. Donald MacKenzie (2006) argued in *An Engine, Not a Camera* that financial equations actively reshape the markets they describe: **Barnesian performativity** when traders adapt habits to fit a formula, and **counterperformativity** when widespread adoption destabilizes liquidity and breaks the model's premises ([Chapter 48](48-reactive-worlds-performativity-goodhart.md)).

Juan Perdomo et al. (2020) formalized this feedback as **performative prediction**: choosing a decision rule $\theta$ shifts the outcome distribution to $(X, Y) \sim \mathcal{D}(\theta)$. Crime forecasts redirect police patrols; credit rules alter borrowing; automated grading reshapes study habits. When a lender tightens cutoff $\theta\_1$ after observing passive defaults under $\theta\_0$, cutting off credit triggers liquidity defaults in $\mathcal{D}(\theta\_1)$—making the harsher cutoff appear "validated" by failures it caused. Checking calibration, causal stability, and performative feedback pre-deployment ([Chapter 48](48-reactive-worlds-performativity-goodhart.md)) is therefore a pre-outcome instrument guard, distinct from retrospective trajectory evaluation ([Chapter 41](41-the-triad-and-the-minimum.md); [Chapter 50](50-methodological-families-reflexivity.md)).

### Why aggregate rates conceal the bearer

Even when total benefits outweigh harms ($G > D$) or average predictive parity holds, aggregate numbers never tell us who absorbed uncompensated loss. Separate from statistical averages, **testimonial injustice** discounts warnings by rank or standing (Fricker 2007), demanding the rank-blind intake and attention-bounded triage of [Chapter 18](18-philosophy-of-science.md) and [Chapter 20](20-legitimacy-and-institutions.md) ([Chapter 49](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md)).

### When the third party cannot be identified

Reciprocal justification tightens when risk spreads thinly across a large anonymous public—such as a municipal vaccine rollout or emissions permit—so everyone faces tiny *ex ante* odds while a few unidentified people will almost certainly suffer *ex post* harm (Frick 2015). Johann Frick's (2015) decomposition test requires multi-stage policies to stay justifiable at every phase of agency. Anonymity never licenses masking concentrated ruin across unrepresented external reception perimeters, demanding periodic audits and reopened rules when living norms shift.
