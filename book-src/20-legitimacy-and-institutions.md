> Fifth and last chapter of the philosophical block in Part II. The subject is the bridge from ethics
> to design: what makes a decision taken on behalf of others legitimate, and what makes it auditable.

## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Distinguish legitimacy from correctness, and say why an institution needs the first to survive being
   wrong.
2. State what accountability requires beyond the existence of a signed decision.
3. Explain why contestability and not benevolence is the structural safeguard.

## 1. Legitimacy Versus Correctness

Consider two water utilities that both survive a dry summer without rationing. The first operated without published
reserve rules, ignored sensor calibration, and gambled on a late-August rainstorm that arrived two days before the
reservoir hit dead storage. The second followed a pre-registered drawdown schedule, published its trigger thresholds
in spring, and held an auditable emergency reserve. Over a single season ($T = 1$) their outcomes look identical.
Over repeated seasons ($T \to \infty$) the first is absorbed at the ruin barrier by the first multi-year drought,
while the second survives being wrong about any single week's weather and retains civic trust.

An institution that makes a correct choice by dumb luck looks superficially indistinguishable from one that makes a
correct choice through calibrated method. Max Weber (1922) distinguished three classical bases of legitimate
authority: traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal. In modern governance under structural
uncertainty, **rational-legal legitimacy** does not require infallibility: an institution cannot guarantee that every
forecast will be accurate or that every policy will succeed. What legitimacy requires is procedural integrity:

- The decision procedure is publicly declared and inspectable *before* outcomes occur;
- The criteria for judgment apply symmetrically to decision-makers and affected populations;
- The evidentiary premises are contestable and subject to external revision when disproven.

A legitimate institution survives being wrong because affected citizens have reason to accept the integrity of the
procedure, even when the outcome is disappointing.

When institutions are conceived as immanent coordination architectures among interdependent agents rather than as
transcendent apparatuses of sovereign command, procedural integrity is inseparable from common survival. Centralised
authorities that hoard vital reserves while externalising catastrophic risk onto vulnerable participants turn their
procedural claims to rational-legal legitimacy into sovereign capture. Immanent institutional legitimacy requires
that the rules governing reserve provisioning, emergency channels, and liability distribution are collectively
constituted and horizontally contestable by those who bear the ultimate costs of systemic failure.

The level at which that constitution is made is part of the requirement, not a detail of its enactment. A rule
governing reserve provisioning is a constraint on the game only if the unit whose payoff it changes cannot revoke it,
because under the competitive regime of [Evolution, Variation, and Selection](25-evolution-variation-selection.md) ($\gamma\_{\mathrm{select}} > 1/T$), a revocable rule is a move in the same game and is repealed by the differential that rewards divestment
before the shock that would have justified the reserve. Benevolence does not supply that
property, and neither does a majority that can be reconstituted. What makes the floor constitutional is that it is
chosen above the units it binds, and legitimacy is a claim about that level as much as about the procedure. An
institution whose reserve rule sits below the level of the units it constrains has published a preference rather than
a constraint.

The same condition governs the governance coordinate, not only the reserve rule. A diagnostic governance reading evaluates rules
that bind, and a rule the assessed unit can revoke is not a constraint whose observance the coordinate can measure. Collapse
Proposition 3 names that failure in its institutional form: limited liability sets the divesting unit's tail cost to
zero ($q = 0$, leaving $\gamma\_{\mathrm{select}} = c$ uncompensated) while status-based testimonial discounting keeps the interval between shocks mispriced
([The Triad and the Minimum](41-the-triad-and-the-minimum.md)). Legitimacy is therefore a claim about the level of
the rule in a second sense as well. An institution whose governance is read at a level below the rules it claims
to hold has recorded a preference and called it a constraint.

Binding is not the only thing a rule can lose. Gramsci's hegemony names the other way to lose it. Where a common
sense has been diffused in which the existing arrangement appears natural, no one proposes to revoke the rule, and
consent is manufactured rather than purchased (Gramsci 1971). An institution can satisfy every formal condition of
contestability and still fail this axis, because the question is not only who may contest a rule but whether the
terms in which it could be contested are available at all.

Hobbes grounded legitimate authority in the authorisation of those who fear the war of all against all, so what an
institution sells for obedience is protection, and an institution that fails to protect has spent the authorisation
(Hobbes 1651).

## 2. The Triad of Institutional Accountability

Picture a post-incident inquiry board requesting the risk-model logs after a flood-gate failure. It discovers that
administrators can overwrite threshold weights after the storm without leaving an audit trail, while a junior shift
operator is dismissed for "human error".

"Accountability" is frequently reduced to a decorative bureaucratic slogan. To be operationally checkable
(Holmström 1979), accountability requires three non-substitutable structural components:

$$\mathrm{Accountability} = \mathrm{Observability} \times \mathrm{Authority} \times \mathrm{Consequence}.$$

1. **Observability**: The decision trail, model assumptions, data vintages, and parameter weights must be independently auditable by parties who played no role in generating them. A decision justified by an opaque, proprietary black box lacks observability. Operationally this means cryptographically sealing the decision before outcomes are observed: committing an RFC 2104 HMAC-SHA256 digest and a hash chain over the decision trail makes opening the holdout a second time an event on the record, permanently downgrading the analysis from confirmatory to exploratory, and makes altering or replaying any log entry detectable ([Moral Luck, Apt Agency, and Institutional Design](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md)).
2. **Authority**: The auditing body or affected party must possess the legal and operational power to halt, quarantine, or overturn the decision, rather than merely submitting non-binding comments.
3. **Consequence**: Material consequences must attach to decision-makers if they disregard evidence, violate safety margins, or suppress warnings. Without personal or organizational consequences, oversight decays into a public relations ritual.

The multiplicative triad has two boundaries. Where all three factors equal $1$, every ex-ante decision rule is
cryptographically sealed, contestable with halting authority, and backed by symmetric liability. If any single
element is missing, the product collapses to zero. Observability without authority produces toothless suggestion
boxes; authority without consequence creates arbitrary bureaucratic discretion; consequence without observability
produces scapegoating of low-ranking frontline workers.

## 3. Philip Pettit's Contestability and Non-Domination

Consider a three-member board voting on a high-pressure pipeline restart, which requires three conjunctive premises:
weld integrity ($P\_1$), valve calibration ($P\_2$) and downstream relief capacity ($P\_3$). Member A doubts $P\_3$
(true, true, false), B doubts $P\_2$ (true, false, true) and C underwrites all three. Polled separately, $P\_1$
passes $3\text{–}0$, $P\_2$ passes $2\text{–}1$ and $P\_3$ passes $2\text{–}1$, yet only one member believes the
pipeline is safe.

Philip Pettit (1997) formulated republican freedom as **non-domination**: an individual is free not when an
authority happens to be benevolent, but when the authority is structurally prevented from exercising arbitrary power
over them. Benevolence is not a safeguard, since a benevolent tyrant can withdraw their kindness at will. The
structural safeguard is **contestability**. An automated or institutional decision system satisfies contestability if
and only if:

- Affected parties are informed of the precise evidentiary reasons and rules that produced the decision;
- They have access to an accessible, independent forum where they can present counter-evidence or prove algorithmic error, through a mandatory channel that applies zero status or credential discount ($\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} = 0$) whenever the claimant bears the error cost, while triaging signals by corroborated likelihood ratio so finite human attention is not flooded by uncorroborated alarms;
- If their contestation is valid, the institution is structurally compelled to alter the decision and remediate any damage.

Contestability transforms an affected subject from a passive recipient of algorithmic coercion into a recognised
interlocutor endowed with epistemic standing. Christian List and Philip Pettit (2011) further proved that collective
bodies face the **discursive dilemma** illustrated in the pipeline scene: a three-member safety
board voting across three conjunctive safety premises can approve every individual premise by a majority while only
one member endorses all three premises simultaneously, so the body's premise-wise verdict and its conjunctive verdict
contradict each other even though no member changed their mind.

Ex-ante contestability has a **structural emergency counterexample**. Facing an imminent systemic payments freeze,
a central bank may have to extend emergency liquidity inside a survival window too brief for a shareholder vote,
public hearing, or judicial contestation. The uncontestable intervention can preserve systemic solvency in the
moment while paying a severe legitimacy price afterward in public backlash and statutory reform. When the survival
window is shorter than the deliberative schedule, an institution must act and pre-commit to mandatory ex-post repair
rather than pretend it deliberated.

## 4. Testimonial Injustice and the Algedonic Bypass

Imagine a floor technician who spots a cooling-pump pressure drop with survival margin $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}}$ before core damage. A direct shutdown takes time $\tau\_{\mathrm{direct}} < \mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}}$ and saves the plant. Layered managerial review committees stretch the response time to $\tau\_{\mathrm{committee}} > \mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}}$, so the warning arrives on time and dies in committee.

Miranda Fricker's (2007) analysis of **testimonial injustice** shows how institutional hierarchies
systematically discount frontline warnings. Stafford Beer (1972) designed the cybernetic antidote in *Brain of the
Firm*: the **algedonic bypass channel**. A frontline alert with per-shift evidence factor $e\_t > 1$, trip
threshold $E\_{\mathrm{trip}}$, and managerial status-based testimonial discount $\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} \in [0, 1)$ requires
recovery stopping times:

$$\mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{undiscounted}} = \left\lceil \frac{\ln E\_{\mathrm{trip}}}{\ln e\_t} \right\rceil, \qquad \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{discounted}} = \left\lceil \frac{\ln E\_{\mathrm{trip}}}{(1 - \delta\_{\mathrm{status}})\ln e\_t} \right\rceil.$$

The channel has two ends. As $\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} \to 0$,
$\mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{discounted}} = \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{undiscounted}}$, so the alarm trips in time whenever
the undiscounted response fits inside the survival window. As $\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} \to 1^-$,
$\mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{discounted}} \to \infty$ and the alarm never trips.

Between the two, any positive status discount inflates recovery time by the factor $1/(1 - \delta\_{\mathrm{status}})$, so a
discount large enough turns a response that fits the window into one that does not: the warning arrives on time and
dies in committee. If a fast shock halves $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}}$, even the undiscounted response can
fail, which is what pre-authorised automatic shutdown is for.

Setting $\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} = 0$ forbids discounting a witness by rank, status, or credential, not filtering by
evidence. Because human behavioral and sensory-motor throughput is bounded near $10\text{ bits/s}$ (Zheng & Meister 2025),
routing every uncorroborated alert unfiltered destroys attentional capacity — as illustrated by external validation of the
Epic Sepsis Model across 27,697 hospitalised patients, which reported a sensitivity of $33\%$ and a positive predictive
value of $12\%$, yielding a derived false-alarm proportion $1 - \mathrm{PPV} = 0.88$ (Wong et al. 2021). The normative
architecture therefore pairs $\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} = 0$ at reception with sequential filtering by corroborated
likelihood ratio $\prod\_{s \le t} e\_s \ge E\_{\mathrm{trip}}$ before consuming executive cognitive bandwidth.

## 5. Hans Jonas's Precautionary Imperative and Irreversible Barriers

In *The Imperative of Responsibility* (1979/1984), Hans Jonas formulated the foundational ethical principle for
modern technological civilisation:

> *"Act so that the effects of your action are compatible with the permanence of genuine human life."*

Jonas recognised that modern technological power introduces a qualitative asymmetry unknown to classical ethics: the
capacity to trigger **irreversible civilisational or biospheric ruin**. An absorbing ruin barrier is an absorbing
Markov state ($R\_t \le 0$): once crossed, recovery is impossible. Under the threat of irreversible ruin, standard
expected-value trade-offs become morally obscene. Survival requires sizing vital reserves so that the survival
margin of [Irreversible Time and the Vital Margin](47-irreversible-time-vital-margin.md) stays non-negative.

Two regimes anchor Jonas's boundary. In the **buffered regime**
($\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} > \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{recovery}}$, with a positive survival margin), the
institution retains positive temporal slack to absorb a shock, convene legitimate contestation, and complete physical
recovery before reserves hit zero. In the **exhausted-slack limit**
($\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} < \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{recovery}}$, with a negative survival margin),
physical absorption occurs before recovery can complete regardless of the quality of executive intentions. An institution that
exhausts its reserves in the name of cost efficiency has violated the precautionary imperative, gambling the
existence of the collective for transient upside.

### Legitimacy does not buy latency

A warning that arrives in time can still arrive too late. With a survival window longer than the undiscounted
response and shorter than the discounted one, the institution had the information, the votes and the mandate. It
lacked a channel whose latency was budgeted, and legitimacy does not repair a delay that exceeds the time to ruin.

The budget is arithmetic. The allowance is the time to survive minus the time to recover, and any longer delay
consumes the margin before the first decision. A deliberative body can be legitimate and late, and the two properties
are measured by different instruments, votes by one and hours by the other.

The latency budget has an electricity-grid twin. A grid operated close to saturation has a time to survive far below
its time to recover, whereas the same grid operated with slack clears both, so the stressed allowance is negative
before any committee meets. When the allowance is already negative, deliberation must be moved before the event or
it is decoration after it (List & Pettit 2011).

The three deliberation tests are separate and a body can fail exactly one: premises sound, decision conjunctive,
discursive dilemma present. A configuration reading (true, false, true) describes an institution that reasoned well
and decided badly, the most common institutional failure and the hardest to see from the minutes.

### When contestation before the fact is impossible

The thesis fails in an irreversible emergency with a certain victim. Contesting beforehand is then impossible, and
the procedure is ex-post repair rather than consent. The condition is a recovery delay that cannot be bounded, the
same failure the vital margin detects when it has no slack. The procedure is a declared barrier plus a mandatory
repair path that prices predatory breach at a penalty multiple under Hannah Arendt's ethic of promise-keeping
(Arendt 1958), with the remainder after the loss registered so that the absence of consent is on the record rather
than in the silence.

## Exercises

1. Compute the delay allowance as survival time minus recovery time, and say what a given delay does to the margin (answer: the allowance is the difference, and a delay above it consumes the margin before the first decision).
2. Distinguish the three deliberation tests and say what $(\text{true}, \text{false}, \text{true})$ means (answer: sound premises, failed conjunctive decision, discursive dilemma present).
3. Construct a case in which illegitimacy is effective and say over what horizon legitimacy pays (answer: a short horizon with bounded loss; legitimacy pays in the tail and on repetition).
4. State the procedure when contesting beforehand is impossible (answer: an irreversible barrier plus mandatory repair; consent cannot be recovered afterwards).
