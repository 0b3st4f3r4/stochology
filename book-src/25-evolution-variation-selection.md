## 1. The Three Algorithmic Conditions of Selection

A hospital network ranks its units every year by one scalar index. The top quartile gains budget, the bottom
decile closes, and new directors are told to copy the top-scoring procedures.

Evolution by natural selection (Darwin, 1859) needs neither foresight nor teleological progress. It is an
algorithmic sorting filter, and it runs wherever three conditions hold:

1. **Phenotypic variation**: Individuals within a population exhibit differences in traits, behaviors, or morphology ($z\_i \in \mathbb{R}$, with some dispersion across the population).
2. **Differential reproductive fitness**: Variants differ in their survival and reproduction rates ($w\_i \ge 0$) in the prevailing environment.
3. **Heritability**: A mechanism exists whereby variations are transmitted across generations (genetic inheritance, cultural transmission, or organizational standard operating procedures).

George Price (1970) and Ronald Fisher (1930) formalized the exact algebra of this filter. In a population of $n$ units where unit $i$ carries trait $z\_i$, achieves fitness $w\_i \ge 0$ with mean $\bar{w} = \frac{1}{n}\sum\_{i=1}^n w\_i > 0$, and transmits offspring with shift $\Delta z\_i = z\_i' - z\_i$, the generational shift in the population mean $\Delta \bar{z} = \bar{z}' - \bar{z}$ obeys **Price's covariance equation**:

$$\Delta \bar{z} = \frac{1}{\bar{w}}\mathrm{Cov}(w\_i, z\_i) + \frac{1}{\bar{w}}\mathbb{E}[w\_i \Delta z\_i].$$

The first term, $\frac{1}{\bar{w}}\mathrm{Cov}(w\_i, z\_i)$, is **selection** acting on standing variation. The second,
$\frac{1}{\bar{w}}\mathbb{E}[w\_i \Delta z\_i]$, is **transmission bias**. Setting $z\_i = w\_i$ with unbiased transmission ($\Delta w\_i = 0$) yields Fisher's **Fundamental Theorem of Natural Selection** (Fisher 1930), $\Delta \bar{w}\_{\mathrm{sel}} = \mathrm{Var}(w\_i) / \bar{w} \ge 0$. Two limits bound the operator:

1. **Nothing left to select ($\mathrm{Var}(z\_i) = 0$):** by Cauchy–Schwarz, $\mathrm{Cov}(w\_i, z\_i) = 0$, so the selective response vanishes however strong the incentive.
2. **Survival independent of the trait ($\mathrm{Cov}(w\_i, z\_i) = 0$):** with survival independent of $z\_i$, $\Delta \bar{z} = \frac{1}{\bar{w}}\mathbb{E}[w\_i \Delta z\_i]$, reducing change to transmission drift.

Where the three conditions coincide, the statistical composition of the population changes mechanically.
Promotions, funding allocations, regulatory inspections reward some behaviors and penalize others. That is an
artificial selection pressure, and it evolves the culture of the workforce, often in directions nobody
intended.

## 2. Fitness Landscapes, Local Optima, and Trade-offs

Sewall Wright (1932) formalized evolutionary dynamics through **fitness landscapes**: a multi-dimensional
topographical map from genotype or phenotype combinations to reproductive fitness. Natural
selection is a local gradient-ascent algorithm. Populations climb the nearest peak.

Landscape geometry imposes two constraints.

The first is **entrapment in local optima**. A population cannot cross a deep adaptive valley of lower fitness
to reach a higher, distant peak. A system becomes exquisitely adapted to a mediocre institutional arrangement,
and trapped there, because every incremental deviation is locally penalized.

The second is **adaptive trade-offs and pleiotropy**. Biological traits are rarely independent, since one gene
influences several. Optimize a single trait (lactation yield in cattle, sprint speed in cheetahs) and the cost
lands on bone density, disease resistance, longevity. There is no free optimization. Every gain is paid for by
sacrificing a margin elsewhere.

When the environment fluctuates, multiplicative reproduction $N\_T = N\_0 \prod\_{t=1}^T W\_t$ enforces
**evolutionary bet-hedging** (Seger & Brockmann 1987). Take a phenotype that multiplies abundantly in every
favourable season and not at all in one catastrophic season. Its arithmetic-mean fitness sits far above
replacement, and it goes extinct at the first catastrophe. Long-run survival maximizes expected logarithmic (geometric-mean) growth (Seger & Brockmann 1987):

$$g = \mathbb{E}[\ln W\_t] \approx \ln \bar{W} - \frac{\sigma\_W^2}{2 \bar{W}^2},$$

which parallels the Itô variance drain and the Kelly fraction in the theory of growth-optimal sizing
(Peters 2019). Desert annuals hedge through seed dormancy. Industrial fisheries management of Northwest
Atlantic cod maximized short-term harvest and truncated the older age classes until recruitment variance
triggered the 1992 stock collapse (Hutchings & Myers 1994).

## 3. Motoo Kimura and Neutral Genetic Drift

Directional change is not the same as adaptation. Motoo Kimura (1968) introduced the **neutral theory of
molecular evolution**. At the molecular and genetic level the vast majority of changes and polymorphisms are
selectively neutral, driven not by Darwinian selection but by stochastic **genetic drift** in finite
populations (Kimura 1968).

In a finite diploid population of effective size $N\_e$, binomial gamete sampling induces a single-generation
allele-frequency variance $\mathrm{Var}(\Delta p) = p(1 - p) / (2N\_e)$. A newly arising neutral mutation has initial frequency $p\_0 = 1/(2N\_e)$ and reaches $100\unicode{x25}$ fixation purely by chance with probability $u(p\_0) = 1/(2N\_e)$ across mean fixation time $\bar{t}\_{\mathrm{fix}} \approx 4N\_e$ generations; the chance of fixation therefore shrinks inversely with population size while the waiting time grows with it. Selection coefficient $s$ dominates drift only when $|s| \gg 1/(2N\_e)$; when $|s| \ll 1/(2N\_e)$, sampling noise governs fixation (Kimura 1968).

In small or insular institutions, bureaucratic drift mirrors genetic drift. Procedural quirks, jargon and
habits become permanent fixtures, and not because they improve operational performance. Random turnover and the
retirement of key personnel do the work. Confusing drift with adaptive wisdom leads institutions to venerate accidental habits as though they were proven best practices.

## 4. W. Ross Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety

W. Ross Ashby (1956) formulated the cybernetic limits of adaptive regulation in *An Introduction to
Cybernetics*, and established the **Law of Requisite Variety**:

> *"Only variety can absorb variety."*

Let $D$ be the set of external disturbances impinging on a system, $R$ be the set of regulatory responses available to the controller, and $O$ be the resulting outcomes. Measuring variety as the logarithm of the number of distinct states ($\mathcal{V} = \log\_2 N$), Ashby's law states:

$$\mathcal{V}\_O \ge \mathcal{V}\_D - \mathcal{V}\_R.$$

Constrain the variety of outcomes $\mathcal{V}\_O$ to a small, safe set of survivable states and the variety
of the regulator $\mathcal{V}\_R$ has to match or exceed the variety of disturbances $\mathcal{V}\_D$:

$$\mathcal{V}\_R \ge \mathcal{V}\_D - \mathcal{V}\_O^{\mathrm{tolerable}}.$$

A complex, volatile environment cannot be regulated by a rigid, low-variety rulebook. Standardize aggressively
(eliminate frontline discretion, strip spare capacity, enforce uniform checklists) and management crushes the
internal regulator variety $\mathcal{V}\_R$.

Human executive attention is bounded by a sensory-motor and behavioral throughput ceiling of approximately
$10\text{ bits/s}$ (Zheng & Meister 2025). A panel of executives is not a wide pipe, however it is staffed.

Route high-variety disturbances through a bottleneck controller whose bit rate is capped below
$\mathcal{V}\_D$ and the unabsorbed variety spills directly into operational failure.

## 5. Artificial Selection as the Biological Root of Goodhart's Law

A dairy breeder selects cows over ten generations on peak single-lactation milk volume alone, and finds the
herd lame, ketotic and short-lived. A funding agency selects laboratories on publication count and significant
p-values, and breeds for low power and irreproducible findings (Smaldino & McElreath 2016). For millennia, agricultural breeders have practised artificial selection on measurable scalar traits: milk
output, seed size, rapid growth. Jay Lush (1937) gave the practice its algebra, the **breeder's equation**:

$$R = h^2 S,$$

where $S = \bar{z}\_{\mathrm{selected}} - \bar{z}\_{\mathrm{population}}$ is the selection differential applied to the parent cohort in the physical units of the trait $z$ (dividing by $\sigma\_P = \sqrt{V\_P}$ gives the dimensionless selection intensity $i = S / \sigma\_P$, so $R = i\mkern2mu h^2 \sigma\_P$), $h^2 = V\_A / V\_P \in [0, 1]$ is narrow-sense heritability (the ratio of additive heritable variance $V\_A$ to total phenotypic variance $V\_P$, with $R \to 0$ as $h^2 \to 0$ and $R \to S$ as $h^2 \to 1$), and $R = \bar{z}\_{\mathrm{offspring}} - \bar{z}\_{\mathrm{population}}$ is the realized single-generation response. Intense single-trait selection breeds for the target scalar and degrades the rest of the organism. Poultry bred
for breast muscle develops skeletal collapse and cardiac failure. Wheat bred for harvest yield loses drought
tolerance and fungal resistance.

Artificial selection is the direct biological ancestor of **Goodhart's Law** and **Campbell's Law** (Goodhart 1975; Campbell 1979; Manheim & Garrabrant 2018):

> *"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."*

Reward staff on a single proxy metric $M$ (test scores, crime closure rates, patient turnaround times) and the
institution becomes an aggressive artificial breeder. The variants of that failure are well catalogued:
regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial (Manheim & Garrabrant 2018).

Selecting on an indicator $M$ improves the true substantive construct $Q$ only under tight causal coupling,
where the marginal effect of the proxy on the construct is close to one and where the cost of gaming the proxy
exceeds the reward it pays.

Where the coupling is loose, the forecast an institution calibrates on a retrospective record is dominated by
genuine discriminative resolution, not by calibration error. A well-calibrated score can still be a proxy that
has detached from its construct. Unguarded proxy pressure then triggers the pillaging of the reserve that would
have absorbed the shock, and the third collapse takes the trajectory.

### Selection consumes what it feeds on

Selection buys response in proportion to heritability and to how hard you select. The relation is
$R = h^2 S$: narrow-sense heritability times the selection differential. That is the breeder's equation, older
than any institution that now quotes it (Lush 1937).

The gain decays, because selection consumes the variation it feeds on. Heritability falls as the favoured
alleles approach fixation, so a response projected from one generation overstates the cumulative gain of a
multi-generation programme by a wide margin, and the error grows geometrically with the horizon. An institution
selecting hard on a metric runs the same arithmetic on a population it cannot re-breed from stock.

The variety it needs is an accounting in bits. The logarithm of the disturbance state count is the regulator
variety required; the logarithm of the regulator's own state count is what it supplies; the deficit between the
two is the shortfall Ashby attributed to any regulator that must absorb variety (Ashby 1956).

### When there is no heritable variation left

The thesis fails at zero heritability, where selection has nothing to act on. Restore the variation before
selecting, and report the variance as the binding constraint, not the incentive.

The same exhaustion appears whenever selection runs on a proxy only causally coupled to the goal. It buys one
generation of real gain and then spends the variation that produced it.

### Selection among institutional forms

The same filter sorts a population of institutions, and it reads cost, not intention. Let a share $x \in (0, 1)$ of units
carry a vital reserve at a continuous cost $c > 0$ per period, and the rest carry none. Let shocks arrive as a
Poisson process of mean interval $T > 0$ (hazard rate $1/T$), and let a unit without a reserve be absorbed at the first shock it
meets. In the calm the replicator equation is

$$\dot{x} = x(1-x)(\pi\_{R} - \pi\_{T}) = -c\mkern3mux(1-x),$$

with $\pi\_{R} = -c$ and $\pi\_{T} = 0$, or equivalently in log-odds form

$$\frac{d}{dt}\ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right) = \frac{\dot{x}}{x(1-x)} = \pi\_{R} - \pi\_{T} = -\gamma\_{\mathrm{select}}, \qquad \gamma\_{\mathrm{select}} := c > 0.$$

The reserve share therefore falls monotonically as $x(t) = x\_0 e^{-ct}/(1 - x\_0 + x\_0 e^{-ct})$, and the deterministic time in the calm to fall from $x\_0$ to $x\_1 \in (0, x\_0)$ is

$$t^{\ast} = \frac{1}{\gamma\_{\mathrm{select}}}\ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{x\_0(1-x\_1)}{x\_1(1-x\_0)}\right) = \frac{1}{c}\ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{x\_0(1-x\_1)}{x\_1(1-x\_0)}\right) > 0.$$

**Proposition (competitive extinction, exponential case).** Let the first shock time satisfy $\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}} \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1/T)$ with mean interval $T > 0$, and let a unit that carries no reserve be absorbed at the first shock it meets. For any $0 < x\_1 < x\_0 < 1$, the probability that the reserve share reaches $x\_1$ before the first shock is

$$\mathbb{P}(\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}} > t^{\ast}) = \exp\mkern-3mu\left(-\frac{t^{\ast}}{T}\right) = \left[\frac{x\_1(1-x\_0)}{x\_0(1-x\_1)}\right]^{1/(cT)},$$

which increases strictly in $cT$ for fixed $x\_0 > x\_1$ and tends to $1$ as $cT \to \infty$. The proof is one line: the decay time $t^{\ast}$ is deterministic in the calm, so reaching $x\_1$ before the first shock is the event $\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}} > t^{\ast}$, whose probability under $\mathrm{Exp}(1/T)$ is the survival function $\exp(-t^{\ast}/T)$.

The competitor does not need to survive the shock in order to win the calm; it wins if the divestment completes first. Because $\mathbb{E}[\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}}] = T$, the expected log-odds at the arrival of the first shock is

$$\mathbb{E}\mkern-3mu\left[\ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{x(\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}})}{1-x(\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}})}\right)\right] = \ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{x\_0}{1-x\_0}\right) - \gamma\_{\mathrm{select}} T.$$

The exact condition $\gamma\_{\mathrm{select}} > 1/T$ (equivalently $\gamma\_{\mathrm{select}} T > 1$) is therefore the threshold under which the log-odds $\ln\bigl(x/(1-x)\bigr)$ decay by more than $1$ nat in expectation before the first shock arrives:

$$\mathbb{E}\mkern-3mu\left[\ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{x(\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}})}{1-x(\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}})}\right)\right] < \ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{x\_0}{1-x\_0}\right) - 1.$$

That inequality is the multipolar trap in its exact form. Every unit prefers the profile in which all hold the
reserve to the profile in which none does, and every unit prefers to be the one that divests while the others
hold. The all-hold profile is therefore not stable under the replicator equation, and the equation reads the
current cost without distinguishing a unit that intends to restore the reserve after the next quarter from one
that does not.

An arbitrary inter-shock law for $\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}}$ with survival function $S(t) = \mathbb{P}(\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}} > t)$ costs no further assumption. The same argument gives

$$\mathbb{P}(\tau\_{\mathrm{shock}} > t^{\ast}) = S(t^{\ast}),$$

of which the exponential case is the specialisation $S(t) = e^{-t/T}$.

The repeated case is where the proof stops, and it is recorded explicitly as a conjecture rather than used. If units
rebuild reserves at a rate $r > 0$ between shocks, the reserve share is no longer monotone and the calm-period inequality $\gamma\_{\mathrm{select}} > 1/T$ is necessary for divestment between shocks, not sufficient for asymptotic absorption. A population can divest in the calm, rebuild after each shock, and
settle at a stationary share that depends jointly on the shock law and on $r$. **Conjecture (repeated replenishment regime).** Under shocks of mean interval $T$, replenishment at rate $r > 0$, and a floor that sets the divesting unit's tail cost to $q \ge c$, the stationary reserve share is bounded below by a positive function of $rT$ and $q/c$. No argument in this book depends on the conjecture.

A rule changes the sign only by entering $\pi\_{T}$ with a term the divesting unit cannot remove. Let the unit
that divests bear the tail cost, $\pi\_{T} = -q$ with $q \ge c$, so that $\pi\_{R} - \pi\_{T} = q - c \ge 0$; the log-odds derivative $\frac{d}{dt}\ln\bigl(x/(1-x)\bigr) = q - c \ge 0$ is then non-negative and the
reserve share non-decreasing. Whether $q \ge c$ holds is arithmetic. Whether the rule can be revoked by the
unit it binds is a question about the level at which the rule is chosen.

Ostrom (1990) separates the operational, collective-choice and constitutional levels at which rules are made,
and only the last is a constraint on the game rather than a move in it. A monitoring or sanctioning rule that
the competing unit can repeal is repealed, by the same differential, before it collects the evidence it was
written to collect.

The non-revocable constitutional floor is therefore not a policy recommendation attached to the model. It is purely the arithmetic name for the tail-internalising term $\pi\_{T} = -q$ ($q \ge c$) without which the replicator equation's own sign selects the divestment.
