## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Say what a predictive model actually computes, and what it cannot compute, without invoking
   any mathematics.
2. Recognise the four recurring shapes of algorithmic deception: fluent wrongness, metric
   capture, authority laundering, and confident extrapolation.
3. Apply a three-question filter to any claim about a model, and reject the claim on evidence
   rather than on taste.

## 1. What a model does

Strip away the marketing vocabulary and any predictive model performs one operation: it stores
statistical regularities observed in historical pairs of inputs and outputs, and it produces, for a
novel input, the output most consistent with those regularities under the lossy compression it was
constrained to use. Every statistical or machine-learned model is therefore an archived *summary of
the past*, and every compression is lossy in a specific direction chosen by an institutional author.

Three fundamental limitations follow immediately:
- A model cannot report a regularity absent from its training record, however blatant and urgent that
  regularity is to the frontline operators inhabiting the situation.
- A model cannot distinguish *the world is structured like this* from *the archive was collected like this*;
  the ontological distinction between territory and map is entirely unrepresentable within its weights.
- A model possesses no internal sensory mechanism for announcing *my compression scheme fails in this region*;
  its mathematical compression applies everywhere by construction, including where its foundational
  assumptions are violated.

Above all, a statistical model cannot supply causal authority. A pattern of association can be fitted
to near-zero training error while the real-world intervention a decision-maker is about to execute moves
the physical system in the opposite direction. The jump from empirical correlation to operational action
is a separate logical claim requiring distinct identifying assumptions; it is never licensed by predictive
accuracy alone.

## 2. Premise-rigging and the four shapes of deception

In *Administrative Behavior*, Herbert Simon observed that organizational power operates most effectively
not through explicit commands, but through the **control of decision premises**. When an automated system
is installed, it does not merely offer advice; it pre-determines the categories of thought, the allowable
variables, and the objective loss function. The decision is rigged at the level of the premise.

This premise-rigging frequently commits what A. W. Kimball identified as a **Type III error**: providing
an exquisitely precise answer to the wrong question. An algorithmic system computes the metric it was given
(e.g., maximizing billing throughput or minimizing bed turn-around latency) with mathematical optimality,
while the genuine operational objective (e.g., patient clinical survival or diagnostic accuracy) is eclipsed.
Furthermore, when algorithmic models assign absolute dogmatic priors ($\mathbb{P}(H) = 0$ or $1$) to
unprecedented events, they violate **Cromwell's rule** (formalised by Dennis Lindley): no finite empirical
evidence can ever revise a posterior probability away from zero or one, creating an impervious cognitive trap.

None of the resulting deceptions requires a malicious engineer. Each arises naturally from the interaction
of pattern compression with institutional incentives:

**Fluent wrongness.** Coherence and truth are generated by distinct mechanisms, yet readers routinely conflate
them. The algorithmic machinery that produces syntactic fluency and persuasive explanations operates whether
the underlying factual premises are sound or completely fabricated. Because the model lacks any internal marker
separating verified knowledge from confabulation, fluency provides zero evidential guarantee of truth.

**Metric capture.** Any published metric tied to organizational evaluation becomes an operational target.
Optimizing the proxy directly improves the measurement while degrading the underlying reality it was meant
to reflect. As formalised in [Chapter 48](48-reactive-worlds-performativity-goodhart.md), this collapse takes
four distinct shapes: regression to the mean, extreme-value concentration, causal disconnection, and adversarial
gaming.

**Authority laundering.** A proposition gains unwarranted credibility as it travels upward through a hierarchy.
A raw correlation discovered by an analyst, incorporated into a dashboard, cited in an executive briefing, and
codified into policy is gradually stripped of its caveats, its sample size limits, and its error margins.
James March and Herbert Simon termed this pathology **uncertainty absorption** `[Literature]`: inferences are
communicated while the underlying evidence that could qualify or contest them is discarded. Standard bureaucratic
prose facilitates this laundering because its grammar allows bare declarative claims with no syntactical slot
for provenance. By contrast, indigenous languages with obligatory **morphological evidentiality** (such as
Tuyuca and Tariana, documented by Alexandra Aikhenvald `[Literature]`) mandate that every declarative verb carry
a morpheme specifying whether the fact was directly witnessed, deduced from physical traces, inferred from
premises, or learned by hearsay; omitting the evidential marker produces an ungrammatical utterance. Furthermore,
in the speech-act theory of J. L. Austin and John Searle, authority laundering conceals a silent reversal in the
**direction of fit**: an assertive statement (`grammar.rs::AgentActionKind`, *word-to-world*) is laundered into
a performative directive (*world-to-word*) that compels human subjects to conform to the algorithm's expectations.

**Confident extrapolation.** When presented with inputs residing outside the support of its historical data,
a model does not halt or express humility. It computes an output using the nearest available mathematical
manifold, radiating the same mathematical confidence as when interpolating dense observations. This is the most
dangerous deception because crises and phase transitions reside precisely in the unsupported tails of the space.

## 3. A three-question filter

Any decision-maker confronted with an algorithmic recommendation should apply a simple, non-negotiable
three-question filter before permitting the claim to influence action:

1. *What specific empirical observation would refute this model's claim?* If the model's architects cannot name
   a concrete outcome that would prove the model wrong, the system is performing unfalsifiable rhetoric rather
   than empirical science.
2. *Who bears the downside if this recommendation fails, and who captures the upside if it succeeds?* When the
   creators and deployers of a model bear zero economic or bodily consequence for false negatives or false
   positives, the model's output is contaminated by moral hazard. The asymmetry of exposure is itself primary
   information.
3. *What physical realities does the archive fail to record, and does this model's validity depend upon them?*
   If the algorithm's recommendations presuppose the stability of factors that leave no digital footprint in the
   database, its confidence is an artifact of institutional blindness.

A recommendation that survives all three questions is not guaranteed to be true, but it is the only kind
legitimate enough to enter serious deliberation.

## 4. Hype as an institutional product

The recurring boom-and-bust cycle of artificial intelligence and predictive modeling — waves of extravagant
claims followed by bitter "AI winters" — is not an accident of public misunderstanding. It is an equilibrium
outcome produced by the political economy of institutional funding. Sponsoring agencies and capital markets
reward bold demonstrations of capability; demonstrations attract capital; but no actor in the speculative loop
is tasked with auditing what has *not* been achieved, nor what structural vulnerabilities have been accumulated.
The historical funding collapses after the 1957 perceptron hype, the 1966 ALPAC report on machine translation,
and the 1973 British Lighthill Report all demonstrate that ungrounded optimism inevitably exhausts institutional
patience.

The enduring lesson for engineering is unambiguous. An intellectual discipline does not avoid collapse through
louder rhetoric; it survives by constructing auditable artifacts whose failure conditions are published in
advance, whose error bars are mathematically bounded, and whose institutional responsibilities are sealed
before the trial begins.

## 5. The Perceptron saga: what a local device structurally cannot see

The story that matters for this chapter is not the rise and fall of a technology but the exact shape of the
mathematical objection that interrupted it. The pattern recurs: a device is demonstrated, the demonstration is
generalised into a claim about intelligence, the claim is inflated by institutional funding, and then a proof
arrives that is narrower than the rebuttal it licenses.

Dartmouth, 1956, is the conventional opening: McCarthy, Minsky, Shannon and Rochester proposed a summer project on
"artificial intelligence" on the premise that every feature of learning could in principle be described precisely
enough for a machine to simulate it `[Literature]`. The institutional accelerant arrived with the Sputnik shock of
1957 and the creation of ARPA in 1958, which funded the field on a demonstration basis rather than on a
replication basis — the same procurement structure that, decades later, would produce the metric-capture
equilibrium of §4.

Rosenblatt's perceptron (1957–1958) was a real device: a thresholded linear combination of an input vector,
trained by a convergent error-correction rule. Its limitation is a fact of geometry, not of engineering. A
single-layer threshold element realises an affine half-space, so it separates exactly those dichotomies whose
positive and negative classes are affinely separable. Exclusive disjunction is the smallest counterexample,
because it is not separable by any hyperplane in the input space; what the device lacks is not power but a
hidden layer, i.e. a change of basis in which the dichotomy becomes separable.

Minsky and Papert's *Perceptrons* (1969) made the objection much stronger than the XOR folklore suggests, and
this is the part the popular memory drops. They proved two families of results about **diameter-limited**
perceptrons — devices whose decision depends only on a bounded neighbourhood of the input, which is exactly the
regime of "local feature detectors" `[Literature]`:

1. **Parity (their Theorem 3.1.1).** The predicate $\psi_{\mathrm{PARITY}}(x) = 1$ iff an odd number of the $n$
   input bits are on cannot be computed by any diameter-limited perceptron whose diameter is smaller than $n$.
   Any local window sees an even or odd count inside the window and nothing about the rest, so the global parity
   of the whole input is not a function of any bounded collection of local views.
2. **Connectedness (their Theorem 0.8).** The predicate $\psi_{\mathrm{CONNECTED}}$, true when the set of "on"
   cells of a retina forms one connected component, cannot be computed by any perceptron whose order — the number
   of input cells a single coefficient may read — is bounded independently of the image size. Deciding whether
   two marked cells lie in the same component is a global reachability question, and reachability has no
   constant-size certificate.

The consequence is the one that matters here and that reaches far beyond 1969: **locality of the evidence is not
neutral with respect to the question being asked**. A device that reads only neighbouring cells can be arbitrarily
accurate on local statistics and still be structurally blind to a global predicate. This is the same obstruction
that reappears in [Chapter 53](53-layer-macro-coupled-network-contagion-order.md) for network topology: the
vulnerability of a coupled system lives in paths, not in nodes, and no aggregation of node-local measurements
produces a betweenness ordering. It is also, in a different vocabulary, the reason the syntactic certificate of
[Chapter 56](56-layer-formal-computable-type-order.md) must inspect the *shape* of the command rather than wait
for the semantics of its execution: the property to be verified is not a function of any bounded observation
window of the running system.

The surrounding institutional record is equally instructive, because it shows how a narrow theorem becomes a
broad verdict. Hubert Dreyfus's *What Computers Can't Do* (1972) argued from phenomenology rather than from
geometry that formal symbol manipulation cannot capture embodied know-how `[Literature]`; the British Science
Research Council's Lighthill Report (1973) concluded that the field had failed to deliver on its combinatorial
promises; and the Mansfield Amendment of 1973 required American defence funding to be justified by direct
military relevance, ending the blank cheque. None of these three interventions proved what the subsequent
"AI winter" narrative claimed. What actually happened is the more interesting and more stochological fact: a
funding equilibrium collapsed because no actor was tasked with auditing the distance between the demonstration
and the claim.

The technical recovery is equally precise about what had to be added. Hopfield networks (1982) reframed
associative memory as the dynamics of an energy function, so that retrieval became descent rather than lookup.
Backpropagation, popularised in 1986, supplied the gradient that makes a hidden layer trainable — precisely the
change of basis whose absence made XOR unlearnable. And Minsky's *Society of Mind* (1988) drew the moral that
the 1969 book had already implied: general competence is not the property of one homogeneous element but of a
structured organisation of specialised, mutually constraining agents. That thesis is the ancestor of the
architecture this treatise uses, and it is why the aggregation of judgment in
[Chapter 49](49-moral-luck-apt-agency-institutions.md) is treated as a constitutional problem — how agents are
composed — rather than as an averaging problem.

## 6. The imitation game and the ladder from AGI to ASI

Turing's 1950 paper replaced an unanswerable question with an operational one: instead of asking whether a
machine thinks, ask whether an interrogator, deprived of the physical facts, can distinguish its answers from a
human's `[Literature]`. That substitution is a genuine epistemic advance and a genuine trap, and the framework
needs both halves of the diagnosis.

The advance is that it converts a metaphysical dispute into a measurable protocol with a pre-registered
question, a stated population of interrogators and a defined loss. The trap is that the protocol measures
**indistinguishability of the transcript**, and a transcript is exactly the artifact that this book's fourth
deception — fluent wrongness, §2 — is designed to corrupt. A system optimised against an interrogator's
discrimination learns the distribution of interrogator expectations, not the structure of the world; and the
interrogator, being a bounded observer, has a finite budget of questions. The imitation game is therefore a
test of a *proxy*, and like every proxy it is exposed to the four variants of
[Chapter 48](48-reactive-worlds-performativity-goodhart.md).

The contemporary ladder inherits the same structure. The DeepMind levels taxonomy orders systems from emerging
to competent, expert, virtuoso and finally artificial general intelligence, with superintelligence defined as
performance beyond the most capable humans across the relevant range `[Literature]`. As a descriptive scale it
is useful; as a target it repeats the error of §4 unless each rung is read through the three axes of this
framework. A system can be expert on the archive tape and blind on the decision tape (no epistemic margin), can
be virtuoso at full utilisation and one shock away from absorption (no vital margin), and can be superhuman in
capability while being captured by the institution that funds it (no governance). The ladder measures the first
tape; the treatise exists because the other two are not implied by it.
