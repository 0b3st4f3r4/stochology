## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Say what a predictive model actually computes, and why historical compression cannot replace judgment.
2. Recognise the four recurring shapes of algorithmic deception: fluent wrongness, metric
   capture, authority laundering, and confident extrapolation.
3. Apply a three-question filter to any claim about a model, and reject the claim on evidence
   rather than on taste.

## 1. What a model does

Picture a regional credit officer reviewing an automated fraud-and-default risk score for a household requesting emergency working capital. The screen shows one crisp probability distilled from years of records. Denying credit destroys the household's seasonal harvest and drops them from future ledgers, an irreversible harm the bank's training archive never captures. Approval risks only a bounded loan write-off. Before trusting the number's "accuracy," the officer must ask what real facts were stripped out to produce it.

Behind all marketing jargon, any statistical or simulation-trained model performs one operation. It compresses regularities from a fixed historical archive or closed ruleset under a chosen loss function. Given a new input, it outputs the pattern most consistent with that compression. Whether trained on past rows or self-play, every compression discards information along lines chosen by an institutional author.

Three structural limitations follow at once:

- A model cannot report a pattern missing from its training record, however obvious or urgent that pattern is to frontline staff on the ground.
- A model cannot separate *how the world works* from *how the archive was filtered*. Inside its weights, territory and map look identical.
- A model cannot flag where its own compression breaks down. Its equations run everywhere by design, even when core assumptions fail.

Above all, predictive correlation supplies zero causal authority. A model can achieve near-zero training error on past associations while the action a manager takes pushes the physical system the opposite way. Moving from association to intervention requires separate causal assumptions that predictive accuracy never provides.

When an optimizer exploits a fitted model beyond its training anchor inside a nonlinear world, greedy optimization imagines returns the world will not pay. The surrogate extrapolates smoothly while real systems bend toward absorbing ruin. Adding a discrepancy penalty curbs extreme jumps inside known support, yet cannot eliminate ruin whenever unbounded shocks or out-of-sample model errors persist over an open horizon, just as in the classical compound-Poisson collective risk model (Cramér 1930; Lundberg 1903).

## 2. Premise-rigging and the four shapes of deception

In *Administrative Behavior*, Herbert Simon showed that institutional power works less through open commands than through the **control of decision premises** (Simon 1997). An automated tool does more than offer advice. It fixes the categories of thought, the allowed variables, and the scoring loss function before a single human reads the screen. Judgment is captured at the premise stage.

A theory that treats an existing order as natural explains only the inside of the machine. Critical inquiry treats how that frame reproduces itself as the primary problem (Horkheimer 2002 [1937]). Logical empiricism stripped away claims untethered from protocol-sentence verification and explicit truth conditions (Carnap 1932), exposing empty slogans. Yet even a rigged statistical frame passes internal checks whenever its own mutilated ledger supplies matching rows. Breaking that circularity requires auditing the institution producing the ledger.

Assigning dogmatic probabilities ($\mathbb{P}(H) = 0$ or $1$) breaks **Cromwell's rule** (Lindley 1985): no finite evidence shifts a posterior off zero or one, locking the frame shut. Even the explicit omission indices of [Chapter 32](32-the-question-before-the-data.md) and [Chapter 43](43-inquiry-openness-multiverse.md) face **pre-seal circularity**: cryptographic hashes prove when rows were locked, never what stayed out. Auditing rigged premises demands independent outside registers and frontline reports rather than internal consistency checks.

None of these institutional deceptions requires a dishonest engineer. Each emerges naturally when pattern compression meets organizational incentives:

**Fluent wrongness.** Coherence and truth arise from separate mechanisms. Language and pattern generators stitch together fluent, persuasive sequences regardless of whether underlying claims hold. As Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and Margaret Mitchell (published under the pseudonym Shmargaret Shmitchell) argued in their **stochastic parrot** analysis, a model trained purely on form produces plausible text without communicative intent, real-world grounding, or accountable reference (Bender et al. 2021). Lacking any internal test separating verified fact from smooth confabulation, fluency offers no proof of truth.

**Metric capture.** Any published number tied to pay, promotion, or funding becomes an operational target. Measured compliance rises while real outcomes decay. As later chapters formalize ([Chapter 40](40-institution-incentive-metric.md) and [Chapter 48](48-reactive-worlds-performativity-goodhart.md)), this capture extends Goodhart's (1975) observation along four mechanisms formalized by Manheim and Garrabrant (2018): *regressional* selection on noisy proxy error ($M = G + \varepsilon$), *extremal* breakdown outside past support, *causal* manipulation of downstream symptoms, and *adversarial* gaming. In practice, these four often strike together, as when aggressive optimization pushes a surrogate off support while exploiting its blind spots.

**Authority laundering.** A fragile claim gains false certainty as it climbs an organizational hierarchy. A shaky sample correlation becomes a dashboard metric, then an executive slide, then policy, shedding caveats, sample limits, and error bands along the way. James March and Herbert Simon named this process **uncertainty absorption** (March & Simon 1958): inferences travel upward while the qualifying evidence gets discarded.

Everyday administrative English hides this laundering because bare declarative sentences carry no grammatical slot for source provenance. By contrast, indigenous languages with obligatory **morphological evidentiality** (such as Tuyuca and Tariana, documented by Alexandra Aikhenvald; Aikhenvald 2004) require every verb to mark whether a claim was witnessed, deduced, inferred, or reported, preventing the speaker from erasing provenance. Building on speech-act theory (Austin 1962; Searle 1969), John Searle's taxonomy exposes a silent **direction-of-fit** reversal (Searle 1975): an assertive claim meant to describe the world (*word-to-world*) quietly turns into a directive forcing people to conform to the algorithm (*world-to-word*).

**Confident extrapolation.** Outside its historical training region, a model neither pauses nor signals ignorance. It projects a value from the nearest learned pattern with the same apparent confidence it shows during interpolation. Crises and regime shifts inhabit those unrecorded tails, making blind extrapolation the deadliest of the four traps.

## 3. A three-question filter

Before a predictive model guides live action, its deployment protocol must pass three non-negotiable framing questions, formalized as the framing lock in [Chapter 29](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md). Sealing those answers before rollout protects shift workers from compliance overload while blocking institutions from turning frontline staff into scapegoats:

1. *What specific empirical observation would refute this model's claim?* If designers cannot specify an observable outcome that proves the model wrong, the system produces unfalsifiable rhetoric, not empirical guidance.
2. *Who bears the downside if this recommendation fails, and who captures the upside if it succeeds?* Whenever builders carry zero physical or financial liability for error, moral hazard contaminates the tool. Exposure asymmetry is essential diagnostic evidence.
3. *What physical realities does the archive fail to record, and does this model's validity depend upon them?* If recommendations assume unmeasured factors remain steady, apparent accuracy is merely an artifact of blind data collection.

Because static rulebooks cannot anticipate every physiological or operational anomaly (Canguilhem 1943/1966), Questions 1 and 3 require pairing live deployment with an undiscounted frontline override. Operators must be able to halt an automated prompt whenever unrecorded threats emerge, regardless of their organizational rank.

Question 2 connects directly to the cost–loss balance introduced in [Chapter 01](01-deciding-without-knowing.md) and formalized in [Chapter 32](32-the-question-before-the-data.md) and [Chapter 36](36-decision-loss-threshold.md), drawing on Pauker & Kassirer's (1975) clinical regret ratio. Suppose an affected resident suffers catastrophic harm while the institution faces only a modest fine ($L\_{\mathrm{affected}} \gg L\_{\mathrm{inst}}$). The exposed resident triggers precaution at a far lower hazard probability than the institution does, so the exact same model forecast produces opposite decisions across that loss divide.

### Name the cost pair before the accusation

Changing the loss ratio moves the action cutoff without touching the statistical forecast. Attacking a model's calibration when parties actually disagree over error costs mistakes an institutional value conflict for a technical bug.

### When the cost pair is not agreed

Refusing to declare affected people's losses never stays neutral. It quietly exports uncompensated risk onto external reception perimeters, turning concealed stakes into governance capture and forcing displaced harm into public contestation (formalized across [Chapter 29](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md) and [Chapter 40](40-institution-incentive-metric.md)).

## 4. Hype as an institutional product

The recurring boom-and-bust cycles of predictive technology (sweeping promises followed by sudden "AI winters") do not stem from public confusion. They arise as equilibrium outcomes in the political economy of funding. Capital markets and funding agencies reward flashy capability demonstrations; demonstrations pull in budgets; and nobody inside the promotional loop gets paid to audit unverified claims or hidden fragility.

The collapses following early perceptron enthusiasm (Rosenblatt's 1957 report and Rosenblatt 1958), the 1966 ALPAC machine-translation review (ALPAC 1966), and the 1973 British Lighthill Report (Lighthill 1973) all followed this same pattern. Confidence outran verification until institutional patience broke.

Hospital audits of the proprietary Epic Sepsis Model show how uncalibrated marketing collides with clinical reality (Wong et al. 2021; quantified in [Chapter 36](36-decision-loss-threshold.md)). Despite strong marketed test scores, low real-world sepsis prevalence crushed positive predictive value, flooding wards with false alarms through base-rate neglect. What guards against deploying uncalibrated hype is score decomposition rather than one headline metric. As Dimitriadis, Gneiting, and Jordan (2021) prove, the Brier score splits cleanly into reliability (miscalibration), resolution, and uncertainty under isotonic recalibration. A model whose miscalibration dwarfs its resolution fails in ways no discrimination score exposes.

The remedy remains constant: publish failure conditions in advance, bound error intervals honestly, and bind institutional accountability before deployment begins.

## 5. Perceptrons, and what a local device structurally cannot see

What matters in the early history of neural computing is the mathematical shape of the objection that stopped it. A narrow geometric proof exposed what a single-layer local machine cannot perceive, even when political reaction overstated what that limit proved.

Early computation split between two competing visions: Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts's logical calculus of threshold neurons (McCulloch & Pitts 1943) alongside Vannevar Bush's associative *Memex* trails (Bush 1945), versus symbolic heuristic search. At the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, McCarthy, Minsky, Shannon, and Rochester argued that learning could be broken down precisely enough for machines to simulate (McCarthy et al. 1955). Allen Newell and Herbert Simon demonstrated the *Logic Theorist* (Newell & Simon 1956), and Oliver Selfridge soon introduced the parallel *Pandemonium* architecture (Selfridge 1959). After the 1957 Sputnik launch and ARPA's creation in 1958, defense budgets rewarded bold prototypes rather than rigorous replication, repeating the hype dynamics of §4.

Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron simulated a probabilistic brain model on the IBM 704 (Rosenblatt 1958), combining input weights into a linear threshold rule whose convergence theorem holds for linearly separable classes (Minsky & Papert 1969, Theorem 11.1). Its constraint is purely geometric. A single-layer threshold element draws a flat hyperplane across input space, separating only categories divided by a straight cut. Exclusive-OR (XOR) is the smallest counterexample because no straight line splits its true and false corners; overcoming it requires a hidden layer that changes the input representation.

In *Perceptrons* (1969), Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert strengthened that objection far beyond XOR. Suppose a machine tries to judge a global image by reading small local patches. Minsky and Papert separated two locality limits on those patches: **diameter-limited** perceptrons (each feature detector $\varphi$ inspects only a bounded spatial patch) and **order-limited** perceptrons (each detector reads at most a bounded number of input bits, support size $|S(\varphi)|$). Under both limits, they proved two impossibility results (Minsky & Papert 1969):

1. **Parity (Theorem 3.1.1, order-limited).** Consider a planar grid $R$ holding $n = |R|$ binary pixels (distinct from [Chapter 27](27-what-judging-is.md)'s physical reserve $R\_t$ and later chapters' missingness indicator $R$). Let $\psi\_{\mathrm{PARITY}}(x) = 1$ precisely when an odd number of pixels are switched on. Minsky and Papert proved this predicate has **order $n$**: no linear-threshold machine reading fewer than all $n$ pixels per detector can compute parity, even if detectors span the entire grid width (on an $m \times m$ square grid of $n = m^2$ cells, geometric diameter grows as $O(\sqrt{n}) \ll n$). Any partial detector reading fewer than $n$ pixels balances out symmetrically across even and odd counts, whereas stacked nonlinear layers of small XOR gates solve parity easily.
2. **Connectedness (Theorem 0.8 and Chapter 5, diameter- and order-limited).** Let $\psi\_{\mathrm{CONNECTED}}$ mark whether all active pixels form a single unbroken shape. No **diameter-limited** perceptron can compute connectedness (Theorem 0.8), nor can any **order-limited** perceptron whose patch size stays fixed as the grid grows (Theorem 5.1 and §5.7 of Minsky & Papert 1969, requiring order at least $C|R|^{1/2}$ for a constant $C > 0$). Global path reachability resists local linear certificates, even though simple Euler counts ignoring nested holes remain local at order at most $4$ (Theorem 5.8.1; Minsky & Papert 1969).

The takeaway extends far beyond early neural nets: **evidence locality is never neutral toward the question asked**. A device reading local neighborhoods can match local statistics flawlessly while staying structurally blind to global connections. The exact same geometry limits coupled infrastructure networks (where node-level checks miss system-wide cascades) and syntactic command checkers, which must inspect global program structure rather than tiny local execution windows. Yet the theorem protects careful models too: when a task genuinely separates along linear local features, penalizing a simple model for failing on a badly framed non-separable target commits an auditor's premise error.

Contemporary policy interventions turned narrow limits into sweeping verdicts. Hubert Dreyfus argued phenomenologically that rule-based symbols miss embodied human skill (Dreyfus 1972). Britain's Lighthill Report concluded AI failed on combinatorial explosions (Lighthill 1973), while the 1969 Mansfield Amendment restricted defense research grants to explicit military missions (Public Law 91-121, 1969). None of those critiques proved intelligence impossible; rather, funding collapsed because nobody audited the gap between demos and claims.

Technical progress confirmed exactly what was missing. John Hopfield (1982) recast associative memory as descent on an energy landscape. Backpropagation, popularized in 1986, supplied the chain-rule gradients needed to train hidden layers, creating the change of basis XOR required (Rumelhart et al. 1986). In *Society of Mind*, Marvin Minsky drew the deeper architectural lesson: competence emerges not from one giant uniform calculator, but from structured collaboration among specialized, mutually checking agents (Minsky 1988). That principle shapes Stochology's treatment of institutional accountability as an organizational constitution ([Chapter 40](40-institution-incentive-metric.md)) rather than a mere average of scores.

## 6. The imitation game and the ladder from AGI to ASI

In 1950, Alan Turing replaced an unanswerable philosophical riddle (*can machines think?*) with an operational game: can a human interrogator, restricted to a text transcript, distinguish a machine's replies from a person's (Turing 1950)? That substitution solved one problem while opening a dangerous trap.

On the positive side, it converted vague metaphysics into a finite, testable protocol. The trap is that it scores **transcript indistinguishability**, precisely what fluent wrongness (§2) manufactures cheaply. Turing imagined skeptical, adversarial questioning at test time. Once developers optimize models directly to win evaluator approval, however, the judge turns into a training target. The system learns sycophantic alignment with what interrogators expect rather than fidelity to the world, leaving machine transcripts with zero independent testimonial standing inside an accountability circuit. Bounded interrogators also face strict question budgets, exposing the imitation game to every variant of metric capture (§2).

Modern capability ladders inherit this same distinction. Kevin Kelly portrays the *technium* as an evolving web of cumulative tool lineages (Kelly 2010). Breakthroughs like AlphaGo combined policy and value neural networks with Monte Carlo tree search to master a closed board (Silver et al. 2016). Recent taxonomies, such as the DeepMind matrix crossing *Performance* (*emerging*, *competent*, *expert*, *virtuoso*/*exceptional*, and *superhuman*, defined as `"outperforming 100% of humans"`) against *Generality* (*narrow* vs. *general*, spanning *Levels 1–4* AGI up to *Level 5* superintelligence; Morris et al. 2023), clarify technical scope cleanly.

Used as a descriptive map, that matrix helps; used as a substitute for judgment, it repeats §4's hype fallacy unless audited across all three safeguards. A model can achieve superhuman mastery inside a fixed board or clean archive while remaining blind to unrecorded communities (zero epistemic margin), operating with zero physical slack against shocks (zero vital margin), and serving captured incentives (zero governance). Benchmark leaderboards measure closed-task performance alone; they establish neither world awareness nor institutional safety.

### When closed-board capability suffices — and where it breaks

High performance ranks justify autonomous operation only inside closed, stationary, reversible rulesets with zero uncompensated spillover onto unconsenting external reception perimeters, as on fixed boards like Go (Silver et al. 2016). In open, shifting, irreversible worlds where harm cannot be pre-listed, scaling benchmark performance buys none of the three safeguards, and treating leaderboard rank as judgment repeats the deception.
