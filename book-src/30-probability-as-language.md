## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Write down the axioms of probability and say what each one forbids you to believe.
2. Update a belief with Bayes' rule and read the result as a statement about evidence, not about
   the world.
3. Explain why an *anytime-valid* statement is different from a statement that is valid at one
   fixed sample size.

## 1. The axioms and what they exclude

Imagine a clinical assay or industrial quality-control laboratory measuring a fixed batch of specimens every Friday, testing whether a new filtration membrane reduces contaminant drift. The director does not run the trial once. She checks a standard $95\unicode{x25}$ confidence interval after the first week, after the second, and every week after that, and halts the trial on the first Friday the nominal interval excludes zero. Every weekly calculation used a textbook formula at nominal error level $\alpha = 0.05$. Peeking across repeated looks still inflated the true false-positive rate far past the nominal level (Ville 1939). Nobody lied about the measurements. The failure was in the temporal grammar of probability.

Andrey Kolmogorov's three foundational conditions of measure-theoretic probability (Kolmogorov 1933) are customarily presented
as constructive definitions. In decision science, where Richard Cox proved that any algebra of plausible reasoning consistent with Boolean logic must be isomorphic to probability (Cox 1946), and Leonard Jimmie Savage grounded subjective probability in coherent preference acts (Savage 1954), they are far better understood as strict prohibitions
on a book of beliefs:

- **Non-negativity** ($\mathbb{P}(A) \ge 0$) forbids holding a degree of belief below impossibility.
- **Normalization** ($\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$) forbids holding a set of mutually exclusive and collectively
  exhaustive outcomes whose degrees of belief do not sum to unity, that is, it strictly prohibits omitting
  unforeseen events while asserting complete coverage.
- **Countable additivity** ($\mathbb{P}(\bigcup\_i A\_i) = \sum\_i \mathbb{P}(A\_i)$ for disjoint events) forbids
  assigning more or less credibility to an aggregate claim than the sum of its partitioned possibilities.

The justification for these conditions is operational rather than aesthetic: as demonstrated in the Dutch book
theorem of [Probability as a Measure](15-probability-as-a-measure.md) §2, an agent whose degrees of belief violate
non-negativity, unit normalization, or finite additivity (Ramsey 1926; de Finetti 1937) can be subjected to a
finite system of simultaneous bets that guarantees a sure loss however the world turns out, while countable
additivity adds monotone continuity along infinite sequences of events (Kolmogorov 1933). Coherence is the minimal
requirement under which an epistemic claim can bind future action without self-destruction.

The axioms do not dictate *which* specific numerical probabilities an agent should hold. Two rational observers can satisfy all three exactly and still hold opposed beliefs from differing priors. Probability theory is a syntax for reasoning about evidence. It is not an oracle for dictating truth.

## 2. Measurement scales, invariance, and the dialectical penumbra

Numbers enter probabilistic calculations carrying a physical meaning, and measurement theory says what that meaning survives. In 1946, S. S. Stevens introduced a foundational taxonomy of measurement scales (Stevens 1946; Krantz et al. 1971), defined strictly by their groups of **permissible transformations**:

1. **Nominal scale**: Numbers serve purely as discrete labels. The permissible group is all one-to-one substitutions
   (permutations). Permissible operations are limited to counts, modes, and equivalence relations.
2. **Ordinal scale**: Numbers represent rank order ($A > B > C$), but the numerical distances between ranks have no
   physical meaning. The permissible group is all strictly monotonic increasing transformations ($x \mapsto f(x)$
   with $f'(x) > 0$). Permissible statistics include medians, quantiles, and rank correlations.
3. **Interval scale**: Equal numerical intervals reflect equal quantities of the measurand, but the zero point is
   arbitrary (e.g., Celsius temperature). The permissible group is positive affine transformations
   ($x \mapsto ax + b$ with $a > 0$). Permissible statistics include arithmetic means, variances, and standard
   deviations.
4. **Ratio scale**: Has a natural, non-arbitrary physical zero (e.g., Kelvin temperature, mass, elapsed time).
   The permissible group is similarity transformations ($x \mapsto ax$ with $a > 0$). Permissible operations include
   ratios, geometric means, and coefficients of variation.

Violating scale invariance is the most pervasive source of pseudo-precision in organizational reporting. Averaging
ordinal customer satisfaction scores or clinical triage codes ("severe = 3, moderate = 2, mild = 1") treats an
arbitrary monotonic relabeling as an interval metric, fabricating mathematical units that the underlying phenomenon
does not possess.

And real-world judgment constantly operates within what Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen termed the **dialectical penumbra** (Georgescu-Roegen 1971) (or margin of vagueness): the boundary region where categorical distinctions ("safe vs unsafe", "healthy vs diseased", "employed vs unemployed") are contested and inherently context-dependent.

The figure has a classical ancestor. Heraclitus described the same thing as living and dead, waking and sleeping, young and old (Heraclitus c. 500 BCE, DK B88), which states the unity of opposites as a fact about the world rather than as a licence for vagueness. The penumbra is that unity given a measure and a width. Forcing sharp real-valued probabilities onto concepts located within the penumbra creates a dangerous illusion of certainty. When the underlying scale is ordinal, the only invariant statements are those based on stochastic order, computed by the Mann–Whitney dominance index over two empirical samples $X = (x\_1, \dots, x\_n)$ and $Y = (y\_1, \dots, y\_m)$:

$$\theta\_{\mathrm{MW}}(X, Y) = \frac{1}{nm}\sum\_{i=1}^n \sum\_{j=1}^m \left(\mathbf{1}\_{\lbrace{}x\_i > y\_j\rbrace} + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\_{\lbrace{}x\_i = y\_j\rbrace}\right) = \mathbb{P}(X > Y) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(X = Y) \in [0, 1].$$

Because $\mathbf{1}\_{\lbrace{}f(x\_i) > f(y\_j)\rbrace} = \mathbf{1}\_{\lbrace{}x\_i > y\_j\rbrace}$ for every strictly increasing transformation $f$, the dominance index is invariant across all monotonic relabelings, while the arithmetic mean of a rescaled sample moves with the rescaling. Averaging an ordinal scale fabricates an interval; the dominance index does not. Nor does the penumbra oblige anyone to pretend it has closed — observations that fall between the named categories remain a measured share of any real sample, and their share shifts when the practice or the coding convention shifts.

## 3. Conditioning and belief revision

A conditional probability is not a novel kind of probability; it is the original probability evaluated over a
restricted sub-universe: given that evidence $E$ occurred, we agree to restrict our inquiry to the subset of
worlds where $E$ holds true. That restriction is what makes empirical observation informative: it eliminates
untenable hypotheses, requiring the surviving beliefs to be renormalized over the remaining space.

Bayes' rule provides the exact arithmetic of this renormalization:

$$\mathbb{P}(H \mid E) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(E \mid H)\mkern3mu\mathbb{P}(H)}{\mathbb{P}(E \mid H)\mkern3mu\mathbb{P}(H) + \mathbb{P}(E \mid \neg H)\mkern3mu\mathbb{P}(\neg H)}.$$

The operational lesson of Bayes' rule is concentrated in the denominator. The posterior probability depends
every bit as heavily on the prior base rate $\mathbb{P}(H)$ as on the likelihood ratio of the evidence. Even an
instrument with a remarkable 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity deployed against a rare event with prevalence
$\mathbb{P}(H) = 0.001$ will produce a posterior probability:

$$\mathbb{P}(H \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{(0.99 \times 0.001) + (0.05 \times 0.999)} \approx \frac{0.00099}{0.00099 + 0.04995} \approx 0.0194.$$

Over 98% of positive alarms will be false. The rarer the event, the more the posterior is governed by the ambient base rate rather than by the detector's nominal accuracy. Alan Turing and I. J. Good's log-likelihood weight of evidence, $W = 10\log\_{10}(\mathrm{TPR}/\mathrm{FPR})$, measures the same gap in decibans (Good 1950), and Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage's natural-frequency format shows that the arithmetic is not the obstacle: the same facts stated as counts make the base rate visible where the conditional probabilities hid it (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995). And as Deborah Mayo proved for severe testing (Mayo 2018) and John Ioannidis demonstrated for low-powered screening (Ioannidis 2005), conditioning on a nominal $p < 0.05$ threshold without pre-registered severity is no guarantee that published discoveries replicate.

## 4. Evidence that accumulates: e-processes and anytime validity

Classical Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing requires the sample size $n$ to be pre-registered and fixed in advance. Monitor the accumulating data instead and stop the experiment the moment a p-value drops below the nominal level $\alpha$, and the cumulative false-positive rate inflates strictly above $\alpha$ as the number of looks grows, approaching certainty under unbounded peeking. This "optional stopping" pathology invalidates classical fixed-horizon statistical inference.

Modern game-theoretic probability and the theory of martingales resolve this through **e-processes** (Ville 1939; Ramdas et al. 2023). An *e-value* $E\_t$ is a non-negative random variable whose expectation under the null hypothesis is bounded by unity: $\mathbb{E}\_{H\_0}[E\_t] \le 1$. Under repeated testing, an e-process is a non-negative supermartingale. Jean Ville's (1939) maximal inequality guarantees that the probability of the process *ever* exceeding a critical threshold $1/\alpha$ across an infinite time horizon is strictly bounded:

$$\mathbb{P}\_{H\_0}\left(\exists t \ge 1:\ E\_t \ge \frac{1}{\alpha}\right) \le \alpha.$$

Because the Ville inequality holds uniformly over all stopping times $\tau$, an investigator or regulatory agency can monitor an ongoing production stream, clinical trial, or financial market continuously. Stopping early when evidence is overwhelming does not compromise statistical validity; the guarantee is baked into the supermartingale structure, and it licenses continuous surveillance without the penalties of classical alpha-spending schedules.

To price the freedom of continuous monitoring in interval width (whose partial-identification coupling is derived in [Chapter 44](44-underdetermination-partial-identification.md)), evaluate the normal-mixture confidence sequence half-width at step $t \ge 1$, dispersion $\sigma > 0$ (in the units of $\bar{x}\_t$), error level $\alpha \in (0, 0.5]$, and mixture scale $\rho^2 > 0$:

$$r\_t(\alpha) = \sigma \sqrt{\frac{2(t\rho^2 + 1)}{t^2\rho^2}\mkern3mu\ln\mkern-3mu\left(\frac{\sqrt{t\rho^2 + 1}}{\alpha}\right)}.$$

The dimensionless ratio $r\_t(\alpha)/\sigma$ is strictly invariant in $\sigma$ and monotonically decreasing in $t$, decaying at rate $\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\ln(t)/t}\bigr)$ rather than at the fixed-sample rate $z\_{1-\alpha/2}/\sqrt{t}$. The price of the freedom to stop is a width premium that shrinks with the horizon and never disappears, and it is worst at the first look, where the mixture has had no data to concentrate on. The honest sequence pays for the looks it has not yet taken.

1. **Single-step initial boundary ($t = 1$):** the mixture is dominated by its prior scale, the logarithm is at its largest relative to $t$, and the half-width is of the order of the dispersion itself rather than of any standard error.
2. **Large-sample horizon ($t \to \infty$):** the half-width contracts toward the fixed-sample nominal one, but stays wider by the factor $\sqrt{\ln t}$, which is exactly the insurance premium for the stopping freedom.
3. **Step-by-step benchmark (any moderate $t$):** the premium is computable in closed form from the expression above and is always greater than one, so an anytime-valid interval quoted at a moderate horizon is visibly wider than the nominal interval a fixed-sample report would print.

Alongside the interval radius, an e-process tracks cumulative betting wealth $E\_T = \prod\_{t=1}^T \exp(\lambda z\_t - \frac{1}{2}\lambda^2)$ ([Chapter 15](15-probability-as-a-measure.md)) and discounts it by Imre Lakatos's problemshift ratio, $E\_T^{\mathrm{adj}} = E\_T / (K + 1)$, where $K$ counts *ad hoc* auxiliary adjustments (Lakatos 1970). The shape of that trajectory is the argument: wealth grows while the bet is smaller than the realized drift and is dragged back toward zero by variance drag once the bet is too aggressive, so an over-bet converts an evidential surplus into nothing at all.

## 5. Probability is a language, not an intrinsic property

There is an obstinate temptation to treat probability as an intrinsic physical property of an object, analogous to mass or electrical charge. Two honest observers with different institutional histories and different prior evidence can evaluate the same data, apply Bayes' rule with impeccable arithmetic, and remain permanently apart in their posterior conclusions.

As John Maynard Keynes argued in *A Treatise on Probability* (Keynes 1921) and Peter Walley formalized in the theory of imprecise probabilities (Walley 1991), a probability statement must always be accompanied by its evidential weight $V(n\_{\mathrm{eff}}) = n\_{\mathrm{eff}} / (n\_{\mathrm{eff}} + n\_0)$. What Stochology forbids is the authoritarian appeal to "the" objective probability as a mandate that silences political and ethical contestation. When two parties disagree on an action under uncertainty, the proper response is to decompose the decision into its constitutive elements (the prior bounds, the loss matrix, the scale invariance, the empirical evidence), so that the locus of disagreement is laid bare.

### The clause that stopping deletes

An interval that is valid at the end of a fixed hundred measurements is not valid if you were allowed to stop at twenty. The price of that freedom is width: the same dispersion, level and sample size buy a wider interval under a rule that permits stopping. Probability is a language, and this is the clause in its grammar that premature stopping tries to delete.

The clause has a shape worth carrying into a meeting: the radius grows with the square root of the logarithm of the number of looks, so looking many times costs less than a linear rule would suggest, and looking once costs nothing at all. A nominal five per cent spent across many looks is not a five per cent claim, and the excess is worse the shorter the horizon over which the looks are spent.

A second clause is about the arithmetic of rescue: every *ad hoc* auxiliary adjustment divides the evidential wealth by one more unit, so a large evidential quantity deflated by a handful of adjustments can lose an order of magnitude in decibans. The language does not stop being probabilistic when adjustments are added; it stops being honest if they are not counted.

### When the event is single and not repeatable

The language must be refused when the event happens once and the bet is not divisible, because no frequency exists for it and the probability has no referent. The procedure is to replace the probability by an order of magnitude with its class declared, using the invariant statistics of dominance, and to say that the substitution was made rather than to leave the reader with a number that looks like a frequency.
