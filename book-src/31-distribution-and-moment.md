## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Describe a quantity by its distribution rather than by its average, and say what the average
   discards.
2. Read expectation, variance and covariance as summaries with different failure modes.
3. Recognise a heavy tail from data, and say why it breaks the arithmetic you were taught first.

## 1. From a number to a distribution

A random variable is a formal rule mapping outcomes of a sample space to numerical values, and its probability distribution is the exhaustive mathematical description of that rule. The word *exhaustive* is load-bearing. Everything the variable can physically do (every extreme excursion, every tail decay rate, every non-linear coupling with other variables) is encoded in the distribution and in nothing less.

Replacing an entire probability distribution with a single scalar average is the most pervasive and destructive simplification in applied science and policy. As demonstrated in [The Average Is Not the Story](02-the-average-is-not-the-story.md), a distribution whose mean sits an order of magnitude above its median will annihilate an organization whose reserves were sized on the mean, the first time the single catastrophic draw arrives. What this chapter formalizes is the machinery of moments, and the non-linear gaps that open where convex losses meet dispersion.

## 2. Expectation, variance, covariance, and their failure modes

The mathematical expectation $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is a probability-weighted integral over all *counterfactual possibilities*, not a descriptive summary of past observations. The difference matters: an expectation can sit at a value that is physically impossible, as with a household size whose expectation is fractional. And where the data-generating process shifts structurally or carries heavy tails, sample averages do not converge to the expectation at the rates intuition assumes.

The variance $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2]$ measures dispersion around the mean, but it inherits and squares every sensitivity of the expectation, so it penalizes extreme outliers heavily. In the heavy-tailed distributions characteristic of operational risk, disaster losses and network delays, the theoretical variance can be literally infinite while every individual observation in the database is finite. Treating a sample standard deviation as a measure of risk in such a regime is a mathematical fallacy, and a dangerous one.

The covariance $\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])]$ quantifies linear co-movement,
answering a far narrower question than decision-makers assume. Two variables can exhibit massive covariance because
$X$ causes $Y$, because $Y$ causes $X$, because an unmeasured common shock drives both, or because the archive's
selection filter admitted only units possessing high values of both. A covariance matrix carries zero causal authority;
the transition from *observed co-movement* to *interventional response* requires structural causal identification
([Chapter 39](39-cause-intervention-counterfactual.md)).

## 3. Heavy tails, the Hill estimator, and Pickands-Balkema-de Haan

A distribution is classified by how its upper tail decays. Under a power-law tail $\mathbb{P}(X > x) \sim L(x) x^{-\alpha}$, the magnitude of the tail index $\alpha > 0$ decides which moments exist, and there are three regimes:

1. **$\alpha > 2$**: The variance and mean are both finite. The classical Central Limit Theorem holds, and sample
   averages converge at standard $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ rates.
2. **$1 < \alpha \le 2$**: The mean is finite, but the theoretical variance is infinite. The sample average converges
   extremely slowly, while sample variances wander erratically and grow without bound as more data is collected.
3. **$\alpha \le 1$**: Even the theoretical mean is infinite. The arithmetic average of a sample does not settle to
   a constant; it drifts upward indefinitely as larger historical records encounter larger catastrophic draws.

Under the Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem of extreme value theory, the distribution of excesses over a sufficiently
high threshold $u$ converges asymptotically to the Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD):

$$G(y; \xi, \beta) = 1 - \left(1 + \frac{\xi y}{\beta}\right)^{-1/\xi},$$

where the shape parameter $\xi = 1/\alpha$ determines tail heaviness ($\xi > 0$ indicating heavy Fréchet-type tails).
To estimate $\alpha$ directly from sample order statistics $X\_{(1)} \le X\_{(2)} \le \dots \le X\_{(n)}$, the
classical **Hill estimator** evaluates the $k$ largest observations above $X\_{(n-k)}$ ($1 \le k \le n - 1$):

$$\hat{\alpha}\_k = \frac{k}{\sum\_{i=1}^k \ln\left(X\_{(n-i+1)} / X\_{(n-k)}\right)}.$$

In real-world data, the estimated $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ varies dramatically with the choice of cut-off $k$. A stable
"Hill plot" plateau is the only valid empirical evidence for a stable tail index; a single estimate without an
interval of stability across $k$ is deceptive.

### 3.1 Where the index comes from

The estimator is a maximum-likelihood estimate under an exact Pareto tail, and the derivation is three lines of algebra. Write the tail as $\mathbb{P}(X > x) = (x/x\_{\min})^{-\alpha}$ above the threshold. The probability integral transform takes it to a log-scale exponential, since $\mathbb{P}\bigl(\ln(X/x\_{\min}) > y\bigr) = e^{-\alpha y}$.
For an exponential sample the log-scale gaps between consecutive order statistics are governed by the
representation of Alfréd Rényi (1953): the gap from the $j$-th largest down to the next is the minimum of $j$
exponential variables and is therefore exponential with rate $j\alpha$, and the gaps are independent as $j$
varies. Multiplying each gap by its own count removes the count from the rate,
$$D\_j = j\bigl(\ln X\_{(n-j+1)} - \ln X\_{(n-j)}\bigr) \mkern5mu\sim\mkern5mu \mathrm{Exp}(\alpha), \qquad D\_1, \dots, D\_k \text{ independent},$$
which is the step that returns $\alpha$: the factor $j$ is not a correction applied afterwards but the reciprocal
of the rate the gap inherited from the $j$ variables sitting above it. Summation by parts turns Hill's average into their sum,
$$\sum\_{i=1}^{k}\ln\frac{X\_{(n-i+1)}}{X\_{(n-k)}} \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \sum\_{j=1}^{k} j\bigl(\ln X\_{(n-j+1)} - \ln X\_{(n-j)}\bigr) \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \sum\_{j=1}^{k} D\_j \mkern5mu=:\mkern5mu S\_k,$$
so that
$$\hat{\alpha}\_k = \frac{k}{S\_k} = \frac{k}{\sum\_{j=1}^{k} D\_j}.$$
The index is the reciprocal of the mean of $k$ independent exponential variables — the maximum-likelihood
estimator of their rate, written in the only form in which its sampling behaviour is visible. What the estimator
assumes is in that derivation and nowhere else: it is exact only if the tail is exactly Pareto above the
threshold, and every departure of $L$ from a constant appears as a departure of the spacings from exponentiality.

### 3.2 The bias of selecting the minimum

The threshold is not fixed outside the sample. $X\_{(n-k)}$ is the smallest value retained, so Hill's estimator
normalizes by the minimum of its own tail. Raise the anchor one notch, to $X\_{(n-k+1)}$, and keep $k$ terms: the
last term becomes $\ln 1 = 0$ and the sum collapses, pathwise, onto the $(k-1)$-term statistic with that same
anchor,
$$\sum\_{i=1}^{k}\ln\frac{X\_{(n-i+1)}}{X\_{(n-k+1)}} \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \sum\_{j=1}^{k-1} D\_j \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu S\_k - D\_k \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \frac{k - 1}{\hat{\alpha}\_{k-1}},$$
so the variant and Hill's statistic do not differ by a constant: they differ by the single spacing $D\_k$, and
their expectations stand in the ratio $(k-1)/k$ because $\mathbb{E}[S\_k] = k/\alpha$ and $\mathbb{E}[S\_k - D\_k] =
(k-1)/\alpha$. The ratio is a statement about means, and it is the sum that carries it.

The anchor enters $S\_k$ with total weight $k$ — once in each of the $k$ terms — and it enters $D\_k$ with the
same weight $k$, since $D\_k = k(\ln X\_{(n-k+1)} - \ln X\_{(n-k)})$. The two appearances therefore cancel in
$S\_k - D\_k$, and the variant's sensitivity to the anchor is of the same order as Hill's rather than $k$ times
larger. That is why the distortion is a factor of order unity and not a factor of $k$: the anchor is used $k$
times in both statistics, and used is not the same as counted.

Correcting the anchor does not remove the bias, it locates it. With the spacings exponential, $\sum\_{j=1}^k D\_j$
is Gamma$(k, \alpha)$, and the reciprocal of a gamma is not unbiased for its rate:
$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}\_k] = \alpha\mkern3mu\frac{k}{k-1}, \qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\alpha}\_k) = \alpha^2\mkern3mu\frac{k^2}{(k-1)^2(k-2)} \quad (k > 2).$$
For the raised anchor the sum is Gamma$(k-1,\alpha)$, so the same reciprocal gives
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}'] = \alpha k/(k-2)$ — larger than Hill's $\alpha k/(k-1)$ by $(k-1)/(k-2)$ (a lighter inferred tail), not by the
reciprocal $k/(k-1)$ of the $(k-1)/k$ factor the sum lost: the reciprocal of a mean is not the mean of the reciprocal, and the
two factors differ by $O(1/k^2)$. A variant that anchors on its own smallest retained value therefore reports a
tail systematically lighter than the one it saw, and most strongly where the tail sample is smallest.
The index is biased upward by $\alpha/(k-1)$, and the bias decays only as $1/k$ while the interval's half-width
decays as $1/\sqrt{k}$: the reported interval is centred on the wrong point by an amount that becomes negligible
only at the large cut-offs where the Pareto approximation is least defensible. That is the minimum-selection bias
in closed form — not an effect of heaviness, but of the anchor being the smallest retained extreme and the
reciprocal amplifying its sampling error.

### 3.3 The cut-off trades bias for variance

When $L$ is not constant, $e^{-\alpha y}$ is only the leading term of the tail ratio. Writing
$L(ue^y)/L(u) = 1 + c\mkern3mu u^{-\beta}e^{-\beta y} + o(u^{-\beta})$ for some $\beta > 0$, the spacings are exponential
times a tilt whose sign follows the sign of $c$; and because the anchor sits at the empirical quantile of level
$k/n$, the estimator's expectation acquires a term of order $(k/n)^{\beta}$, increasing in $k$, while its
variance falls as $\alpha^2/k$. Raising the cut-off therefore buys precision with bias, the optimum depends on a
second-order parameter the tail does not report, and two estimates at two cut-offs are not two measurements of
one quantity but two points on one trade. A plateau is the region where the two errors are of comparable size;
outside it one of them dominates, and the estimate describes the cut-off rather than the tail.

### 3.4 When the mean stops representing risk

The three regimes above are one integral read at three values. For the Pareto tail the $p$-th moment is
$$\mathbb{E}[X^p] = \int\_{x\_{\min}}^{\infty} x^p\mkern3mu \alpha x\_{\min}^{\alpha}x^{-\alpha-1}\mkern3mu dx
= \frac{\alpha\mkern3mu x\_{\min}^{p}}{\alpha - p} \quad (p < \alpha),$$
and the integral diverges for $p \ge \alpha$, so the boundaries at $p = 1$ and $p = 2$ are that identity rather
than a convention. The operational content is in the rate, not in the existence. For $1 < \alpha \le 2$ the sample
mean converges, but its fluctuations scale as $n^{1/\alpha - 1}$, which decays more slowly than $n^{-1/2}$ exactly
because $\alpha < 2$ (Gnedenko & Kolmogorov 1954): a standard error computed as $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ therefore
narrows faster than the estimate improves, and the interval tightens around a mean whose error is governed by a
different exponent. At $\alpha \le 1$ there is no exponent left to apply — the average drifts, and no sample size
turns it into an estimate.

The mean therefore represents risk in the sense a standard error assumes only for $\alpha > 2$; between one and
two it is a convergent quantity with a non-Gaussian error; at or below one it is not a quantity at all. Which case
holds is what the index reports, and because the index carries the bias of §3.2, the interval around it is part of
the answer rather than a decoration on it.

## 4. Proper scoring rules and CRPS decomposition

Because point forecasts discard distribution information, evaluating probabilistic predictions requires **proper
scoring rules** that reward both calibration (honesty) and sharpness (resolution). Tilmann Gneiting and Adrian Raftery
formalized the **Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)** for a predictive cumulative distribution $F$ and an
observed scalar outcome $y$:

$$\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int\_{-\infty}^{\infty} \bigl(F(x) - \mathbf{1}\_{\lbrace{}x \ge y\rbrace}\bigr)^2\mkern3mu dx.$$

CRPS measures the integrated squared distance between the predicted distribution and the empirical Heaviside step function of the realization. It generalizes mean absolute error to probabilistic forecasts and keeps the same units as the variable. It also decomposes cleanly:

$$\mathrm{CRPS} = \text{Reliability (Miscalibration)} - \text{Resolution} + \text{Uncertainty}.$$

An honest forecasting institution strives to minimize CRPS by improving resolution while holding reliability near zero,
rather than gaming simple point accuracy metrics.

## 5. Convexity, Jensen's inequality, and the Jensen gap

When a decision system converts a physical quantity $X$ into financial cost, casualty loss, or waiting delay, the
transformation function $\phi(x)$ is rarely linear. In almost all real-world processes, damage accelerates: under
the quadratic damage curve used in flood risk, a doubling of flood height produces a quadrupling of structural
damage, and under the Kingman hyperbola $\rho/(1-\rho)$ the expected wait diverges as load approaches capacity
rather than rising in proportion to it.

A function $\phi$ is strictly convex if any secant line between two points lies strictly above the curve.
**Jensen's inequality** states that for any convex loss function $\phi$ and any non-degenerate random variable $X$:

$$\phi\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]\bigr) \le \mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(X)\bigr].$$

The difference between the expected loss and the loss evaluated at the expected input is the **Jensen gap**:

$$\Delta\_J = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(X)\bigr] - \phi\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]\bigr) \ge 0.$$

The operational consequence is severe: an institution that sizes its vital reserves, staffing, or financial liquidity
against the *average* load $\mathbb{E}[X]$ will systematically underfund the actual expected damage $\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]$.
The Jensen gap $\Delta\_J$ is not an optional buffer added for conservatism; it is the deficit incurred by mistaking
a point expectation for a distributed reality.

## 6. Underdetermination and competing risks bounds

When the historical archive is incomplete or censored, even the probability distribution $F$ cannot be
uniquely identified. Under dependent competing risks (patient death from disease versus death from competing
co-morbidities), Arthur V. Peterson (1976) proved that without unverifiable independence assumptions the true
joint survival function $S\_T(t) = \mathbb{P}(\min(T\_1, T\_2) > t)$ is bounded by sharp Fréchet bounds:

$$\max\bigl(0,\mkern3mu S\_1(t) + S\_2(t) - 1\bigr) \le S\_T(t) \le \min\bigl(S\_1(t),\mkern3mu S\_2(t)\bigr),$$

where $S\_1(t)$ and $S\_2(t)$ are the observed marginal survival rates. A naive calculation that multiplies
$S\_1(t) S\_2(t)$ asserts dogmatic independence where the empirical archive supports only the bracketed interval.
When underdetermination is structural, the set is the only honest distribution.

### Which moments exist

On the Copenhagen reinsurance fire-claim series studied by McNeil (1997) the Hill index sits well below two, and
it stays below two across a wide plateau in the cut-off $k$. That placement is the whole finding, and it is not a
sampling artifact: it says the variance of the severity distribution does not exist, while the sample mean still
converges and looks reassuring. A moment either exists or it does not, and no amount of data moves the boundary:
more observations estimate the index better, they do not make the second moment arrive.

Evaluating the loss at the mean rather than the mean of the loss understates it by an amount that is large and
entirely attributable to curvature: with a convex damage function the two quantities differ by the Jensen gap,
and the gap does not shrink as the archive grows.

A forecast that is a distribution must be scored as a distribution: the mean squared error rewards a point and
ignores the shape, while a proper score charges for the shape. Two forecasts with the same point error and
different spreads are the same forecast to the first rule and different forecasts to the second.

### When the damage per event is capped

The heavy tail stops governing the decision once the damage per event is bounded by contract, because the cap
replaces the index as the relevant statistic. The procedure is to substitute the indemnity ceiling for the
estimated index and to recompute the score, and the conclusion survives only while the ceiling stays above the
quantile of the severity against which the reserve was sized.

## 7. The index at the cut, the cap and the retention

### Two cuts solved by hand

The Hill estimator is a ratio of logarithms, which means both of its extreme values can be written out without a
computer. At $k = 1$ the denominator carries a single term and
$\hat{\alpha}\_1 = 1/\ln(X\_{(n)}/X\_{(n-1)})$, the reciprocal log-gap of the two largest order statistics. At the opposite extreme $k = n - 1$, every observation above the sample minimum $X\_{(1)}$ enters and $\hat{\alpha}\_{n-1} = (n - 1) / \sum\_{i=2}^{n} \ln(X\_{(i)}/X\_{(1)})$. Both figures are
exact functions of where the analyst cuts, not of what the world does, and any quoted index sits between them at
its own $k$. The estimator at $k=1$ is scale-free: a redenomination of the currency moves neither term, so a shift
in the index across two reinsurance treaties is a statement about their cut-offs until proven otherwise. What an
honest report therefore carries is the whole curve rather than one point of it, the index over a grid of $k$
together with the spread across that grid.

The second limit decides budgets. Expanding $\phi$ about $\mu$ gives
$\phi(\mu+\epsilon) = \phi(\mu) + \phi'(\mu)\epsilon + \tfrac12 \phi''(\mu)\epsilon^2$, hence
$\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)] - \phi(\mu) \approx \tfrac12 \phi''(\mu)\sigma^2$: the Jensen gap is locally the product of
the second derivative at the mean and the variance, and it vanishes quadratically as the dispersion vanishes. At
zero dispersion the two accounts of the loss coincide exactly, and the sentence *we hold the mean plus a buffer*
turns out to mean *the buffer is the gap*.

### The cap that makes the suggested reserve wrong

The chapter's recommendation is arithmetic rather than prudential: fund $\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]$ and not
$\phi(\mathbb{E}[X])$, because the difference is a deficit and not a margin. Put the same severity distribution
behind an indemnity contract with a retention $M$, so that the insured severity is $X \wedge M$. The insured
expectation $\mathbb{E}[\phi(X \wedge M)]$ falls below the uncapped one by the whole mass of severity above the
retention, and an institution that follows the recommendation literally carries, per event, units that no contract
will ever pay. The sharper failure runs the other way: the gap does not disappear under the cap, because
$\mathbb{E}[\phi(X \wedge M)]$ still exceeds $\phi(\mathbb{E}[X \wedge M])$, so the companion rule *substitute the
ceiling for the index and stop* is wrong twice — it stops too early, because curvature survives the truncation,
and it stops at the wrong number, because the truncation removes part of the expectation from the fund.

What the form of §5 licenses is therefore narrower than the chapter claims. The Jensen gap is exact for the
quantity $\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]$, and a reserve is a payment function of $X$ that is almost never that quantity;
whenever the payment is capped, netted, or shared, the object to fund is $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$ for the institution's
own $g$, and the gap of $g$ must be recomputed rather than inherited.

### The archive is left-truncated, so the index is conditional

The condition that voids the chapter is not a tail event but a selection filter. The archive that supplies such
estimates is the Copenhagen reinsurance series of fire claims above a retention, assembled for exactly this kind of
tail estimation (McNeil 1997). Its smallest claim sits exactly at the retention and no observation falls below it:
the series records claims *ceded above a retention*, so the empirical severity is left-truncated by a contractual
decision and the estimated index is the index of that decision's output. Change the retention and the index changes
with it; nothing physical has moved, and the estimator will report the new policy as a new law of nature. The
procedure when this matters is to estimate on the exposure rather than on the ceded book (the number of policies
written and their limits) or, where the exposure is unavailable, to print the index as conditional on the retention
and to refuse any comparison of indices across two retentions. The archive's own lower boundary is the tell, and it
is visible at a glance.

Truncation is not the only way the conditional hides. Split the same series at any administrative boundary — a
change of reinsurance treaty or of accounting regime — and the index at a fixed $k$ moves while both halves remain
below two. The conclusion about the variance survives the split intact while the index that was supposed to support
it shifts, which is the useful pattern: the moment boundary holds because it is a property of the mechanism, and
the index is fragile because it is a property of the sample.
