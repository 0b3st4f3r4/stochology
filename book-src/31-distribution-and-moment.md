## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Describe a quantity by its distribution rather than by its average, and say what the average
   discards.
2. Read expectation, variance and covariance as summaries with different failure modes.
3. Recognise a heavy tail from data, and say why it breaks the arithmetic you were taught first.

## 1. From a number to a distribution

In [Chapter 13](13-integrals-and-accumulation.md) and [Chapter 15](15-probability-as-a-measure.md), a random variable appeared as a measurable map sending sample outcomes to numbers, weighted by probability integrals. Its cumulative distribution $F(x)$ captures the entire law of that single variable—every typical day and every catastrophic tail jump—whereas nonlinear dependencies across multiple variables live in their joint law and cannot be reconstructed from single variables alone.

Replacing a full distribution with one average is the most destructive habit in policy and engineering. As [The Average Is Not the Story](02-the-average-is-not-the-story.md) showed, when a mean sits far above a median, buffers sized to the average break on the very surge that mattered. This chapter formalizes distributions, moment breakdowns, tail indices, proper scoring rules, and convexity gaps at formalization guard `T4` ([Chapter 29](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md)).

## 2. Expectation, variance, covariance, and their failure modes

Building on Lebesgue integration ([Chapter 13](13-integrals-and-accumulation.md)), the mathematical expectation $\mathbb{E}[X]$ weights every admissible outcome by probability rather than summarizing past draws. It can sit at an impossible value such as a fractional family size. Worse, when distributions carry heavy tails or shift across regimes, sample averages converge slowly—or drift without bound.

The variance $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2]$ measures spread around the mean by squaring every deviation. Because squaring amplifies extreme values, operational disasters, floods, and network outages often carry **infinite theoretical variance** even while every logged row in a table stays finite. Using a sample standard deviation to price reserves in that regime is a mathematical error.

The covariance $\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])]$ measures only linear co-movement, answering a far narrower question than managers assume. Two variables move together when $X$ drives $Y$, when $Y$ drives $X$, when an unseen shock moves both, or when intake filters log only high values of both. Covariance carries zero causal authority; moving from passive association to real intervention requires structural causal identification ([Chapter 39](39-cause-intervention-counterfactual.md)).

## 3. Heavy tails, the Hill estimator, and Pickands-Balkema-de Haan

Standard bell curves decay exponentially, so events ten times larger than average practically never happen. Real operational, flood, and financial shocks often follow **power-law tails**, where exceeding huge thresholds declines algebraically as $\mathbb{P}(X > x) \sim L(x) x^{-\alpha}$ ($\alpha > 0$, with $L(x)$ a slowly varying adjustment). The tail exponent $\alpha$ tells us how fast extreme probability drops: smaller $\alpha$ means a fatter tail.

On non-negative losses $X \ge 0$ (or two-sided tails $\mathbb{P}(|X| > x) \sim L(x) x^{-\alpha}$ when both signs carry extremes), that single exponent splits moment existence—which averages and spreads even exist mathematically—into four distinct regimes:

1. **$\alpha > 4$:** Mean, variance, and fourth moment are all finite. Sample variances stabilize cleanly, and classic Gaussian central limit rates ($O(1/\sqrt{n})$) hold.
2. **$2 < \alpha \le 4$:** Mean and variance remain finite and averages converge, yet for pure Pareto tails ($L(x) = c > 0$, or whenever $\int^\infty x^3 L(x)x^{-\alpha}\mkern3mu dx = \infty$) the fourth moment diverges (infinite kurtosis, unless a decaying factor like $L(x) = (\ln x)^{-2}$ rescues finiteness right at $\alpha = 4$; for $2 < \alpha \le 3$ and pure Pareto tails even skewness $\gamma\_1$ and cubic Taylor terms diverge), making sample variance erratic.
3. **$1 < \alpha \le 2$:** The mean stays finite, yet for pure Pareto tails ($L(x) = c > 0$, or whenever $\int^\infty x L(x)x^{-\alpha}\mkern3mu dx = \infty$) theoretical variance explodes to infinity and sample variances grow indefinitely (unless a decaying factor like $L(x) = (\ln x)^{-2}$ rescues finiteness right at $\alpha = 2$).
4. **$\alpha \le 1$:** For pure Pareto tails (and whenever $\int^\infty L(x)x^{-\alpha}\mkern3mu dx = \infty$), even the mean does not exist. Sample averages climb upward whenever a bigger extreme arrives.

To model shocks above a high threshold $u$ without guessing the whole body of a distribution, extreme-value theory looks only at excesses $y = X - u > 0$. For laws in the maximum domain of attraction $F \in \mathrm{MDA}(H\_\xi)$, the **Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem** (Balkema & de Haan 1974; Pickands 1975) proves that excesses converge toward the **Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD)** with shape $\xi$ and threshold-dependent scale $\beta = \beta(u) > 0$:

$$G(y; \xi, \beta) = 1 - \left(1 + \frac{\xi y}{\beta}\right)^{-1/\xi} \qquad \left(\beta > 0,\mkern5mu y > 0,\mkern5mu 1 + \frac{\xi y}{\beta} > 0,\mkern5mu \xi \ne 0\right).$$

Read aloud, $\beta > 0$ sets the scale of typical excesses and $1 + \xi y/\beta > 0$ keeps the support well-defined (forcing $y < -\beta/\xi$ when $\xi < 0$, and approaching $1 - \exp(-y/\beta)$ as $\xi \to 0$). Heavy power-law tails correspond to $\xi = 1/\alpha > 0$.

When tails follow power laws, how do we estimate $\alpha$ from data? Sort the $n$ observations $X\_{(1)} \le \dots \le X\_{(n)}$, pick the largest $k$ points above cutoff $X\_{(n-k)} > 0$ ($1 \le k \le n - 1$), and measure how far those top observations stretch in logarithmic ratios above the cutoff. The **Hill estimator** (Hill 1975) computes the reciprocal of that average log-stretch:

$$\hat{\alpha}\_k = \frac{k}{S\_k}, \qquad S\_k = \sum\_{i=1}^k \ln\\!\left(\frac{X\_{(n-i+1)}}{X\_{(n-k)}}\right) > 0.$$

Requiring $X\_{(n-k)} > 0$ and $S\_k > 0$ prevents undefined logarithms and division-by-zero blow-up when top observations tie ($X\_{(n)} = X\_{(n-k)}$). Because $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ shifts as $k$ changes, Embrechts, Klüppelberg, and Mikosch (1997) warn against "Hill horror plots," where slowly varying factors $L(x)$ or log-normal shapes mimic fake plateaus. A pre-registered $k$-grid with uncertainty bands therefore serves only as an offline diagnostic, never crisis proof of finite moments ([Chapter 36](36-decision-loss-threshold.md)).

### 3.1 Where the index comes from

Why does averaging log-ratios estimate $\alpha$? On a log scale, power-law excesses turn into ordinary exponential waiting times, and the gaps between sorted exponential variables behave like independent waiting times whose rates grow linearly with how many top values sit above each step.

Write the exact Pareto tail above threshold $x\_{\min} > 0$ as $\mathbb{P}(X > x) = (x/x\_{\min})^{-\alpha}$. Taking logs gives $\mathbb{P}\bigl(\ln(X/x\_{\min}) > y\bigr) = e^{-\alpha y}$, an exponential distribution. For exponential draws, Alfréd Rényi (1953) showed that the spacing from the $j$-th largest down to the next equals the minimum of $j$ independent exponential timers, so each gap is independent and exponential with rate $j\alpha$. Multiplying each gap by its index $j$ restores rate $\alpha$:

$$D\_j = j\bigl(\ln X\_{(n-j+1)} - \ln X\_{(n-j)}\bigr) \mkern5mu\sim\mkern5mu \mathrm{Exp}(\alpha), \qquad D\_1, \dots, D\_k \text{ independent}.$$

Summation by parts turns Hill's total $S\_k$ directly into the sum of those scaled gaps:

$$\sum\_{i=1}^{k}\ln\frac{X\_{(n-i+1)}}{X\_{(n-k)}} \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \sum\_{j=1}^{k} j\bigl(\ln X\_{(n-j+1)} - \ln X\_{(n-j)}\bigr) \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \sum\_{j=1}^{k} D\_j \mkern5mu=:\mkern5mu S\_k.$$

Substituting into $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ yields:

$$\hat{\alpha}\_k = \frac{k}{S\_k} = \frac{k}{\sum\_{j=1}^{k} D\_j}.$$

In plain words, Hill's index is simply the maximum-likelihood rate estimator of $k$ independent exponential draws. Every assumption of the estimator sits inside that derivation: whenever the true tail deviates from exact Pareto ($L(x)$ non-constant), the spacings deviate from pure exponentiality.

### 3.2 The bias of selecting the minimum

Notice that Hill's cutoff $X\_{(n-k)}$ is not an external fixed number—it is a data-selected threshold sitting below the $k$ top observations. What happens if someone shifts that anchor up one notch to $X\_{(n-k+1)}$ while keeping $k$ in the numerator?

With the raised anchor ($k \ge 2$), the last log-ratio equals $\ln 1 = 0$, so the sum drops the $k$-th gap $D\_k$ and shrinks pathwise to $S\_k - D\_k$:

$$\sum\_{i=1}^{k}\ln\frac{X\_{(n-i+1)}}{X\_{(n-k+1)}} \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \sum\_{j=1}^{k-1} D\_j \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu S\_k - D\_k \mkern5mu=\mkern5mu \frac{k - 1}{\hat{\alpha}\_{k-1}} \qquad (k \ge 2).$$

Their expectations stand in the ratio $(k-1)/k$ because $\mathbb{E}[S\_k] = k/\alpha$ while $\mathbb{E}[S\_k - D\_k] = (k-1)/\alpha$. Why doesn't moving the anchor shift the total $k$ times as much? Because the anchor enters $S\_k$ with weight $k$ and enters $D\_k$ with the exact same weight $k$, so both terms cancel in $S\_k - D\_k$.

Even with the standard anchor, taking a reciprocal introduces systematic upward bias. Under exact Pareto tails, $\sum\_{j=1}^k D\_j$ follows a Gamma$(k, \alpha)$ law, and the expectation of its reciprocal sits above $\alpha$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}\_k] = \alpha\mkern3mu\frac{k}{k-1}, \qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\alpha}\_k) = \alpha^2\mkern3mu\frac{k^2}{(k-1)^2(k-2)} \quad (k > 1 \text{ for } \mathbb{E};\mkern5mu k > 2 \text{ for } \mathrm{Var}).$$

Raising the anchor to $X\_{(n-k+1)}$ turns the sum into Gamma$(k - 1, \alpha)$, pushing expected index even higher to $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}'] = \alpha k/(k-2)$ for $k > 2$—inflating $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}\_k]$ by factor $(k-1)/(k-2)$, which differs from the inverse $k/(k-1)$ of the sum's $(k-1)/k$ factor by $\frac{1}{(k-1)(k-2)} = O(1/k^2)$ (and from $(k-1)/k$ itself by $\Theta(1/k)$) because the reciprocal of a mean never equals the mean of a reciprocal. Even standard $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ carries positive bias $\alpha/(k-1) = O(1/k)$; while that bias shrinks faster asymptotically than the $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ interval half-width as $k \to \infty$, at small tail counts (such as $k = 3$) it still slants estimates sharply upward toward comforting light tails unless corrected.

### 3.3 The cut-off trades bias for variance

When $L(x)$ varies slowly rather than staying constant, picking $k$ trades threshold fidelity against sampling noise. Under a Hall second-order expansion $L(x) = C(1 + c\mkern2mu x^{-\kappa} + o(x^{-\kappa}))$ (Hall 1982, writing $\beta$ on the $x$-scale for convergence rate $\kappa > 0$, distinct from GPD scale $\beta > 0$), the log-excess tail ratio perturbs as:

$$\frac{L(u e^y)}{L(u)} = 1 + c\mkern3mu u^{-\kappa}\bigl(e^{-\kappa y} - 1\bigr) + o(u^{-\kappa}).$$

The correction vanishes at $y = 0$ and bends positive excesses ($y > 0$) by $e^{-\kappa y} - 1 < 0$. That tilt shifts $\mathbb{E}[\ln(X/u) \mid X > u]$ by $-\frac{c\kappa}{\alpha(\alpha+\kappa)}u^{-\kappa} + o(u^{-\kappa})$ (sign $-c$), biasing $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ in the direction of $+c$. Since exceedance probability scales as $k/n \sim C u^{-\alpha}$, the threshold behaves as $u \asymp (n/k)^{1/\alpha}$. Thus the bias grows with $k$ at rate $(k/n)^{\tilde{\beta}}$ (where $\tilde{\beta} = \kappa/\alpha = -\rho > 0$ marks the quantile-scale second-order exponent corresponding to Hall's (1982) $x$-scale exponent $\kappa$, distinct from queue utilization $\rho$ in §5), while estimation standard error shrinks as $\alpha/\sqrt{k}$. At the mean-squared-error optimum where bias matches standard error, unadjusted intervals around $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ miss nominal coverage unless we undersmooth ($k = o(k^\ast)$) or subtract bias—and under slow logarithmic decay, no flat Hill plateau exists at all (Embrechts, Klüppelberg & Mikosch 1997).

### 3.4 When the mean stops representing risk

All four tail regimes read one integral across four cutoffs. For a pure Pareto tail above $x\_{\min} > 0$, the $p$-th raw moment is:

$$\mathbb{E}[X^p] = \int\_{x\_{\min}}^{\infty} x^p\mkern3mu \alpha x\_{\min}^{\alpha}x^{-\alpha-1}\mkern3mu dx
= \frac{\alpha\mkern3mu x\_{\min}^{p}}{\alpha - p} \quad (p < \alpha),$$

and diverges to infinity whenever $p \ge \alpha$ (unless a decaying factor $L(x)$ keeps $\int^\infty x^{p-1}L(x)x^{-\alpha}\mkern3mu dx < \infty$). Read by regime: for $1 < \alpha < 2$, the population mean exists, yet sample averages converge at the sluggish rate $n^{1/\alpha - 1}$ toward an $\alpha$-stable distribution rather than Gaussian $n^{-1/2}$ (Gnedenko & Kolmogorov 1954). Right at $\alpha = 2$, exact Pareto variance diverges so errors decay slowly as $\sqrt{\ln n / n}$. For $\alpha \le 1$, averages wander forever. Standard $\sqrt{n}$ confidence widths therefore require $\alpha > 2$ (and $\alpha > 4$ for stable sample variance). Because every finite-$k$ Hill index carries bias from §3.2–§3.3, the uncertainty band is part of the answer, not garnish.

## 4. Proper scoring rules and CRPS decomposition

How do we grade a full forecast distribution $F$ rather than a single guessed point? A **proper scoring rule** rewards honest probability forecasts for both calibration and sharpness. Building on [Chapter 13](13-integrals-and-accumulation.md), Gneiting and Raftery (2007) formalize the **Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)** comparing cumulative forecast $F(x)$ against scalar outcome $y$:

$$\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int\_{-\infty}^{\infty} \bigl(F(x) - \mathbf{1}\_{\lbrace{}x \ge y\rbrace}\bigr)^2\mkern3mu dx.$$

In plain words, CRPS integrates the squared gap between your predicted probability curve and the step jump at the realized outcome $y$, measured in the physical units of $y$. Gneiting and Raftery (2007) prove that the integral form is proper across Borel probability laws $\mathcal{P}$ (including vacuously $\infty \le \infty$ when expectations diverge) and that finite expected-score comparisons and non-vacuous **strict propriety** hold strictly on the finite-first-moment subclass $\mathcal{P}\_1$ ($\mathbb{E}[|Y|] < \infty$, requiring Pareto $\alpha > 1$, where the kernel form $\mathbb{E}\_F|X - y| - \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\_F|X - X'|$ applies). Under power-law tails, pointwise $\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y)$ converges for every $y \in \mathbb{R}$ when $\alpha > 1/2$ (diverging identically to $+\infty$ for $\alpha \le 1/2$), yet for $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$ we have $\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) \sim y$ as $y \to \infty$ so expected CRPS still diverges to $+\infty$ and the kernel difference collapses to $\infty - \infty$ ([Appendix A](appendix-a-mathematics.md) §A.2). Within $\mathcal{P}\_1$, Hersbach (2000) splits mean CRPS into three legible pieces:

$$\mathrm{CRPS} = \text{Reliability (Miscalibration)} - \text{Resolution} + \text{Uncertainty}.$$

Outside calibrated ensembles, residual resolution can turn negative; good forecasters cut CRPS by sharpening resolution while driving miscalibration to zero.

## 5. Convexity, Jensen's inequality, and the Jensen gap

In [Chapter 12](12-derivatives-and-convexity.md), we saw how curving-upward (convex) harm turns dispersion into extra loss. Flood damage scales faster than flood height, and queue wait times explode as arrival utilization $\rho \in [0, 1)$ nears capacity along Kingman's curve $\rho/(1-\rho)$ (Kingman 1961; formalized in [Chapter 38](38-time-and-dependence.md)).

Recall **Jensen's inequality** (Jensen 1906): whenever loss map $\phi(x)$ is convex—meaning every chord sits on or above the curve—and expectations exist ($\mathbb{E}[|X|] < \infty$, $\mathbb{E}[|\phi(X)|] < \infty$), average loss never falls below the loss at the average input (strictly exceeding it whenever $X$ is non-degenerate and $\phi$ is non-affine on the support of $X$, such as strictly convex loss):

$$\phi\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]\bigr) \le \mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(X)\bigr].$$

The **Jensen gap** is that unpriced excess deficit:

$$\Delta\_J = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(X)\bigr] - \phi\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]\bigr) \ge 0.$$

When harm grows as a power law $\phi(x) \propto x^p$ ($p \ge 1$) under pure Pareto shocks of exponent $\alpha$, both expectations stay finite only if $\alpha > p$. For $1 < \alpha \le p$, mean exposure $\phi(\mathbb{E}[X])$ looks comfortably finite while true expected harm $\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]$ explodes to $+\infty$. Budgets sized at $\mathbb{E}[X]$ systematically underfund real damage—violating Georges Canguilhem's (1943/1966) vital tolerance latitude (formalized via survival-versus-recovery clocks in [Chapter 38](38-time-and-dependence.md)) and triggering Collapse Proposition 2 (*Impotent Lucidity Before Ruin*, [Chapter 41](41-the-triad-and-the-minimum.md)).

## 6. Underdetermination and competing risks bounds

When records miss failed paths or face competing hazards, even the distribution $F(x)$ itself becomes only partially identified. Suppose patients or machine parts face two competing failure causes ($1$ and $2$) with latent failure times $T\_1$ and $T\_2$ (distinct almost surely). The archive sees only the earlier failure time $T = \min(T\_1, T\_2)$ and winning cause $J \in \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$. Here, let $P\_1^\ast(t) = \mathbb{P}(T \le t, J = 1)$ and $P\_2^\ast(t) = \mathbb{P}(T \le t, J = 2)$ denote observed **crude cumulative incidences** for causes 1 and 2 (unrelated to the mediation planes of Ch 29), leaving overall observed survival $S\_T(t) = 1 - P\_1^\ast(t) - P\_2^\ast(t)$.

What the archive *cannot* identify without untestable assumptions is each cause's isolated **net survival** $S\_j(t) = \mathbb{P}(T\_j > t)$ ($j \in \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace$). Arthur V. Peterson Jr. (1976) showed that observed data trap each latent survival curve strictly between two sharp bounds:

$$S\_T(t) \le S\_j(t) \le 1 - P\_j^\ast(t) \qquad (j \in \lbrace 1, 2 \rbrace).$$

Conversely, given latent net curves $S\_1(t)$ and $S\_2(t)$ without pretending both hazards are independent ($S\_1(t) S\_2(t)$), Peterson (1976) applied the **Fréchet–Hoeffding copula inequalities** to bound overall survival $S\_T(t)$:

$$\max\bigl(0,\mkern3mu S\_1(t) + S\_2(t) - 1\bigr) \le S\_T(t) \le \min\bigl(S\_1(t),\mkern3mu S\_2(t)\bigr).$$

Multiplying $S\_1(t) S\_2(t)$ silently assumes independence where data yield only an interval ([Chapter 44, *Underdetermination, Partial Identification, and Severe Testing*](44-underdetermination-partial-identification.md)). Worse, whenever a competing cause kills a positive share of units ($P\_k^\ast(\infty) > 0$ for $k \ne j$), Peterson's upper bound $1 - P\_j^\ast(t)$ never falls to zero as $t \to \infty$—leaving unidentified probability mass at infinity and making the sharp identified upper bound $\sup \mathbb{E}[T\_j^p] = +\infty$ ($p > 0$) infinite without extra dependence assumptions.

### Which moments exist

Real loss archives illustrate how sensitive tail inference is to single shocks and cutoffs. On the 2,156 Danish fire-insurance claims above 1 million DKK (1980–1990, 1985 prices), McNeil (1997) fitted GPD models across thresholds $u \in \lbrace 3, 4, 5, 10, 20\rbrace$ million DKK. Across four thresholds, the shape estimate stayed above the infinite-variance boundary ($\hat{\xi} \in [0.63, 0.72]$), with overall range $\hat{\xi} \in [0.50, 0.72]$ and full-series estimate $\hat{\xi} = 0.60$ (standard error $0.04$). Right at $u = 10$, the estimate sat on $\hat{\xi} = 0.50$ (standard error $0.14$), and dropping the single 263-million-DKK fire dropped $\hat{\xi}$ to $0.39$ (standard error $0.13$; McNeil 1997). Concluding that variance is infinite ($\alpha < 2$) thus relies on lower thresholds $u \in \lbrace 3, 4, 5\rbrace$ and the full series rather than one cutoff. Moments belong to physical mechanisms; larger samples tighten bands without manufacturing moments that do not exist.

## 7. The index at the cut, the cap and the retention

Estimated tail indices shift automatically whenever analysts move estimator cutoffs $k$, insurers impose payout caps $K$ (distinct from Hill cutoff $k$), or contracts apply deductible floors $\delta$ and retentions $r$. Separating contract arithmetic from physical tail decay stops accounting limits from disguising real risk.

### Two cuts solved by hand

Hill's estimator is a log-ratio whose endpoints check by hand. At $k = 1$ (with $X\_{(n)} > X\_{(n-1)} > 0$), $\hat{\alpha}\_1 = 1/\ln(X\_{(n)}/X\_{(n-1)})$ uses only the top two records; at $k = n - 1$, $\hat{\alpha}\_{n-1} = (n - 1) / \sum\_{i=2}^{n} \ln(X\_{(i)}/X\_{(1)})$ spans the whole ordered sample. Both remain unit-free under currency rescaling, so differing treaty indices trace back to cutoffs or shifts until proven otherwise. Honest diagnostics publish the full Hill curve and band over a pre-registered grid of $k$.

A second cutoff governs Taylor budget approximations whenever variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$ holds ([Chapter 12](12-derivatives-and-convexity.md)). Expanding smooth loss $\phi$ around mean $\mu$ gives $\phi(\mu+\epsilon) = \phi(\mu) + \phi'(\mu)\epsilon + \frac{1}{2} \phi''(\mu)\epsilon^2 + O(\epsilon^3)$, which approximates the Jensen gap by $\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)] - \phi(\mu) \approx \frac{1}{2} \phi''(\mu)\sigma^2$. This quadratic adjustment works only when $\alpha > 2$ (finite variance) and skewness stays tame: third moments diverge across $2 < \alpha \le 3$, and for $\alpha > 3$ with $\phi'''(\mu) \ne 0$, cubic distortion swamps the quadratic estimate once $|\gamma\_1|\sigma > 3|\phi''(\mu)|/|\phi'''(\mu)|$.

### The cap that makes the suggested reserve wrong

A contractual policy limit $K$ caps only the **payer's liability** $g(X) := X \wedge K = \min(X, K)$ per event, leaving the **physical tail index** $\alpha$ of $X$ unchanged. Below $K$, funding $\phi(\mathbb{E}[X \wedge K])$ still under-reserves convex damage because curvature persists ($\mathbb{E}[\phi(X \wedge K)] \ge \phi(\mathbb{E}[X \wedge K])$). Treating $K$ as physical safety ignores sub-cap convexity and exports uncapped excess $(X - K)\_+$ onto unconsenting external reception perimeters; payers must fund $\mathbb{E}[\phi(g(X))] = \mathbb{E}[\phi(X \wedge K)]$ directly (or the corresponding declared retained-payout map $(X - r)\_+ \wedge K$ with retention $r \ge 0$), while governance audits $(X - K)\_+$ ex post rather than as scalar targets.

### The archive is left-truncated, so the index is conditional

Recording thresholds create fake tail shifts when frames mix ground-up losses with deductible excesses. McNeil's (1997) Danish series records total event losses above 1 million DKK, not net payouts above a deductible. Under clean left-truncation ($X \mid X > \delta$ with $\mathbb{P}(X > \delta) > 0$), regular variation preserves $\alpha$ identically: for $x > \delta$, $\mathbb{P}(X > x \mid X > \delta) = \mathbb{P}(X > x)/\mathbb{P}(X > \delta) \sim c\_\delta L(x)x^{-\alpha}$ (with $c\_\delta = 1/\mathbb{P}(X > \delta)$), leaving $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ bit-for-bit identical whenever $X\_{(n-k)} > \delta$.

Apparent shifts across treaties stem instead from three concrete artifacts: applying non-shift-invariant Hill ratios to net excesses $Y = X - r$ above deductible $r > 0$ rather than shift-stable GPD excess fits; shifting post-retention sample counts $n\_r$ (and quantile fraction $k/n\_r$) along pre-asymptotic $L(x)$ (§3.3); or right-capping losses under limits $X \wedge K$. Policy counts reflect exposure frequency, and caps censor right tails without fixing left-truncation; sound practice pre-registers floor $\delta$, cap $K$, and grid $k$ offline, fits ground-up losses or GPD excesses, and bans Hill comparisons across mismatched cuts.
