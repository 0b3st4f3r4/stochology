## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Distinguish an ensemble average across parallel copies from a time average along one path, and state when they coincide.
2. Explain why positive expected return can still end in absorbing ruin under Itô variance drain or excess leverage.
3. Show why queue delay grows hyperbolically near saturation and test whether survival time clears recovery latency across a vital basin.

## 1. Two averages and the breakdown of ergodicity

In [Chapter 23](23-statistical-physics-and-time.md), statistical physics drew a bright line between two averages. The **ensemble average** averages across many parallel, non-interacting copies at one moment. The **time average** tracks one single system across its own history. Both averages converge together across integrable observables if and only if the process is **ergodic**.

Real institutions, clinics, and grids are **non-ergodic** whenever compounding, absorbing ruin barriers, or regime shifts break invariance. One hospital has only one balance sheet, one runway, and one path through time. As Ole Peters (2019) notes, treating ensemble expectations as safe assumes physical risk pooling and sharing across many independent entities. Even a model cleanly validated in [Chapter 37](37-model-fit-and-error.md) fails once a path crosses an absorbing floor or jumps regime.

Picture two hospital treasurers facing identical return sequences with the same positive arithmetic average return. One levers up for the ensemble expectation across imaginary parallel worlds. An early liquidity freeze triggers a forced margin call at the trough, wiping her hospital out permanently. The second sizes her buffer for the single trajectory her hospital actually traverses, and survives.

Along a single history, **stationarity**—where all finite joint distributions stay time-invariant—differs sharply from fixed-step random walks whose variance drifts endlessly, and from **regime shifts**, where burstiness $c\_a^2$ or tail exponent $\alpha$ jump abruptly. Confusing a regime shift with calm noise turns long historical logs into traps.

## 2. Ruin as an absorbing state

Expected-value optimization breaks whenever dynamics include an **absorbing barrier**: a terminal floor like insolvency, organ failure, or grid meltdown with zero exit.

Suppose a compounding gamble pays generous positive average return across parallel worlds—including bankrupt worlds that stopped at zero. A real operator lives on just one trajectory, where hitting zero terminates compounding forever. Expected value routinely endorses gambles that drive almost every single trajectory to ruin. Under irreversible time, rational survival aims not to maximize expected wealth, but to **maximize survival probability** by holding an unbroken buffer away from ruin.

Absorbing barriers also warp hindsight through **survivorship bias**, when lethal hits disappear from returning records (Wald 1943; Mangel & Samaniego 1984; [Chapter 06](06-who-is-missing-from-the-table.md); [Chapter 47](47-irreversible-time-vital-margin.md)). The exact same truncation spoils follow-up archives: evaluating an intervention only on surviving firms or non-dropouts conditions on avoiding the barrier, answering a completely different question than the intervention asked.

## 3. Itô calculus and the variance drain

Why does volatility eat away single-path growth even when average return looks positive? Simple arithmetic shows why: a $+50\\%$ gain followed by a $-50\\%$ loss averages $0\\%$, yet leaves you at $1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75$ of your starting stake. In continuous compounding, volatility steals half its variance from every realized trajectory—exactly the variance drain derived in [Chapter 11](11-sequences-limits-continuity.md).

Let compounding capital $X\_t$ ($X\_0 > 0$) evolve under geometric Brownian motion with drift $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$, volatility $\sigma > 0$, and standard Brownian motion $W\_t$:

$$dX\_t = \mu X\_t\mkern3mu dt + \sigma X\_t\mkern3mu dW\_t.$$

Applying Itô's lemma to $\ln X\_t$ isolates the logarithmic growth rate:

$$d(\ln X\_t) = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) dt + \sigma\mkern3mu dW\_t.$$

Using $\lim\_{t \to \infty} W\_t / t = 0$ almost surely ($t > 0$, $X\_0 > 0$), the almost-sure time-average growth rate along one path is:

$$g = \lim\_{t \to \infty} \frac{1}{t} \ln\left(\frac{X\_t}{X\_0}\right) = \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}.$$

The penalty $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ is the **Itô–Kelly variance drain** (Itô 1951; Kelly 1956; Merton 1969; Peters 2019). Read aloud, expected wealth $\mathbb{E}[X\_t] = X\_0 e^{\mu t}$ grows at rate $\mu$, whereas any single path grows at $\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$. Note how this multiplicative drain differs from additive stock depletion $dR\_t = (u\_t - \dot{D}\_t)\,dt$ hitting $R\_t \le 0$ ([Chapter 23](23-statistical-physics-and-time.md); [Appendix A](appendix-a-mathematics.md) §A.2): $X\_t > 0$ a.s. at finite times, yet whenever $0 < 2\mu < \sigma^2$, $g < 0$ drives $X\_t \to 0$ a.s.—crossing any positive floor $b \in (0, X\_0)$ in finite time even while $\mathbb{E}[X\_t] \to +\infty$ (and for $\mu \le 0 < \sigma$, $g < 0$ directly).

Decision theorists carefully separate that physical boundary from a universal preference axiom. Peters (2019) advances time-average growth as a descriptive physical baseline rather than an exhaustive psychology of choice. Over finite horizons $T > 0$, Hulme et al. (2023, pp. 339, 343) prove that growth-rate variance decays as $1/T$ while terminal-wealth variance explodes exponentially. Ergodicity economics matches expected utility only when utility $u$ equals the linearizing growth map $f$ (Hulme et al. 2023, p. 339); Hulme et al. (2023, eq. 9, p. 343) construct a single-period linear-utility bet yielding expected payout $1.05\mkern3mu x(0)$ from initial wealth $x(0)$ despite negative log growth.

Earlier, Paul A. Samuelson (1971, eqs. 4, 20, pp. 2493–2496) proved that asymptotic geometric-mean rules exhibit both over-riskiness and under-riskiness across finite horizons under bounded or isoelastic ($x^\gamma/\gamma$) utility and fail under dependence ("Pandora's Box"). Matthew C. Ford and John A. Kay (2023, pp. 316, 321, 326) add that reducing choice to asymptotic growth breaks the completeness axiom and ignores dynamic multi-period utility. In their rejoinder, Ford and Kay (2024, pp. 72–73) correct a terminal-convergence misstatement while keeping Latané's criterion: at any finite $t > 0$, wealth $w\_t$ keeps positive chance of exceeding start wealth $w\_0$, $P(w\_t > w\_0) > 0$. The synthesis is clear: variance drain diagnoses structural ruin along unpooled trajectories without reducing finite-horizon ethics to log-wealth maximization.

Moreover, continuous diffusion assumes prices move smoothly enough to trade out in time. Benoît B. Mandelbrot (1963, pp. 404–406, 417) proved cotton prices follow infinite-variance Pareto laws ($\alpha = 1.7$) whose paths are "almost surely almost everywhere discontinuous," leaping past stop-loss orders. Likewise, autocorrelation early warnings (Scheffer et al. 2009) presuppose slow diffusive drift and miss sudden Lévy jumps breaching ecological or operational resilience basins, demanding pre-sized physical reserves ([Chapter 47](47-irreversible-time-vital-margin.md)).

Extending John Kelly's (1956) log-optimal fraction to diffusion around hurdle rate $r \in \mathbb{R}$ ($\sigma > 0$) yields Merton's (1969) growth curve $g(f) = r + f(\mu - r) - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f^2$. For $\mu > r$, optimal leverage is $f^\ast = (\mu - r)/\sigma^2 > 0$ ($\mu/\sigma^2$ at $r = 0$). Doubling leverage past $2f^\ast$ cuts growth below $r$, and at $r = 0$ crossing the **ruin boundary** $f\_{\mathrm{ruin}} = 2f^\ast$ ensures long-run decay (beyond $f^\ast + \sqrt{(f^\ast)^2 + 2r/\sigma^2}$ for general hurdles with positive peak growth $g(f^\ast) > 0$, i.e., $(f^\ast)^2 + 2r/\sigma^2 > 0$).

When drift $\hat{\mu} > r$ is estimated from finite variance-effective sample $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}} > 0$ (known $\sigma > 0$), **epistemic Kelly shrinkage** (motivated by Baker & McHale 2013) scales plug-in leverage $\hat{f}^\ast\_{\mathrm{plug-in}} = (\hat{\mu} - r)/\sigma^2$ by $k(n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}) = n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}/(n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}} + 1) \in (0, 1)$. Once the left-tail Hill exponent $\hat{\alpha}\_k$ (Hill 1975) approaches or enters infinite variance ($\alpha \le 2$), unknown-variance scaling and heavy tails defer to [Chapter 46](46-non-identical-asymmetry-heavy-tails.md) and [Chapter 47](47-irreversible-time-vital-margin.md). Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) in August–September 1998—leveraged on convergence trades (Lowenstein 2000)—showed why finite horizons bite: Russia's default triggered margin hikes and funding spirals that erased equity weeks before spreads converged (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009).

## 4. Congestion and the Kingman hyperbola

In [Chapter 26](26-regulation-and-homeostasis.md), we saw why running any service system near $100\\%$ utilization turns minor arrival spikes into endless gridlock. Queueing theory makes that explosion exact.

In 1961, John Kingman showed that in a single-server renewal queue ($GI/G/1$, i.i.d. arrivals and service) with arrival rate $\lambda > 0$, service rate $\mu > 0$ (distinct from §3's drift), utilization $\rho = \lambda/\mu \in (0, 1)$, and inter-arrival and service variability $0 \le c\_a, c\_s < \infty$, expected waiting time $\mathbb{E}[W\_q]$ scales as:

$$\mathbb{E}[W\_q] \approx \left(\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}\right) \left(\frac{c\_a^2 + c\_s^2}{2}\right) \frac{1}{\mu}.$$

(Notice finite second moments are essential: $\alpha \le 2$ tails blow up $c\_a^2$ or $c\_s^2$, and correlated arrivals break renewal assumptions.)

```
Expected
Waiting
Time E[Wq]
  ^
  |                                        *
  |                                       *
  |                                      *   <- Hyperbolic Divergence:
  |                                     *       rho / (1 - rho) -> inf as rho -> 1^-
  |                                    *
  |                                  *
  |                           * * *
  |                 * * * * *
  +---------------------------------------------> Utilization rho
  0                                             1
```

Read aloud with slack $s := 1 - \rho \in (0, 1)$, the **Kingman hyperbola** $\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} = \frac{1}{s} - 1$ grows like $1/s$ as $\rho \to 1^-$. Cutting slack by factor $k > 1$ ($s\_2 = s\_1/k$) multiplies delay by more than $k$, marginal sensitivity $(1-\rho)^{-2}$ grows quadratically, and burstiness $\frac{1}{2}(c\_a^2 + c\_s^2)$ scales every wait. Running hospitals or compute clusters near $\rho \to 1^-$ builds gridlock into normal operation.

A power grid or clinic crosses both regimes across a day:

1. **Moderate-slack regime ($\rho \in (0, 1/2)$):** With positive variability ($c\_a^2 + c\_s^2 > 0$), $\rho/(1-\rho) < 1$ (reaching $1$ at $\rho = 1/2$), keeping heavy-traffic delay below $\frac{c\_a^2 + c\_s^2}{2\mu}$ (and $0$ in deterministic $D/D/1$). Outside Poisson arrivals ($M/G/1, c\_a^2 = 1$), bursty arrivals can still exceed the heavy-traffic curve at low load, bounded by Kingman's (1962) distribution-free ceiling $\mathbb{E}[W\_q] \le \bigl(\frac{c\_a^2/\rho^2 + c\_s^2}{2}\bigr)\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}\frac{1}{\mu}$.
2. **Near-saturation regime ($\rho \to 1^-$):** Shrinking slack $1 - \rho \to 0^+$ makes waits explode to multiples of service time.

## 5. Vital normativity, resilience basins, and the master survival inequality

In *The Normal and the Pathological*, Georges Canguilhem defined biological health through **vital normativity** (Canguilhem 1943/1966): health is not rigid adherence to a static average—which snaps under environmental shifts (*infidélité du milieu*)—but capacity to absorb shocks and institute new norms. Andean Aymara *Qhipnayra* pairs that attitude with time: placing the inspected past *in front of the eyes* and the unlived future at one's back (Núñez & Sweetser 2006). Ecologically, C. S. Holling (1973) separated local **engineering resilience** (return rate $\lambda\_{\mathrm{eng}}$ near an equilibrium) from **ecological resilience**: **basin width** $D\_t = x\_{\mathrm{high}}^\ast - x\_{\mathrm{tip}}^\ast$ ($D\_t / D\_0$, $D\_0 > 0$) before tipping into collapse.

To test whether an institution can actually survive a crisis before tipping, [Chapter 26](26-regulation-and-homeostasis.md) compared how long reserves last against how long recovery takes. Formalize that comparison as the **Master Survival Inequality**. Let **Time to Survive** be $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} = B\_t / \dot{D}\_{\mathrm{crisis}}$ on unencumbered physical buffer $B\_t \ge 0$ under crisis drain $\dot{D}\_{\mathrm{crisis}} > 0$ (where coupled shocks can impair fraction $c\_{\mathrm{imp}} \in [0, 1)$, leaving $(1 - c\_{\mathrm{imp}})B\_t/\dot{D}\_{\mathrm{crisis}}$ in [Chapter 47](47-irreversible-time-vital-margin.md)). Let **Effective Time to Recover** at sealed state $\mathcal{I}\_t$ ($\mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{recovery}} := \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{I}\_t)$) sum three clocks: $\mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{I}\_t) = \tau\_{\mathrm{detect}}(\delta\_{\mathrm{status}}) + \tau\_{\mathrm{delib}}(\mathcal{I}\_t) + \tau\_{\mathrm{actuate}}$—detection (stretched by testimonial rank discounting $\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} \in [0, 1)$), deliberation (capped by live cognitive bandwidth, [Chapter 36](36-decision-loss-threshold.md)), and actuation (inflated by Kingman wait $\mathbb{E}[W\_q]$). Survival demands:

$$\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} \ge \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{I}\_t).$$

Read aloud in common units of time, your uncommitted reserve must outlast detection, decision, and execution combined. Violating this condition anchors **Collapse Proposition 2** (*Impotent Lucidity*, [Chapter 41](41-the-triad-and-the-minimum.md)): knowing disaster equations cannot save a system whose recovery arrives after ruin (while upper reserve caps curb idle hoarding in [Chapter 47](47-irreversible-time-vital-margin.md) and [Chapter 50](50-methodological-families-reflexivity.md)).

Recall that clustered shocks ($\mathrm{DEFF}\_{\mathrm{tail}} > 1$) distort tail chances ([Chapter 46](46-non-identical-asymmetry-heavy-tails.md)) and autocorrelation early warnings (Scheffer et al. 2009) miss sudden jumps across basin width $D\_t$. Two regimes govern audits: if $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} < \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{I}\_t)$, survival margin is negative so buffers drain before help arrives even when basin ratio $D\_t / D\_0$ looks intact; in buffered or short-window regimes where worst-case impaired drawdown strictly clears the horizon, absorption cannot happen inside that window.

### The hyperbola is not a line

Near capacity ($\rho \to 1^-$), queue delay explodes and flips survival margin negative; at moderate load ($\rho \in (0, 1/2)$, $c\_a^2 + c\_s^2 > 0$), $\rho/(1-\rho) < 1$ bounded by Kingman's (1962) exact $GI/G/1$ limit. No linear trade-off bridges them because a hyperbola is not a line.

Dependence enters the same balance: variance drain ($\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ at unit leverage) separates ensemble expectations from single-path survival regardless of sample length. An overloaded queue and a volatile compounded path send one warning: the average describes an imaginary parallel universe, not the trajectory your institution must live through.

### When absorption is impossible

Non-ergodicity stops binding only when three conditions hold jointly across horizon $T > 0$: worst-case impaired survival time clears $T$ ($\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} > T$), payoffs over unpooled stakes stay linear (concave curvature restores risk aversion; Samuelson 1971; Hulme et al. 2023), and zero irreversible ruin spills onto external reception perimeters' capacity ([Chapter 29](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md); [Chapter 41](41-the-triad-and-the-minimum.md); [Appendix A.7](appendix-a-mathematics.md)). Test absorption, payoff linearity, and non-spillover first; use ensemble averages only when all three pass, keeping the single-path rule whenever any fails.
