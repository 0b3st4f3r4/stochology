## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Distinguish an average over many parallel worlds from an average over one historical path.
2. Explain why a positive expected return can still produce a ruined trajectory.
3. Say what a queue does as utilisation approaches its ceiling, and why the delay is not linear.

## 1. Two averages and the breakdown of ergodicity

The distinction between statistical averages comes from thermodynamics. The **ensemble average** averages a property across infinitely many non-interacting parallel systems at a single instant. The **time average** follows one system as it evolves forward through historical time. The two averages coincide if and only if the underlying stochastic process is **ergodic**.

The systems that management, engineering and epidemiology govern are **strictly non-ergodic**. An institution does not hold a thousand parallel copies among which it can distribute its losses. It has one runway, one electrical grid, one balance sheet, one historical path. The state it occupies at timestamp $t+1$ is physically dependent upon the sequence of states it traversed to arrive there, so planning on ensemble averages is an invitation to sudden ruin.

Consider two regional hospital endowment treasurers who allocate operating reserves across the same historical return sequence. Both read the same positive arithmetic mean return on their prospectus. The first treasurer sizes her leverage to maximize the ensemble expectation across hypothetical parallel markets; when a multi-week liquidity freeze strikes in year seven, a margin call forces liquidation at the bottom, locking her hospital out of the subsequent twenty-three years of recovery. The second treasurer sizes her buffer for the single historical trajectory her institution must physically traverse and survives all thirty years.

Even within a single path, an operator must distinguish **stationarity** (where the transition law $P(X\_{t+1} \mid X\_t)$ and serial autocorrelation structure stay constant over time) from a **regime shift** (where structural parameters such as arrival burstiness $c\_a^2$ or tail thickness $\alpha$ jump abruptly across epochs) (Peters 2019). Confusing a temporary stationary fluctuation with a permanent regime shift (or assuming stationary ergodicity when an absorbing boundary sits inside the decision horizon) turns a long historical archive into a trap.

## 2. Ruin as an absorbing state

Classical expected-value optimization fails once a stochastic dynamic contains an **absorbing barrier**: a boundary state from which no subsequent transition is possible, such as biological death, insolvency or a physical core meltdown.

Consider a multiplicative gambling game or financial portfolio with a positive expected return across parallel
universes. The expectation is an ensemble calculation: it sums the payoffs across all hypothetical paths,
including those that struck the zero barrier and terminated. A real-world operator does not experience the
ensemble but the time average of a single realization, and the moment a path strikes zero, multiplicative growth
ends permanently.

Expected value optimization routinely prescribes strategies that assign a positive expected payoff to trajectories
that possess an absorbing probability of unity. The rational objective for an entity traversing irreversible
time is not the maximization of expected wealth, but the **maximization of long-term survival probability**,
prioritizing the maintenance of an unbroken buffer between current operations and the absorbing ruin barrier.

Absorbing barriers corrupt not only forward planning but also backward observation, through **survivorship bias**. In 1943 Abraham Wald, working in the Statistical Research Group at Columbia, wrote memoranda on how to estimate the vulnerability of aircraft from the damage seen on those that returned (Wald 1943). The telling that made the work famous, returning bombers with holes concentrated on fuselage and wings and almost none on the engines, and a command inclined to armour the riddled parts, is a later gloss on a document that is mathematical throughout (Mangel & Samaniego 1984), but it states the inference correctly: missing bombers were absorbed at the barrier of combat loss precisely because hits to the engine and cockpit had far higher conditional lethality, $q\_B = \mathbb{P}(\text{Lost} \mid \text{Hit}\_B) \gg q\_A$. Solving the latent $2 \times 2$ sortie system reconstructs the unobserved hits on downed aircraft via $\hat{M}\_z = S\_z \frac{q\_z}{1 - q\_z}$ from the surviving hit counts $S\_z$, and the reconstruction inverts the visible ranking whenever the observed survivor hit rates reverse the true vulnerability.

The same truncation distorts socioeconomic archives. In the National Supported Work randomized trial that Robert LaLonde (1986) analysed and Rajeev Dehejia and Sadek Wahba (1999) later reworked (LaLonde 1986; Dehejia & Wahba 1999), the unconditional experimental average treatment effect on 1978 earnings is positive; conditioning the evaluation solely on the surviving sub-cohort with positive post-program earnings shrinks the apparent effect toward zero and can erase it entirely, by censoring the absorbed zero-earners. Conditioning on employment is conditioning on not having hit the barrier, and the treatment effect measured inside that condition is a different quantity from the one the experiment was run to estimate.

## 3. Itô calculus and the variance drain

In continuous-time compounding processes, the divergence between ensemble drift and time-average growth is
governed by Itô's lemma. Consider an asset or resource reserve $S\_t$ following geometric Brownian motion:

$$dS\_t = \mu S\_t\mkern3mu dt + \sigma S\_t\mkern3mu dW\_t.$$

Applying Itô's lemma to the logarithmic trajectory $X\_t = \ln S\_t$ yields:

$$d(\ln S\_t) = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) dt + \sigma\mkern3mu dW\_t.$$

Integrating over time and invoking the strong law of large numbers for Brownian motion shows that the
almost-sure time-average growth rate is:

$$g = \lim\_{t \to \infty} \frac{1}{t} \ln\left(\frac{S\_t}{S\_0}\right) = \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}.$$

The term $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ is the **Itô–Kelly variance drain**. Itô's 1951 stochastic differential lemma supplies it, and Kelly 1956, Robert C. Merton 1969 and Peters 2019 establish it as the continuous-time geometric growth-rate corollary (Itô 1951; Kelly 1956; Merton 1969; Peters 2019). While the ensemble expectation $\mathbb{E}[S\_t] = S\_0 e^{\mu t}$
grows at rate $\mu$, any individual sample path grows at the substantially lower rate $\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$.
If volatility is sufficiently elevated ($\sigma^2 > 2\mu$), the system suffers negative growth ($g < 0$) and
collapses to zero with probability 1, even while its arithmetic expectation diverges to positive infinity.

In capital allocation, John Kelly (1956) showed that when leveraging a bet with fraction $f$, growth follows
$g(f) = \mu f - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f^2$. The optimal growth fraction is $f^\ast = \mu/\sigma^2$. Beyond the
**ruin boundary** $f\_{\mathrm{ruin}} = 2f^\ast$, the variance drain overwhelms drift, guaranteeing long-run extinction.
And in real applications where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are estimated with sampling noise, the **Baker-McHale
Kelly shrinkage** mandates scaling $f^\ast$ downward, because parameter uncertainty inflates the risk of over-betting
into the absorbing zone.

A century-long equity archive makes the arithmetic visible. The arithmetic drift of the ensemble sits above the realized time-average logarithmic growth rate of the single path, and the difference between them is the Itô variance drain, consuming a fixed share of the ensemble drift every day and compounding over the whole archive to a cumulative logarithmic divergence that no terminal wealth comparison can erase.

When leverage is sized under a finite sampling-variance effective sample size $n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}$, Baker–McHale shrinkage scales the plug-in fraction $f^\ast = (\hat{\mu} - r)/\hat{\sigma}^2$ by $k(n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}) = n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}}/(n\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{var}} + 1)$ (Baker & McHale 2013), so the honest allocation is strictly below the plug-in one at every finite sample. A separate guard watches the Hill left-tail exponent $\hat{\alpha}\_k$: once the estimated exponent approaches the range in which the sample variance has no stable meaning, the denominator switches from the sample variance to a Wasserstein-robust $\mathrm{CVaR}\_{95}$ tail scale, because a moment that does not exist cannot be estimated and the plug-in fraction built on it is a number without a referent. The historical counterexample is Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) in August–September 1998 (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009): holding convergence trades with positive ensemble expected return at leverage beyond twice the Kelly fraction, a Russian sovereign default triggered a correlated liquidity spiral that absorbed the fund's capital buffer weeks before prices converged.

## 4. Congestion and the Kingman hyperbola

Queuing systems carry the same failure mode as the absorbing barrier, in time. As utilization approaches capacity, delay grows hyperbolically, not linearly.

In 1961, John Kingman proved that for a general single-server queue ($G/G/1$), the expected waiting time in
queue $\mathbb{E}[W\_q]$ under heavy traffic satisfies:

$$\mathbb{E}[W\_q] \approx \left(\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}\right) \left(\frac{c\_a^2 + c\_s^2}{2}\right) \frac{1}{\mu},$$

where $\rho = \lambda/\mu$ is capacity utilization, $c\_a$ is the coefficient of variation of inter-arrival times,
and $c\_s$ is the coefficient of variation of service times.

```
Expected
Waiting
Time E[Wq]
  ^
  |                                        *
  |                                       *
  |                                      *   <- Hyperbolic Divergence:
  |                                     *       rho / (1 - rho)
  |                                    *
  |                                  *
  |                           * * *
  |                 * * * * *
  +---------------------------------------------> Utilization rho
  0               0.5               0.8   0.95  1.0
```

The term $\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}$ represents the **Kingman hyperbola**. Sizing an emergency department, a highway,
or a compute cluster at $95\unicode{x25}$ utilization ($\rho = 0.95$) multiplies waiting time by a factor of
$0.95 / 0.05 = 19$. Sizing at $99\unicode{x25}$ multiplies it by $99$. And variability enters multiplicatively:
bursty arrivals ($c\_a \gg 1$) amplify congestion delay across all utilization levels. Operating a critical system
near $100\unicode{x25}$ efficiency engineers catastrophic gridlock at the arrival of the first cluster of shocks, which is the
opposite of lean optimization.

An electricity grid runs both regimes over a single day (Kingman 1961).

1. **Moderate-slack regime:** In normal off-peak operation the Kingman utilization multiplier $\rho/(1-\rho)$ stays below one, which holds exactly while utilization remains under one half — one half being the load at which the multiplier equals one, keeping heavy-traffic expected wait below the dispersion-scaled unit $\frac{c\_a^2 + c\_s^2}{2\mu}$ (one service interval in the $c\_a^2 + c\_s^2 = 2$ benchmark, though bursty arrivals $c\_a \gg 1$ inflate waits at any load and require the finite-load ceiling of [Chapter 52](52-layer-meso-buffered-queue-order.md)).
2. **Near-saturation heatwave regime:** When air-conditioning load drives grid utilization close to capacity, shrinking the slack $1 - \rho \to 0^+$ makes the hyperbolic multiplier $\rho/(1-\rho)$ diverge without bound, stretching the expected wait to many times the dispersion-scaled service interval. A small absolute shift in utilization near one collapses an entire remaining headroom fraction and amplifies delay nonlinearly.

## 5. Vital normativity, resilience basins, and the master survival inequality

In *The Normal and the Pathological*, Georges Canguilhem established that biological life is defined by **vital
normativity**: health is not conformity to a statistical average but the organic capacity to tolerate
environmental perturbations and establish new functional norms. In ecology, C. S. Holling formalized
this as the width of the **resilience basin**: the maximum displacement a system can endure without flipping into
an alternate, degraded regime.

One comparison joins them, the **Master Survival Inequality**. Let $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}}$ be the **Time to Survive**: the duration an institution can keep core operations running on its existing physical, vital and financial reserves under emergency depletion.
Let $\mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{recovery}}$ be the **Time to Recover**, the latency required to detect the failure,
mobilize replacement supply, clear queuing congestion, and restore equilibrium. A system facing disruption survives only if this necessary physical survival condition holds:

$$\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} \ge \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{recovery}}.$$

Both sides of the inequality are measured in identical physical units (seconds, hours, or days), making the
condition auditable in advance: a reserve that satisfies it holds enough slack to withstand the Kingman
congestion wave, overcome the Itô variance drain, and avoid absorption at the ruin barrier.

The Master Survival Inequality is evaluated alongside a basin-rupture audit, which propagates cluster tail design effects ($\mathrm{DEFF}\_{\mathrm{tail}}$) through the convex Pareto basin-rupture mapping $\hat{P}\_{\mathrm{rup}}(\hat{\alpha}\_k) = (x\_m / D\_t)^{\hat{\alpha}\_k}$ so that second-order Jensen optimism is removed before sizing reserves (Canguilhem 1943/1966; Holling 1973). And for stopping-time claims over sequential trials, Abraham Wald's identity $\mathbb{E}[S\_N] = \mathbb{E}[N]\mkern3mu\mathbb{E}[X\_1]$ holds only when stopping time $N$ has finite expectation and steps are integrable; under heavy-tailed congestion or endogenous reserve exhaustion, naive optional stopping invalidates point-mean recovery estimates (Wald 1945). Two boundary regimes govern the survival audit: when the time to survive falls short of the time to recover, the survival deficit is negative and the Holling basin ratio collapses below the point at which the system can hold its operating regime, guaranteeing shedding before backup generation can synchronize. Conversely, in a buffered off-peak regime the time to survive exceeds the time to recover and the basin ratio stays high, and in a benign short-horizon regime where worst-case depletion time exceeds the entire operational window ($\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} > T$), absorption cannot occur inside the horizon at all.

### The hyperbola is not a line

At near-saturation the expected wait is many multiples of the dispersion-scaled service interval and the basin ratio has fallen below the survival mark. The system cannot survive: a survival horizon far shorter than the recovery latency computed on the same archive. Under unit dispersion ($c\_a^2 + c\_s^2 = 2$), the same queue at moderate slack ($\rho < 1/2$) keeps its heavy-traffic wait below a single service interval, whereas higher arrival burstiness multiplies it throughout. Nothing in between is stable, because the hyperbola is not a line.

Dependence enters the same ledger through a different door. The variance drain on a long return series is the difference between the drift across parallel worlds and the drift along one path, a fixed share of the ensemble growth over the same archive. The gap is not estimation error and a longer series does not close it.

The practical consequence is that a queue near saturation and a series with memory are the same warning in two costumes: the average describes a population the decision does not live in. What the decision lives in is the basin, and the basin shrinks faster than the average moves.

### When absorption is impossible

Non-ergodicity is irrelevant when absorption cannot occur inside the horizon. The procedure is a test of absorption first: if the worst survival time over the horizon exceeds the horizon itself, use the ensemble mean and record that the choice was made, and if it does not, keep the trajectory. The test is one line of arithmetic and it decides which of the two averages the whole chapter is allowed to use.

One line, one decision, and the ledger stops being ambiguous.
