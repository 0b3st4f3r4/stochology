## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Separate *what goes with what* from *what happens if we act*, and say which one a decision needs.
2. Read a causal claim and name the assumption that would make it false.
3. Ask a counterfactual question precisely enough that it has one answer instead of many.

## 1. Pearl's three-rung causal hierarchy

Causal queries are not all of the same mathematical species; they inhabit three strictly hierarchical levels,
formalized by Judea Pearl as the **Ladder of Causation**:

```
Level 3: COUNTERFACTUALS  P(y_x' | x, y)  "What if we had acted differently? Why did it happen?"
         ^
Level 2: INTERVENTIONS    P(y | do(x))    "What will happen if we take action x?"
         ^
Level 1: ASSOCIATIONS     P(y | x)        "What does observing symptom x tell us about y?"
```

1. **Rung 1 (Association)**: Queries about passive observation, conditional probabilities, and correlational
   patterns in the historical record. This is the exclusive domain of standard curve-fitting, regression, and
   supervised deep learning.
2. **Rung 2 (Intervention)**: Queries about active manipulation of the environment, denoted by Pearl's do-calculus
   $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$. An interventional distribution strips away the confounding arrows entering $X$,
   simulating the physical consequence of setting $X=x$ by external policy.
3. **Rung 3 (Counterfactuals)**: Queries about retrospective necessity and moral responsibility: *given that
   outcome $y$ actually occurred after action $x$, what would have happened if we had chosen $x'$ instead?*
   Counterfactuals cannot be observed directly in any prospective trial, because the individual case has already
   traversed history.

A policy decision is invariably a Rung 2 or Rung 3 query. Attempting to answer an interventional question
using Rung 1 associative data without structural identification assumptions is the most pervasive category
error in automated governance.

## 2. Invariant Causal Prediction and structural identification

An investigator can reach interventional invariance without a prohibitive randomized trial, if the data arrive under heterogeneous regimes. Classical methods rely on untestable conditional independence assumptions (backdoor adjustment) or on exclusion restrictions (instrumental variables).

Jonas Peters, Peter Bühlmann, and Nicolai Meinshausen (2016) formalized **Invariant Causal Prediction (ICP)**.
Suppose an institution collects observational telemetry across multiple distinct environments $e \in \mathcal{E}$
(differing operational sites, distinct policy regimes, or shifting seasonal conditions). Under arbitrary external interventions that do not perturb the target
variable's direct structural mechanism, the conditional distribution of target $Y$ given its true direct causal
parents $X\_{\mathrm{PA}}$ remains strictly **invariant across all environments**:

$$P(Y \mid X\_{\mathrm{PA}}, E = e\_1) = P(Y \mid X\_{\mathrm{PA}}, E = e\_2) \qquad \forall e\_1, e\_2 \in \mathcal{E}.$$

Spurious correlates and downstream symptoms break under environmental shifts: they appear predictive in regime $e\_1$
and invert in regime $e\_2$. The ICP algorithm tests candidate predictor subsets $S \subseteq X$ for invariance
across environments; the intersection of all statistically invariant subsets identifies the genuine causal parents.
If the intersection is empty, the procedure halts, demonstrating that
the candidate variables do not contain the complete causal mechanism.

## 3. Counterfactual responsibility: Tian-Pearl bounds

When a catastrophic failure occurs, legal and institutional accountability requires evaluating counterfactual
attribution: *did the deployment of this automated system cause the harm, or would the harm have occurred anyway?*

Pearl formalizes this through the **Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS)**: the probability that
taking action $x$ would produce outcome $y$, while withholding action ($x'$) would avert it:

$$\mathrm{PNS} = \mathbb{P}(Y\_x = 1, Y\_{x'} = 0).$$

Because potential outcomes $Y\_x$ and $Y\_{x'}$ are never simultaneously observable for the same unit, PNS cannot
be estimated as a point. From the experimental trial marginals $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$ and $P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x'))$ alone (writing $y$ for $Y = 1$ and $y'$ for $Y = 0$), the Fréchet–Hoeffding coupling bounds give

$$\max\bigl(0,\mkern3mu P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) - P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x'))\bigr) \le \mathrm{PNS} \le \min\bigl(P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)),\mkern3mu P(y' \mid \mathrm{do}(x'))\bigr).$$

In 2000, Jin Tian and Judea Pearl proved that synthesizing observational joint probabilities $P(x, y)$ (with marginal $P(y) = P(x, y) + P(x', y)$) with those experimental marginals tightens the sharp counterfactual bounds to (Tian & Pearl 2000):

$$\begin{aligned}
\max\Bigl\lbrace{}0,\mkern4mu P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) - P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x')),\mkern4mu P(y) - P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x')),\mkern4mu P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) - P(y)\Bigr\rbrace{} &\le \mathrm{PNS}, \\\\
\mathrm{PNS} &\le \min\Bigl\lbrace{}P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)),\mkern4mu P(y' \mid \mathrm{do}(x')),\mkern4mu P(x, y) + P(x', y'),\mkern4mu P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) - P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x')) + P(x, y') + P(x', y)\Bigr\rbrace{}.
\end{aligned}$$

These bounds define the
mathematical boundary of retrospective attribution.

## 4. Institutional design: the three pillars, Sosa AAA, and Fricker's injustice

Causal inference identifies what an intervention would produce; institutional governance determines who holds
the standing to decide and who must answer for the outcome. A functioning accountability framework rests upon
**Three Pillars of Institutional Design**:

1. **Observability**: Unfettered, un-sanitized sensory access to operational logs and physical telemetry.
2. **Authority**: Formal, legally binding jurisdiction to intervene, halt operations, or veto unsafe deployments.
3. **Consequence**: Symmetric liability; decision-makers must hold meaningful downside exposure if their policies
   inflict disaster.

Ernest Sosa's **AAA virtue epistemology** evaluates the quality of the decider:

- *Accuracy*: Did the decision produce a successful physical outcome?
- *Adroitness*: Was the choice grounded in rigorous causal inference and valid risk bounds?
- *Aptness*: Did the success occur *because* of the agent's adroitness, and not through moral luck?

When organizations fail, the failure is social and structural, not cognitive alone. In *Epistemic
Injustice*, Miranda Fricker conceptualized **testimonial injustice**: the structural discounting of a speaker's
credibility caused by institutional prejudice regarding status or identity. Frontline operators and junior
engineers frequently detect physical anomalies long before they appear on executive dashboards. When institutional
hierarchies impose a status-based credibility discount ($\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} > 0$), the warning signal is silenced,
truncating the organization's sensory capacity.
Stafford Beer's cybernetic **algedonic channel** exists to bypass this status filtering with zero status discount ($\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} = 0$), routing warnings straight to the executive veto holder under corroborated likelihood-ratio sequential filtering so that finite attention is reserved for signals above the noise floor.

## 5. Chouldechova's algorithmic fairness impossibility theorem

When automated decision algorithms are deployed across demographically diverse populations, institutions
frequently demand that the model be simultaneously "unbiased" and "fair". In 2017, Alexandra Chouldechova proved
that this demand is mathematically impossible.

Let $Y \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace$ be the true outcome, $\hat{Y} = \mathbf{1}\lbrace{}S > s^\ast\rbrace \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace$ a binary classification derived from risk score $S \in [0, 1]$ at threshold $s^\ast$ with positive prediction probability $0 < \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = a) < 1$, and $A \in \lbrace0, 1\rbrace$ a protected demographic attribute. Suppose the groups exhibit unequal non-trivial baseline prevalence rates:

$$\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid A = 1) \neq \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid A = 0), \qquad \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid A = a) \in (0, 1).$$

Chouldechova (2017, Eq. 2.5) proved that no binary classification $\hat{Y} = \mathbf{1}\lbrace{}S > s^\ast\rbrace$ can simultaneously satisfy:

1. **Predictive parity (equal positive predictive value at the threshold)**: $\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid S > s^\ast, A = 1) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid S > s^\ast, A = 0)$.
2. **False Positive Rate parity**: $\mathbb{P}(S > s^\ast \mid Y = 0, A = 1) = \mathbb{P}(S > s^\ast \mid Y = 0, A = 0)$.
3. **False Negative Rate parity**: $\mathbb{P}(S \le s^\ast \mid Y = 1, A = 1) = \mathbb{P}(S \le s^\ast \mid Y = 1, A = 0)$,

unless the classification rule at $s^\ast$ is error-free ($\mathrm{FPR}\_a = \mathrm{FNR}\_a = 0$, so $\mathrm{PPV}\_a = 1$; note that score-level calibration $\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid S = s, A = a) = s$ across a multi-level score does not by itself equalize thresholded $\mathrm{PPV}\_a = \mathbb{E}[S \mid S > s^\ast, A = a]$). If an institution enforces predictive parity together with equal false-negative rates across groups with differing base rates for an imperfect classifier, it is mathematically forced to generate unequal false-positive rates; more generally, enforcing predictive parity forces disparity in false-positive rates, false-negative rates, or both. Causal fairness cannot be engineered by tuning an algorithm; it requires restructuring the institutional loss matrix that assigns consequences to errors.

## 6. Why an attribution score is not an intervention

The most widely deployed explanations in applied machine learning (Shapley-value attributions such as SHAP (Lundberg & Lee 2017), local surrogate
models such as LIME (Ribeiro, Singh & Guestrin 2016), and partial-dependence plots) share a structure, and the structure determines what they can and cannot
support. Each of them decomposes the model's output into contributions from the model's own inputs, holding the
*model* fixed (Pearl 2009). That is a statement about the fitted function on the archive, and the archive is the
second plane, not the first.

Each of the three is a way of mistaking the archive for the world.

1. **An attribution is a rung-one quantity.** It answers "how does the fitted function respond when this covariate
   moves, with everything else in the model unchanged?" It does not answer "what happens to the outcome if we
   intervene on this covariate in the world?", because an intervention breaks the incoming edges to the variable; it does not re-weight a term in a regression. Those two questions have different answers whenever the
   covariates are correlated, the standard case. Take a confounded structural model in which a confounder $Z$ drives both the cause $X$, the outcome $Y$, and a spurious proxy $W$, while $X$ drives $Y$: the true cause has a unit interventional effect on the outcome and the proxy $W$ has identically zero interventional effect, yet because $W$ carries less measurement noise from the confounder, a regularized linear model assigns it a larger weight than the cause, and an exact interventional Shapley attribution faithfully mirrors that observational weight, inverting the causal ranking.
2. **The decomposition is conditional on the model being right where the explanation is read.** Attributions are
   computed at points the analyst chooses, usually the points the model already scores. A local surrogate
   fitted where the structural discrepancy $\delta(x)$ is large explains the *discrepancy*, faithfully and
   persuasively.
3. **Feature importance inherits the encoding.** Correlated features split their attribution arbitrarily, and
   monotone transforms change the units without changing the ranking, so a top-five list is not a causal ordering
   even when it is stable across seeds.

What restores a causal reading is the machinery of §2 and §3, and it is worth stating what it costs.
The Tian–Pearl bounds return *intervals*, because counterfactual probabilities are not identified
by observational data alone; the ICP machinery of §2 returns a *set* of predictors whose conditional
relationship survives across environments, a claim about invariance; magnitude is not what survives. Both are
weaker in presentation and stronger in warrant: an interval guaranteed to contain the intervention effect is
usable for a decision, while a point attribution that is not identified for one is a rhetorical object.

The institutional form of the rule follows from that difference. An explanation may be reported
to the person affected by a decision as an account of *how the model reasoned*, which is a claim on the second
plane. It may be reported as an account of *what would change the outcome*, which is a claim on the first plane,
only when the identification argument of §2–§3 has been made and its interval is attached. Collapse Proposition 3
is what happens when the first is dressed as the second: the appearance of causal transparency becomes the alibi
that forecloses the audit.

### How many environments

The invariant causal predictor is fitted on a chosen number of seasonal environments, and the number matters because the candidate set is what the invariance test has to separate. Fewer environments leave a wider accepted intersection; more leave only what the narrower test licenses, and the count of environments is therefore a design decision, not a finding. Reporting the accepted set without reporting how many environments it survived is reporting a claim whose warrant has been withheld.

The audit of a rule is separate from the rule. Reading a working flag as a classifier of the recorded outcome, and taking the false-positive rate as the share of the released who would have been flagged, gives one ratio between the groups; taking instead the share of each group carrying the flag gives another, close to the first but not equal to it. Both ratios are contrasts on the same records and differ only in the denominator, which is why a single published figure without its denominator cannot be audited. The rule was chosen for its aggregate accuracy and neither ratio is what the aggregate priced.

Counterfactuals are the third object, and the hardest: a bound on the average effect under monotone selection is not a prediction about any individual, and reporting it as one is the error that makes the interval look like an estimate.

### When the counterfactual is not identified

The thesis fails when the selection mechanism is not monotone and no instrument is available: the bound is then uninformative and the honest report is that the counterfactual is not identified. The procedure is to state which assumption would identify it, what it would cost to secure, and to decide on the bound's worst case in the meantime. The discipline is the same one the invariance test imposes: state what would identify the object, price it, and let the decision run on the conservative end while the price is unpaid.

The price is stated before the decision, not after the failure.

## 7. The two edges of the attribution interval, the remedy and the missing intervention

### Two degenerate arms

When only the experimental arms are available, the Fréchet–Hoeffding attribution interval of §3 is two lines of arithmetic once the arms are named: with $a = P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$ and
$b = P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x'))$ the region is $[\max(0,\mkern3mua-b),\mkern5mu \min(a,\mkern3mu1-b)]$. The interval collapses to a point when one of the arms is degenerate ($a = 0$, $a = 1$, $b = 0$, or $b = 1$): fixing $a = 1$ or $b = 0$ (no unexposed harm) forces $\mathrm{PNS} = a - b$, while $a = 0$ or $b = 1$ (certainty of harm in the unexposed arm) pins $\mathrm{PNS} = 0$. Every
remaining pair of arms leaves an interval, so the chapter's insistence on reporting it is necessity.

The margin between the arms does not pin down the interval either: at a fixed positive difference $d = a - b > 0$, the lower edge sits at $d$, while the upper edge $\min(a, 1 - b) = d + \min(b, 1 - a)$ rises from $d$ to its maximum $(1 + d)/2$ at intermediate risk ($a + b = 1$) and shrinks back toward $d$ when both arms sit near one. Reading institutional liability off an unidentifiable interval's midpoint — or off a summary difference $a - b$ alone — therefore quotes a number that neither the arms nor the bounds identify, especially when the interval is widest near the middle of the risk range.
Where the exposure is monotone, no unit's outcome is prevented by it, $Y(1) \ge Y(0)$, the lower edge is
attained exactly, $\mathrm{PNS} = a - b$, and the upper edge is pure slack: a worst-case rule of
attribution then charges the full $a$ for a harm that is only $a - b$, an over-attribution by the factor $a/(a-b)$,
which grows without bound as the margin between the arms shrinks.

### The remedy that the base rates forbid

The chapter's institutional conclusion in §5 is that disparity is not a tuning problem and must be met by
restructuring the loss matrix that assigns consequences to errors. That recommendation is the strongest in the
chapter and it is not executable as stated, because the loss matrix the institution controls is not the one the
theorem constrains. The two groups in an arrest archive do not share a base rate, which is the unequal-prevalence premise Chouldechova's theorem needs
and the condition under which no imperfect binary rule satisfying predictive parity ($\mathrm{PPV}\_1 = \mathrm{PPV}\_0$) can equalise both error rates. Take a working flag
and read it as a classifier of the recorded outcome: its false-positive rate among
those released differs between the groups. Equalising them upward means flagging many records that are currently
released, and equalising them downward means flagging fewer; either move is a
group-specific threshold, that is, the move the audit exists to forbid, and neither of them touches the loss matrix
at all.

So the recommendation fails in the way policy recommendations usually fail: it names the correct formal object and
misplaces the available lever. What the archive supports is a menu of three honest options (accept
unequal error rates and say so, accept a group-specific threshold and survive the legal test for it, or change the
base rate by changing what is policed) and the audit's job is to force that menu into the record. Recommending the first item on it is a separate act. The two rates and their ratio are computable from any classified archive; the
choice among the three options is not a computation.

### When there is no intervention to reason about

The chapter's premise is that every policy question is a rung-two or rung-three query. The condition that voids the
premise is the case in which no consistent manipulation exists to be denoted by $\mathrm{do}(x)$: "assign the better
teacher", "raise the standard", "improve the neighbourhood" name intentions, and two implementations of the same intention are two different treatments with two different effects. When that happens the causal query is not unidentified but ill-posed, and no amount of archive repairs it. The honest procedure is to write the manipulation as a protocol (who sets which variable, to what value, on which population, from when) and,
where no protocol can be written, to demote the question to a descriptive one and say that it was demoted. The
audit keeps the environments of §2 explicit for this reason, and
the invariance test returns an empty intersection when the candidate
set cannot be reconciled, which is the way of reporting that the variables it was given do not contain
the mechanism, and should be read as a refusal to answer, not as a null result.
