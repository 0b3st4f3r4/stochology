## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Classify a loss archive into one of the four power-law moment regimes from Hill's index and its interval, and say which summaries that regime licenses.
2. Compute the Jensen gap for a convex harm function and separate the part a finite sample supports from the part the population does not.
3. Decide when an ordinal rubric may be summed against a calibrated threshold and when the invariance argument of §5.2 governs instead.

An archive can be audited by Canonical Operator I (Chapters 43–45) and still leave the question of physical capacity open. Estimation and sizing are different problems, and the second one is about how a physical or social phenomenon acts on a living institution.

Mediating Filter II · Axis 2 ($S\_{\mathrm{vital}}$: Vital Margin) carries the vital axis, and Chapters 46 and 47 divide it between them. Its operator is Canonical Operator II, the vital margin state:

$$\Delta\_J(\phi, X) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\phi(X)\bigr] - \phi\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]\bigr) \approx \frac{1}{2}\phi''\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X]\bigr)\mkern3mu\mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathcal{R}\_{\mathrm{tail}}(\hat{\alpha})$$

The tension it opens on is Tension 3: formal equivalence and the tyranny of the lone mean against the non-identical, measurement scale invariance, convex harm and heavy-tailed extremes (Stevens, 1946; Jensen, 1906; Adorno, 1966; Georgescu-Roegen, 1971; Hill, 1975; Desrosières, 1993; Porter, 1995; Hacking, 2002; Gneiting & Raftery, 2007).

## 5.1 The Non-Identical and the Dialectical Penumbra

In *Negative Dialectics* (1966), Theodor W. Adorno observed that conceptual and statistical classification operates by forcing heterogeneous particulars under an identity sign ("the non-identical," *das Nichtidentische*). Deleuze reaches the same refusal of identity from the opposite road: difference is primary and negation derived, so that the dialectic becomes the thought of representation, while the remainder is what identity never captured and negation never used up (Deleuze 1968). The disagreement with this book is not about the remainder but about whether it moves. Stochology needs negation because a collapse is not a difference: it is a loss that can be dated. A few years later, Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (*The Entropy Law and the Economic Process*, 1971) distinguished **arithmomorphic concepts** (discrete numbers separated by an empty void) from **dialectical concepts** (justice, clinical severity, ecological distress, delay disruption), which are surrounded by a continuous **dialectical penumbra** $[c\_L, c\_U)$ where opposites overlap. Adorno's title is exact: the dialectic stays negative because the non-identical is what keeps it in motion, the concept's failure to exhaust its object being not a defect to be repaired but the moment that forces the next determination (Adorno 1966). What the machinery below measures is that remainder under a statistical description.

A bureaucracy that replaces a continuous phenomenon $X$ with a rigid binary indicator $\mathbb{I}\lbrace X \ge c\rbrace$ leaves a contested boundary zone $[c\_L, c\_U)$: a flight is "on time" one minute inside a schedule bar and "delayed" one minute outside it, a household is "eligible" or "ineligible" at a poverty line chosen to the currency unit. How much of the population sits inside that zone is the **penumbra mass** $\mathbb{P}(c\_L \le X < c\_U)$, and the operator computes it.

Whenever the mass is non-negligible, reporting a single binary classification rate hides the boundary sensitivity of the category itself: the rate is a statement about the cut, and the cut is a decision nobody defended.

## 5.2 Stevens's Scale Hierarchy and the Forbidden Ordinal Mean

In his landmark 1946 paper *On the Theory of Scales of Measurement*, S. S. Stevens proved that a statistical summary is meaningful only if its conclusions are invariant under the admissible transformations $\phi$ of its measurement scale:

| Scale Type | Admissible Transformation $\phi(x)$ | Invariant Statistics | Illegitimate Operations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominal** | Any bijection | Mode, frequency, entropy | Mean, median, order |
| **Ordinal** | Any strictly increasing $\phi$ ($x < y \implies \phi(x) < \phi(y)$) | Median, quantiles, Mann–Whitney $U = \mathbb{P}(A > B) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(A = B)$ | Arithmetic mean $\bar{x}$, standard deviation |
| **Interval** | Positive affine $\phi(x) = a x + b$ ($a > 0$) | Mean, variance, Pearson correlation | Coefficient of variation $\sigma / \mu$, ratios |
| **Ratio** | Positive similarity $\phi(x) = a x$ ($a > 0$) | All of the above + geometric mean, ratios, Kelly growth | — |

Institutions routinely collect **ordinal** scores such as Likert satisfaction rubrics, clinical pain scales, ESG grades or model-judge rubrics, and average them as if the gap between adjacent codes were identical across the scale. Two facts follow from the definition alone:

1. A strictly increasing convex transformation can **invert the arithmetic mean ranking**: the arm with the higher mean can have the lower mean after the transformation, even though the transformation preserved every ordering within each arm.
2. The ordinal Mann–Whitney dominance probability $U(A, B) = \mathbb{P}(A > B) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(A = B)$ is **invariant under every admissible transformation**, because it depends only on the order the transformation is required to preserve.

The pair is the argument of §5.7.2 in advance: a summary that is not invariant is not thereby forbidden, but its use has to be justified by something other than the scale: typically a threshold calibrated against an outcome, which is a claim about the pre-registration and not about the measurement.

## 5.3 Jensen's Convexity Gap and the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)

Even on a genuine ratio scale (megawatts of electricity demand, minutes of delay, millions of kroner in fire damage), reporting only the sample mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$ commits **the flaw of averages** whenever harm $\phi(x)$ is convex ($\phi''(x) > 0$). By Johan Jensen's (1906) inequality:
$$\Delta\_J = \mathbb{E}[\phi(X)] - \phi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \ge 0$$
For quadratic loss $\phi(x) = x^2$, the gap is exactly the variance: $\Delta\_J = \mathrm{Var}(X)$. A second-order Taylor expansion around the mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$ reveals the general dependence on curvature:
$$\Delta\_J \approx \frac{1}{2}\phi''(\mu)\mkern3mu\sigma^2 + \frac{1}{6}\phi'''(\mu)\mkern3mu\mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^3] + \dots$$
Whenever the underlying distribution is right-skewed or heavy-tailed, higher-order moments dominate, widening $\Delta\_J$ far beyond the Gaussian approximation.

Every nonlinear evaluation therefore reports the full distribution summary:

- the sample mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$, the median $q\_{50}$, and the tail quantiles $(q\_{05}, q\_{95})$;
- the evaluation at the mean $\phi(\mathbb{E}[X])$, the expected evaluation $\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]$, and the **Jensen gap** $\Delta\_J$ introduced in [Chapter 31](31-distribution-and-moment.md) §5;
- Tilmann Gneiting & Adrian Raftery's (2007) strictly proper **Continuous Ranked Probability Score** of the predictive distribution $F$ against the realized outcome $y$.

### 5.3.1 CRPS Integral Representation vs. MSE Failure

Under heavy-tailed losses, classical Mean Squared Error ($\mathrm{MSE} = (y - \hat{y})^2$) is pathological: squaring an extreme loss produces massive variance in the evaluation metric itself. Gneiting & Raftery's CRPS solves this by integrating the squared difference between the cumulative distribution function $F(t)$ and the empirical Heaviside step $\mathbf{1}\{y \le t\}$ across all thresholds $t \in \mathbb{R}$:
$$\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int\_{-\infty}^\infty \bigl(F(t) - \mathbf{1}\{y \le t\}\bigr)^2 dt = \mathbb{E}\_F|X - y| - \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\_F|X - X'|$$
where $X, X' \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} F$. CRPS has three virtues:

1. **Strict Propriety:** The expected CRPS is minimized if and only if the forecaster reports the true data-generating distribution $F$;
2. **Linear Tail Sensitivity:** Unlike MSE, CRPS grows linearly with extreme errors in the tails ($|X - y|$), remaining finite and numerically stable even when second moments diverge;
3. **Distribution-Wide Evaluation:** It assesses the entire distribution (center, dispersion, and extreme quantiles) in the natural units of the outcome.

## 5.4 Bruce M. Hill's (1975) Tail Index, Four Moment Regimes, and the Breakdown of the CLT

$\Delta\_J$ can grow by orders of magnitude as sample size increases, because many physical, financial and operational losses follow **Pareto power-law tails**:
$$\mathbb{P}(X > x) \sim L(x)\mkern3mux^{-\alpha} \quad \text{as } x \to \infty$$
where $L(x)$ is a slowly varying function ($\lim\_{t\to\infty} L(t x)/L(t) = 1$) and $\alpha > 0$ is the **tail index**.

Using the top $k + 1$ order statistics $X\_{(n)} \ge X\_{(n-1)} \ge \dots \ge X\_{(n-k)} > 0$, Bruce M. Hill's (1975) conditional maximum-likelihood tail estimator is:
$$\hat{\alpha}\_{k,n} = \left(\frac{1}{k}\sum\_{i=1}^k \ln\frac{X\_{(n - i + 1)}}{X\_{(n - k)}}\right)^{-1}$$

### 5.4.1 The Four Power-Law Moment Regimes

The tail exponent $\alpha$ dictates the convergence of the tail integral $\mathbb{E}[X^p] = \int\_0^\infty p t^{p-1}\mathbb{P}(X > t) dt$, partitioning all phenomena into **four fundamental regimes**:

1. **$\alpha \le 1$: Infinite Mean and Variance:**
   Even the population mean does not exist ($\mathbb{E}[X] = \infty$). The sample average $\bar{X}\_n$ does not stabilize around any constant; as $n \to \infty$, $\bar{X}\_n$ continues to grow without bound, dominated by rare colossal records.
2. **$1 < \alpha \le 2$: Finite Mean, Infinite Variance:**
   The population mean exists ($\mathbb{E}[X] < \infty$), but the population variance is infinite ($\mathrm{Var}(X) = \infty$). As established in [Chapter 13](13-integrals-and-accumulation.md) §2, the integral for the first moment converges, but the second moment integral diverges. Catastrophic insurance losses sit squarely in this regime, which is why reinsurance stress cannot be sized from a variance.
3. **$2 < \alpha \le 4$: Finite Variance, Infinite Kurtosis:**
   The mean and variance are finite, but the fourth moment diverges ($\mathbb{E}[X^4] = \infty$). Sample estimates of variance fluctuate erratically across samples, and higher-order risk measures (such as tail risk measures) suffer massive pre-asymptotic instability.
4. **$\alpha > 4$: Gaussian Domain (Finite Kurtosis):**
   Higher moments exist, sample variances converge stably, and classical Gaussian asymptotics provide reliable approximations.

### 5.4.2 Failure and Extreme Pre-Asymptotic Slowdown of the Central Limit Theorem

When $\alpha < 2$, the Lindeberg–Lévy Central Limit Theorem fails completely: normalized sums $(S\_n - n\mu) / n^{1/\alpha}$ do not converge to a normal distribution, but to an **$\alpha$-stable Lévy distribution** with heavy tails and infinite variance (Gnedenko & Kolmogorov, 1954).

Even more dangerous is the pre-asymptotic behavior for $1 < \alpha \le 2$: while the Strong Law of Large Numbers guarantees $\bar{X}\_n \xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}} \mu$, the rate of convergence is governed by the stable scaling factor:
$$|\bar{X}\_n - \mu| = \mathcal{O}\_p\bigl(n^{1/\alpha - 1}\bigr)$$
For a Gaussian distribution, errors shrink as $n^{-0.50}$, so shrinking the error by a factor of ten costs a hundredfold sample. For an index close to one the same improvement costs $10^{1/(1 - 1/\alpha)}$ times the sample — a number that grows without bound as $\alpha$ falls toward one, and that no institution can fund. Operating on the naive sample mean creates a dangerous illusion of certainty while leaving the institution completely exposed to unmodeled tail surprises.

When extreme tail observations are clustered ($\mathrm{DEFF}\_{\mathrm{tail}} > 1$), the effective number of tail order statistics shrinks to $k\_{\mathrm{eff}} = k / \mathrm{DEFF}\_{\mathrm{tail}}$ and the index estimate is distributed as an inverse gamma around the true exponent. Because the probability of Holling basin rupture is strictly convex in that estimate, a plug-in evaluation inflates the expected rupture probability, and the audit therefore reports both the second-order Jensen-debiased estimate and the exact confidence interval around it — the plugin, the debiased value and the interval are three different statements about the same archive.

## 5.5 The Same Estimator on Insurance and Flight Archives

The battery is deliberately narrow: Hill's index with its stability envelope and its interval, the penumbra mass at the boundary the institution actually uses, the Jensen gap under a convex evaluation, CRPS against the realized outcome, and the Holling rupture probability under tail clustering. Run on a catastrophic insurance loss archive it answers the first question a capacity planner should ask, which moment if any exists, and the answer is not a point but a regime together with the envelope that keeps it in that regime.

The convex evaluation is where the gap becomes institutional. A reinsurance or penalty schedule that grows faster than linearly in the loss makes the evaluation at the mean and the expected evaluation two different numbers, and the gap between them is not estimation error: it is what convexity costs, computable from the archive's own distribution and irreducible to better estimation. The same archive then supplies the ordinal question of §5.2, because the boundary between "reportable" and "not reportable" is a dial the institution sets, and the penumbra mass measures how many losses sit inside it.

The flight archives supply the comparison the insurance archive cannot. Delay tails are thinner than insurance losses, and their regime can shift across a decade, so the same estimator produces a different classification on a different archive, and a different classification on the *same* archive at a different time. That is the estimator's own uncertainty showing through: the object to report is the plateau of the index across retained counts and the interval that comes with it, not the value at a chosen count. A point estimate of $\hat{\alpha}$ without its interval invites a planner to treat a regime label as a measurement, and the four regimes of §5.4.1 differ in what they license, not in how alarming they sound.

## 5.6 The same battery on a national air-transport archive

The flight tables this chapter draws on are North American: several New York departure windows and a Houston
window. A second national archive is available and is distributed as an open statistical transport series,
naturally combined with a live tracking feed (Agência Nacional de Aviação Civil 2024), and the reason for naming it explicitly is methodological: it is a second institutional
producer, with its own reporting thresholds and its own missingness pattern, so it
exercises the archive-to-population inference of [Chapter 34](34-sample-population-archive.md) on a different
σ-algebra of questions.

The battery of §5.3–§5.4 transfers without modification, and the order in which it should be run is the point:

1. **Declare the archive boundary before computing anything.** Which carrier-months are present, which scheduled
   services are absent, which airports appear only after a certain year. The deltas that matter are computed inside
   the declared window; a tail index estimated across a boundary shift estimates the boundary.
2. **Estimate the tail on the declared support.** Hill's estimator $\hat{\alpha}\_{k,n}$ is a function of the number
   of upper-order statistics retained, so the plateau, not the single value at a chosen $k$, is the object to
   report. Compare it with the New York and Houston plateaus: a *different* $\hat{\alpha}$ is informative, and a
   suspiciously identical one usually means the same cancellation policy is being measured.
3. **Measure concentration as a topology, not a mean.** Hub structure is a statement about the share of movements
   carried by a few primary national transfer nodes (such as the São Paulo–Guarulhos, Congonhas, and Brasília trunk corridors) relative to regional spokes, and the right summary is a concentration curve paired with the load-redistribution topology of
   [Chapter 53](53-layer-macro-coupled-topology-order.md), not an average delay.
4. **Test the cascade separately from the level.** A delay that propagates across aircraft rotations and crew connections is a queue phenomenon with the Kingman
   multiplier of [Chapter 47](47-irreversible-time-vital-margin.md); a delay that does not propagate is a local
   shock. The two have different tail behaviour and the same mean.

The same discipline governs the two graphical environments in which this material is usually taught. A no-code statistics front end (Orange) and a spreadsheet-adjacent package (JASP) both make it easy to run a test whose assumptions are invisible: a $t$-test on a heavy-tailed delay distribution, a correlation on a non-randomly-missing pair, a $p$-value reported without the number of comparisons behind it. The audit questions belong on a written checklist: which population was sampled, what was dropped by the join, how many models were tried before the reported one, and whether the reported interval is a confidence interval or a credible interval. A tool that will not answer those four questions about its own output has automated the production of Type III error.

The national archive is **not** part of this edition's sealed evidence base, and the reason is a provenance commitment and not an oversight: a dataset earns that standing only with a pinned digest, a documented retrieval path and a stated schema contract, because a table whose upstream can change silently is not evidence. Until that is in place, the chapter's measured tails are the North American windows.

### Two tails, two regimes

The tail index can fall between two flight archives and can be accompanied by intervals that do not overlap. Both regimes have finite variance and infinite kurtosis, and neither shares a regime with catastrophic insurance losses, where the variance itself does not exist. Three archives, three regimes, and one estimator that reports all three honestly only if it also reports its interval.

The consequence for a decision is that the regime decides which summary is admissible; the truism that tails are dangerous decides nothing. Below two the variance is a fiction, between two and four the variance exists and the fourth moment does not, and above four the classical moments are usable. A plan that quotes a variance without naming the regime is quoting a number whose existence it has not checked.

Asymmetry is the second axis and it is not the same as thickness: a thin-tailed loss distribution skewed to the right can have a larger expected shortfall than a symmetric fat-tailed one at the same standard deviation. The operator combines the two because a decision that reads only one of them has read the wrong one.

### When the damage per event is capped

The heavy tail stops governing the decision when the damage per event has a ceiling. The procedure is to truncate the distribution at the ceiling, recompute the score and the index, and to say that the ceiling (not the tail) is now the operative statistic; the conclusion survives while the ceiling stays above the measured upper quantile.

### What the operator is for

The operator on this page is deliberately narrow, and the narrowness is the claim. Instead of forecasting the tail, it classifies the regime and reports the interval that the classification can support. A reader who wants a number for the next loss should read the ceiling and the shortfall; a reader who wants to know whether a variance is a meaningful quantity should read the index and its interval. The two readers need different statistics from the same archive. The regime is the sentence that has to appear next to any moment quoted from an archive, and its absence is the most common form of quiet overclaim in applied work.

## 5.7 The estimator's own regime

### 5.7.1 Two limits of the index, worked by hand

Take first $k = 1$ in Hill's estimator. The estimator is then a single log spacing, $\hat{\alpha} = S^{-1}$ with $S = \ln(X\_{(n)}/X\_{(n-1)})$, and if the upper tail is Pareto with index $\alpha$ then $S$ is exponential with rate $\alpha$. Its mean is $\mathbb{E}[S] = 1/\alpha$, and the reciprocal does not inherit that: $\mathbb{E}[S^{-1}] = \int\_0^\infty (\alpha/s)\mkern3mue^{-\alpha s}\mkern3muds$ behaves like $\alpha/s$ near the origin, so it diverges and $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}] = \infty$. At a single retained order statistic the estimator's mean does not exist at all, which is why the stability envelope, not a single $k$, is the object of report.

Take next the retained count an archival estimate actually uses. At nominal confidence $1 - \delta$ with $\delta \in (0, 1)$ kept distinct from the tail index $\alpha$, read the two-sided interval as a normal approximation on the asymptotic standard error $\alpha/\sqrt{k}$: the half-width is $z\_{1-\delta/2}\mkern2mu\hat{\alpha}\_{k,n}/\sqrt{k}$, valid only in the intermediate regime $k \to \infty$ with $k/n \to 0$. Below that window finite-$k$ reciprocal bias of order $\alpha/(k - 1)$ and $1/\sqrt{k}$ variance dominate; above it, departures of the slowly varying prefactor $L(x)$ from a constant introduce bias of order $(k/n)^{\beta}$ under second-order regular variation ([Chapter 31](31-distribution-and-moment.md), §3.2–§3.3). An interval computed at a single $k$ is therefore a claim about where the bias–variance plateau sits, and it inherits every assumption that plateau makes.

One more computation changes what the Jensen gap is. When the convexity exponent of the harm function exceeds the tail index, the moment $\mathbb{E}[X^{p}]$ converges only if $p < \alpha$, and if $p > \alpha$ the population expectation diverges while the sample expectation is finite. The sample number is then not an estimate of a finite quantity: it is dominated by the largest observations in the file, and deleting the single largest lowers it by a visible share. That is what makes a Jensen gap computed on a heavy tail a statement about the sample, and the world is not in it.

### 5.7.2 An ordinal sum that earns its threshold

§5.2 licenses the median and the dominance probability for an ordinal rubric and rules averaging its codes illegitimate. Virginia Apgar's 1953 scoring rule performs the forbidden operation literally. Five signs (heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, colour) are each coded $0$, $1$ or $2$, and the codes are summed into an integer on $0$ to $10$ compared with a threshold (Apgar 1953). Nothing in the total is invariant under a strictly increasing rescaling of a component, and the rule is used because of that.

The arithmetic is two lines and it needs no particular infants. Take two cases whose summed codes order one way. Replace the colour code $(0, 1, 2)$ by any strictly increasing relabelling — say the middle and top codes moved apart — and the ordering of the two cases can reverse, the comparison with a fixed threshold can flip, and the two cases change places. By the letter of §5.2 the sum is not a statistic at all.

The conclusion usually drawn (replace the sum by the invariant median) is the wrong recommendation, and this is the counterexample. The median of five codes carries no cross-infant ordering strong enough to face a threshold, and the dominance probability answers a question about a pair; it says nothing about one infant. What the sum delivers is an instrument: weights fixed before use, a threshold fitted to neonatal outcome, one number comparable with that threshold for every infant. The invariance that matters is that of the calibrated threshold across the population, a property of the pre-registration, not of the scale.

That is also the structure of $\Delta\_J$, which measures nothing in isolation and decides between two policies while deciding nothing about one alone. "Illegitimate operation" names a class of statistics; the class a decision needs is fixed by what that decision must withstand.

### 5.7.3 When the parts' index does not fix the whole's

The thesis of this chapter is that the regime decides which summary is admissible. It has an unstated precondition: the regime must be a property of the variable the decision is about. Institutions rarely track a single loss; they track a ratio, claims over premium, or a sum across lines. An index estimated on each component does not determine the tail of any of these, and the instruction to read the index and choose the summary then has nothing to read.

Two lines with the same margins make the failure exact. Let $X$ and $Y$ be Pareto with index $\alpha \in (1, 2)$ and consider the ratio $X/Y$. If the lines are comonotone, $Y = cX$ for fixed $c$, then $X/Y = 1/c$ is constant: no upper tail, no Jensen gap. If they are independent, every threshold $t$ has $\mathbb{P}(X/Y > t) > 0$, so an increasing convex functional of the ratio has no finite expectation. One pair of indices, one label, two answers of different kinds.

When the precondition fails, declare the aggregation functional first and bound, not estimate. For a functional of two heavy-tailed arguments the bound has two ends computable from the marginals alone, independence and comonotonicity. Report the bound, name the copula assumption behind each end, and withhold the regime label. The literature that makes the move routine treats ratio and sum as distinct objects of regular variation; they are not functions of a shared exponent (Embrechts et al. 1997). A chapter that classifies regimes is as useful as the functional it classifies, and the functional is a choice made before the data arrived.
