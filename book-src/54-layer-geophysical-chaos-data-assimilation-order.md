## 1. Deterministic chaos, tangent horizons, and multiscale ceilings

Picture two hurricane forecast runs started on Monday from barometer readings that differ by no more than a thermometer's rounding error. On Wednesday both tracks kiss the same coastline; by Friday one sends the storm roaring into the harbor while the other curves harmlessly out to sea, yet a seawall cannot be poured on Thursday night.

That vanishing lead time is the core trap of geophysical prediction. Here, `T5Temporal` protects the finite clock before tiny measurement gaps grow past usable warning time. Even though planetary fluids obey deterministic Navier–Stokes equations, hydrostatic across broad weather aspect ratios ($H/L \ll 1$; [Mechanics, Determinism, and Chaos](21-mechanics-determinism-chaos.md)), their practical forecast window stays tightly bounded.

In Lorenz's (1963) three-mode truncation of Saltzman's (1962) planar convection, trajectories on a strange attractor separate exponentially along the fastest unstable direction ($\lambda\_1 > 0$). Inside the tiny linear growth regime ($\varepsilon\_0 \ll \delta\_{\mathrm{tol}} \ll \mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{A})$), starting from initial error $\varepsilon\_0 > 0$ to an error tolerance $\delta\_{\mathrm{tol}} > \varepsilon\_0$ fixes a finite Lyapunov horizon $\tau\_{\mathrm{Lyap}}$:

$$\tau\_{\mathrm{Lyap}} = \lambda\_1^{-1}\ln\\!\left(\delta\_{\mathrm{tol}}/\varepsilon\_0\right).$$

Read aloud, cutting your initial sensor error in half adds only one fixed step $(\ln 2)/\lambda\_1$ of warning time, diverging only in the impossible zero-error limit ($\varepsilon\_0 \to 0^+$).

Real multi-scale atmospheres impose an even stricter wall. Atmospheric analogues drift away from single exponential growth toward climatological averages (Lorenz 1969). Finite-size Lyapunov exponents $\lambda(\delta)$ across multiscale turbulence show that tiny, fast-growing thunderstorms saturate early and cap large-scale predictability even as $\varepsilon\_0 \to 0^+$ (Boffetta et al. 1998). Chaotic growth $\lambda\_1$ differs fundamentally from discrete autoregressive persistence $\rho\_1$ (distinct from §3's data-defect correlation $\rho\_{R,Y}$; [Statistical Physics and the Arrow of Time](23-statistical-physics-and-time.md)): beyond $\tau\_{\mathrm{Lyap}}$, single-track forecasts collapse into climate distributions.

## 2. Subgrid closure, Type-B discrepancy, and convex gusts

Here, `T4Formalization` protects against smoothing away unresolved subgrid storms and underpricing gusts whose damage rises faster than wind speed. Synoptic weather follows quasi-geostrophic cascades ($k^{-3}$) before handing off to the three-dimensional swirl cascade drawn by Richardson (1922). Across that inertial subrange ($L^{-1} \ll k \ll \eta^{-1}$), depending solely on dissipation rate $\varepsilon > 0$ and wavenumber $k > 0$ ($[\varepsilon] = \mathrm{m^2\mkern3mus^{-3}}$, $[E] = \mathrm{m^3\mkern3mus^{-2}}$) fixes Kolmogorov's (1941) energy spectrum $E(k) = C\_K \varepsilon^{2/3} k^{-5/3}$ ($C\_K > 0$).

Kilometer-scale weather grids still miss updraft cores, cloud microphysics, gravity-wave drag, and surface boundary layers. Truncating those scales creates Type-B structural error ([Layer 08](58-layer-metrological-information-geometric-order.md)), modeled as a Kennedy–O'Hagan (2001) discrepancy $\delta\_{\mathrm{KOH}}(x)$. Because field residuals cannot untangle $\delta\_{\mathrm{KOH}}(x)$ from empirical tuning parameters $\theta$, no parameter calibration removes it.

Wind damage adds an asymmetric penalty on top of subgrid omission. Kinetic wind-power flux scales with the cube of wind speed $W \ge 0$, so any strictly convex loss $L(W)$ (distinct from §1–§2's fluid length scale $L$) with finite mean $\mathbb{E}[L(W)] < \infty$ creates a positive shortfall gap $\Delta\_J$ under Jensen's (1906) inequality for any variable wind:

$$\Delta\_J = \mathbb{E}[L(W)] - L(\mathbb{E}[W]) > 0.$$

In plain words, average damage from fluctuating gusts always exceeds the damage caused by an average wind. Under heavy-tailed Pareto gusts with $1 < \alpha \le 3$, mean speed $\mathbb{E}[W]$ remains finite while cubic energy $\mathbb{E}[W^3]$ blows up to infinity, sending $\Delta\_J \to +\infty$ and ruining defenses sized to mean wind forecasts.

## 3. Assimilation, the best-track archive, and proper scoring

Here, `T3Archive` protects the observational record from unlogged station gaps, missed unstable storm directions, and mixing real-time advisories with hindsight storm tracks. Data assimilation merges physical balance equations with sparse, asynchronous measurements across an assimilation window $[t\_0, t\_K]$.

Classical tools split along a tight tradeoff. Strong-constraint 4D-Var fits a deterministic track assuming a perfect model ($\delta\_{\mathrm{KOH}} = 0$), static background error $\mathbf{B}$, and tangent-linear approximations that fail inside moist thunderstorms without weak-constraint error terms. The ensemble Kalman filter (Evensen 1994) evolves flow-dependent spread across $N\_e \ll n\_{\mathrm{state}}$ members, requiring spatial localization and covariance inflation against *filter divergence* and struggling when storms split into two rival paths. Hybrid systems (`EnVar` and weak-constraint 4D-Var) blend both tools, yet still inherit $\delta\_{\mathrm{KOH}}$ and stay blind to unobservable unstable directions ($w\_1 = 0$, the squared projection weight onto the observed subspace defined in §5) missed by sensor grids.

Learned variational methods sharpen this split between visible smoothness and hidden instability (Fablet et al. 2021). Across $2{,}000$ Lorenz-63 trajectories ($\Delta t=0.01$, $200$ steps, first component observed every $8$ steps, variance-$2$ noise), classical 4D-Var (`L63-RK4`) achieved `4DVar-score` $7.70\times 10^{-3}$ and `ODE-score` $<10^{-4}$ yet `R-score` $3.55$, whereas supervised `L63-GENN1` (`aG-Conv`) cut `R-score` to $1.34$ despite `4DVar-score` $1.10\times 10^{-1}$ and `ODE-score` $0.16$; across $256$ Lorenz-96 trajectories ($\Delta t=0.05$, $200$ steps, $F=8$, $20$ of $40$ variables observed every $4$ steps, variance-$2$ noise), `L96-RK4` posted `R-score` $1.06$ (`4DVar-score` $1.47\times 10^{-2}$, `ODE-score` $<10^{-4}$) versus `R-score` $0.38$ ($5.30\times 10^{-2}$, $7.20\times 10^{-2}$) for `L96-GENN` (`aG-LSTM`) (Fablet et al. 2021). Tight observational fit on $\Omega$ leaves unobserved modes drifting, while supervised `R-score` tuning presupposes complete ground truth and degrades physical balance.

Forecast verification also requires strict bitemporal bookkeeping ([Aim and the Three Planes](29-aim-and-the-three-planes.md); Snodgrass 2000). Analysts must keep real-time warnings ($t\_{\mathrm{disp}}$) distinct from post-storm best-track reanalyses ($t\_{\mathrm{ev}}$): the North Atlantic archive logs six-hourly fixes smoothed after the fact without row-level uncertainty tags (Landsea & Franklin 2013), and Kaplan and DeMaria (2003) defined rapid intensification near the 95th percentile as at least $30\text{ kt}$ ($15.4\text{ m/s}$) in $24\text{ h}$. Collapsing ensemble spread into one track incurs a calibration penalty under the Continuous Ranked Probability Score ($\mathrm{CRPS}$; Gneiting & Raftery 2007; [Appendix A](appendix-a-mathematics.md) §A.2), strictly proper over $\mathcal{P}\_1$ ($\mathbb{E}|X| < \infty$).

Digital twins inherit that `T3Archive` ceiling: assimilation constrains only mask $\Omega$, while silent outages or unlogged relocations create dark-archive gaps ($\rho\_{R,Y}\ne 0$) disguised as calm seas.

## 5. Two limits, subspace projection, and variable growth rates

### Single-cycle analysis gain and asymptotic limits

Consider a scalar observation ($H = 1$, observation operator distinct from §1's aspect depth $H$) combining independent background variance $B > 0$ with observation variance $R > 0$ (distinct from §3's `R-score` and §3/§6's recording indicator $R$ in $\rho\_{R,Y}$). Setting initial error $\varepsilon\_0 = \sigma\_a < \delta\_{\mathrm{tol}}$ in §1's horizon $\tau\_{\mathrm{Lyap}}$ under daily growth $\lambda\_1 > 0$ defines Kalman gain $K \in (0, 1)$ (distinct from §3's assimilation endpoint $t\_K$ and Part IV's contractual cap $K$) and analysis variance $\sigma\_a^2 > 0$:

$$K = \frac{B}{B+R}, \qquad \sigma\_a^2 = \frac{BR}{B+R}.$$

In plain words, Kalman gain $K$ weighs how strongly each new gauge reading pulls the forecast away from the prior guess.

Cutting $\varepsilon\_0$ by factor $e^{\lambda\_1 \Delta\tau}$ buys lead time $\Delta\tau > 0$. Doubling independent sensors ($\mathrm{DEFF} = 1$) halves observation variance ($R \mapsto R/2$), gaining exactly

$$\Delta\tau = \frac{1}{2\lambda\_1}\ln\\!\left(\frac{2\mkern3mu(B + R/2)}{B + R}\right) = \frac{1}{2\lambda\_1}\ln(1 + K).$$

Read aloud, adding twice as many sensors buys at most half a step of extra forecast lead time, and even that gain shrinks whenever the prior forecast is already tighter than the sensors.

Two clean limits bracket sensor upgrades: in the **observation-dominated limit** ($R/B \to 0^+$, $K \to 1^-$), lead-time gain saturates at $\Delta\tau \to (\ln 2)/(2\lambda\_1)$, half the gain of halving $\varepsilon\_0$ directly, since halving variance cuts root-mean-square error only by $\sqrt{2}$. In the **background-dominated limit** ($B/R \to 0^+$, $K \to 0^+$), gain shrinks to $\Delta\tau \approx B/(2\lambda\_1 R) \to 0^+$. Along unobserved modes ($H = 0$), $K = 0$ buys zero lead time; across scalar assimilation cycles, $B\_{t+1} = M^2\sigma\_{a,t}^2 + Q \ge Q$ (or, in matrix form with state covariance $\mathbf{P}\_{a,t}$ and positive-definite model-error covariance $\mathbf{Q} \succ \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{B}\_{t+1} = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{P}\_{a,t}\mathbf{M}^\top + \mathbf{Q} \succeq \mathbf{Q}$) ensures irreducible model error $Q > 0$ ($\delta\_{\mathrm{KOH}}$) caps improvement even as $R \to 0^+$.

### Subspace projection: isotropic RMSE versus unstable growth

Why can global error scores drop while a storm surprise still ambushes a forecast? Suppose observed orthogonal directions carry fraction $w \in [0, 1]$ of initial error variance and get a fourfold variance cut (halving root-mean-square error there), leaving the unseen complement $1 - w$ untouched. Total RMSE falls to $\sqrt{1 - 3w/4} \in [1/2, 1]$ of baseline for any $w > 0$.

Yet if a leading unstable singular vector $\mathbf{u}\_1$ ($\lVert\mathbf{u}\_1\rVert\_2 = 1$) sits orthogonal to the observed column space $\mathrm{range}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}^\top)$, its projected variance weight vanishes ($w\_1 := \mathbf{u}\_1^\top \mathbf{\Pi}\_{\mathrm{obs}}\mathbf{u}\_1 = 0$, where $\mathbf{\Pi}\_{\mathrm{obs}}$ projects orthogonally onto $\mathrm{range}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}^\top)$ and $w < 1$), so its error stays untouched and grows at full speed ($\Delta\tau = 0$) even while global RMSE drops. Targeting observations therefore demands projecting onto tangent-linear singular vectors, whose validity collapses inside moist convection. This geometric split clarifies §3's gap between `4DVar-score` and `R-score` (Fablet et al. 2021): learned assimilators shrink error across well-observed or smooth patterns ($w > 0$) while leaving orthogonal unstable modes ($w\_1 = 0$) and unseen shocks intact.

### Random divergence rates and rapid-intensification tails

Rapid-intensification jumps ($\Delta V\_{24\text{h}}$ in §3) squeeze $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}}$ and break linear error models. Even under a varying positive growth rate $\Lambda > 0$, horizon time $\tau(\Lambda) = \Lambda^{-1}\ln(\delta\_{\mathrm{tol}}/\varepsilon\_0)$ is strictly convex in $\Lambda$. By Jensen's (1906) inequality, average predictability obeys $\mathbb{E}[\tau(\Lambda)] = \ln(\delta\_{\mathrm{tol}}/\varepsilon\_0)\mkern2mu\mathbb{E}[\Lambda^{-1}] > \tau(\mathbb{E}[\Lambda])$ whenever $\Lambda > 0$ varies (diverging if $\Lambda$ puts excessive mass near zero).

Crucially, lower warning horizons $\tau\_q = \Lambda\_{1-q}^{-1}\ln(\delta\_{\mathrm{tol}}/\varepsilon\_0)$ fall strictly below plug-in $\tau(\mathbb{E}[\Lambda])$ specifically in fast-intensifying tails where $\Lambda\_{1-q} > \mathbb{E}[\Lambda]$ (and below $\mathbb{E}[\tau]$ whenever $\Lambda\_{1-q} > (\mathbb{E}[\Lambda^{-1}])^{-1}$), while diffusive first-passage times skew right. Valid statistics demand bitemporal separation between real-time warnings ($t\_{\mathrm{disp}}$) and post-storm tracks ($t\_{\mathrm{ev}}$); without a reliable pre-onset detector, forecasters must publish the full mixture against $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} \ge \mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{I}\_t)$ instead of one deterministic track.

## 6. Why local `(1, 2, 0)` triggers Collapse Proposition 3 despite $\mathrm{Cone}(L\_{04})$
