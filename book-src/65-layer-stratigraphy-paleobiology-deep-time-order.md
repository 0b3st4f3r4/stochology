## 1. The interval is chosen before it is measured

A date is an answer, and an answer needs a question that fixes what would count as one. In deep time the question is never simply *when did this happen*. Nothing in the rock carries a label. The question is *which interval is this, and where do its edges fall*, and the edges are not found. They are designated. A boundary is a point in a section, chosen because it is identifiable and then ratified; the date assigned to it is the date of that point, not of a joint in nature. That is why framing is the primary site here: the object of every later measurement is fixed before any measurement begins.

The denominator comes first. A rate is a count per interval, and an interval chosen after the counts are tallied is a statistic of the choice, not of the world — widen the window and the same deaths become a background rate, narrow it and they become an extinction. Then identity across time. A taxon is the same taxon across an interval only under a criterion of identity, and the frame decides whether a change of form is a change of kind or a change within a lineage. Then membership. The frame decides which deposits belong to the section at all, and so decides which observations will be available to the archive that the next section prices.

No measurement can test the frame it works inside. A section whose boundary was drawn to include a particular fauna will report that fauna as present throughout the interval, and no care taken inside the section will detect the circularity, because the section is the unit of care. This is the reflexive lock that [Chapter 32](32-the-question-before-the-data.md) places before the data, applied to a substrate that keeps no record of the question asked of it. So the frame travels beside the date and is never inferred from it. A date published without its frame is a number whose scope nobody can state.

## 2. The record is the only access

The section is an envelope in time. Whatever passed through it and left nothing is not stored anywhere else, so the incompleteness of the record is not damage to a copy: there is no original the copy could be found wanting against. *Bias* suggests a true value that a better sample would recover. Here the sample is the only measurement of the population, and what can be recovered is the structure of the sampling, not a complete census.

That structure is a rate. Preservation happens where accumulation outruns destruction, so the record thins where the rate is low. A series' observed range is a lower bound on its true range, and the shortfall widens as the archive thins. The correction multiplies the observed range, it does not divide it, and it grows largest for the archive with the fewest dated horizons (Signor & Lipps 1982; Strauss & Sadler 1989). The correction is short enough to derive. Suppose $H$ fossiliferous horizons fall
independently and uniformly along a true range of length $T$, and the observed range between the outermost two is $R$. Conditional on the first
horizon, the other $H - 1$ all lie within a stretch of length $R$ with probability $(R/T)^{H-1}$, so the chance that the true end lies more than
$aR$ beyond the last find is $(1 + a)^{-(H-1)}$. Setting that chance equal to $1 - C$ gives the extension at confidence $C$,

$$a = (1 - C)^{-1/(H-1)} - 1,$$

and the range is reported as $R$ extended by $aR$ at the end in question (Strauss & Sadler 1989). Both limits are visible in the formula: with
two horizons the exponent is $-1$ and the extension is $C/(1 - C)$ times the observed range, unbounded as the confidence approaches one; as $H$
grows, $a$ falls toward zero like $-\ln(1 - C)/(H - 1)$. The correction multiplies $R$ because the gap is measured in units of the mean spacing
between finds, and the archive's precision is therefore a property of its density, with the formula assuming the uniformity that the
next paragraphs deny. Two sections holding the same number of specimens but different numbers of dated horizons do not carry the same information, and the difference is computable before a single taxon is named.

Direction is the second structure. What the record keeps is not a random subset of what existed: hard parts survive where soft bodies do not, shallow water where uplands do not, rapid burial where exposure does not. A bias with a known direction is a parameter. A bias treated as noise is a mystery. Carry the direction explicitly, then, because a faunal turnover measured across a boundary may be a turnover in preservation, and from inside the count the two look the same.

Resolution decides what the archive can be asked. A section does not deposit at a constant rate: a condensed bed may compress a long interval into a thickness a hand can span, and a lag surface may spend a long time producing nothing at all. The record's temporal resolution is a property of the substrate, not of the question put to it. An event that took less time than the resolution cannot be ordered against another event that took less time than the resolution, not because the evidence is weak but because the archive's coordinate along the axis has a grain. Read a rate off a coarse archive and the fine structure averages away; the averaged rate is a property of the grain.

Absence then becomes an observation with a length. A taxon missing from an interval is not thereby absent from it until the interval's sampling has been priced, and the price is not rhetorical. It is the same range-end correction, applied to the other end of the same series. The gap is not a hole in the data. It is data about the rate at which the substrate keeps what falls into it, and this order has to read it that way, because its object is reachable only through what survived.

## 3. The date is a measurement with its own error

A date is an estimate with a variance, and the variance has two parts that do not convert into each other. Counting is one. Radioactive decay is a Poisson process, so a count $N$ carries a standard deviation $\sqrt{N}$ and a relative error

$$\frac{\sigma\_{N}}{N} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.$$

Precision is bought with material and with time, at a rate that punishes: halve the error and you need four times the counts, and those counts are decays the sample will not give again. Closure is the other. The clock assumes the mineral exchanged nothing with its surroundings after it formed, and that assumption is not measured. It is chosen, from the mineral's history, before the measurement is read. Where the material has been altered, closure dominates the counting error, and the published interval is a claim about geology wearing the clothes of a claim about physics.

A date, then, is not a point with noise around it. It is an interval whose width states something about the material's history, and two dates whose intervals overlap are not ordered by their midpoints. A boundary inside an overlap is not dated. It is bracketed, and the bracket is what any later claim about sequence inherits.

The temporal site keeps a second clock, and that one decides whether there is a record at all. A section exists only where accumulation outruns destruction, so the record's presence is itself a rate comparison, and erosion sets the interval's upper edge, not interest. The decay clock runs inside the mineral, the burial clock outside it, and a date is only as good as the claim that the second did not reset the first. Without this site an archive is a list. With it, an archive is a partially ordered set carrying an uncertainty, which is the only form in which deep time can be a measurement instead of a chronology of names.

## 4. The circuit, and the office that is missing

Frame, archive and clock form one circuit. The frame fixes the object, the archive supplies the sample, the clock prices the interval, and each supplies a premise the others cannot check. The frame cannot be tested by the section it defines. The archive cannot price its own gaps without the clock. The clock cannot say what its interval contains without the frame. The signature $(2, 1, 0)$ reads exactly that way: two epistemic sites, one vital, the governance site empty.

What the empty site would have to hold can be said precisely. It would hold the office that answers for the completeness of the record: who must sample the gap, who bears the consequence of a boundary declared on an unpriced gap, who certifies that the section beneath a ratified boundary is adequate to the claim made on it. The body that ratifies a boundary ratifies a definition. No body is charged with the adequacy of the record beneath the definition, and the gap has no owner.

So the failure is not epistemic, and that is this chapter's central claim. The frame is explicit, the archive's bias is priced, the clock's error is published; a reader who wants to know how good the evidence is can find out. What no one can find out is who is obliged to act on any of it. The order can state that a boundary rests on an unpriced gap and still be used, because stating it costs the user nothing.

Collapse Proposition 3 fits without strain ([Chapter 41](41-the-triad-and-the-minimum.md)). *The record is incomplete* is true of every interval, so it excuses any boundary and any rate: an agnotological alibi that cannot be refuted because it is true. The party who bears a wrong boundary — a claim on a reservoir, a liability for a displaced fauna, a date that decides which deposit is whose — is not the party who can commission the sampling. And the reserve, which is the record itself, is treated as free, because its depletion is invisible on the timescale of the decision that depletes it.

The residue this order contributes is the pricing of the gap. Every other order prices an observation. This one prices the absence of observations, and it must, because its object is reachable only through what the substrate kept. A stratigraphy that reported its boundaries and not the adequacy of the sections under them would be a nomenclature, not an order of knowledge.
