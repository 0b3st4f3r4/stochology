## 1. Two clocks and the ratio between them

Imagine a municipal health department on a Monday morning after three clinics call in with matching fever clusters. Lab confirmations take five days and contact interviews take four, while the infection itself doubles every three days. Before any epidemic forecast runs, the city faces a clock race: how fast contagion spreads versus how fast clinics detect and isolate cases.

Under Markovian SIR linearization with constant susceptibility $S > 0$, transmission rate $\beta > 0$, and recovery rate $\gamma > 0$, the mean infectious generation time is $T = 1/\gamma$ (Kermack & McKendrick 1927). Whenever the effective reproduction number satisfies $R\_t = \beta S/\gamma = 1 + rT > 1$, incidence grows exponentially at rate $r = \beta S - \gamma = \gamma(R\_t - 1) > 0$.

That growth fixes two clocks: the doubling time $\tau\_2 = \ln 2/r$, and the remaining time $t\_{\mathrm{cap}} = \frac{1}{r}\ln(C\_{\mathrm{cap}}/I\_0)$ until cases $I\_0 > 0$ hit hospital ceiling $C\_{\mathrm{cap}} > I\_0$ ([Appendix A.2](appendix-a-mathematics.md)). Those two clocks coincide only at half capacity ($I\_0 = C\_{\mathrm{cap}}/2$), and $t\_{\mathrm{cap}}$ shrinks toward zero as $I\_0 \to C\_{\mathrm{cap}}^-$. Doubling $r$ halves both clocks, whereas pushing $R\_t \to 1^+$ sends $\tau\_2 \to +\infty$, separating slower growth ($R\_t > 1$) from containment ($R\_t < 1$).

## 2. What the reported count cannot carry

Raw daily case counts hide three structural failures between the true transmission clock and the public chart.

First, transmission is heavily overdispersed rather than evenly spread. Fitting negative binomial offspring distributions with mean $R\_0$ and variance $\mathrm{Var}(Z) = R\_0(1 + R\_0/k)$ ($k > 0$), Lloyd-Smith, Schreiber, Kopp and Getz (2005) found $\hat{k} = 0.16$ (90% CI `0.11–0.64`) for 2003 Singapore SARS. In their data, 73% of cases had expected secondary count $\nu < 1$ while 6% had $\nu > 8$ (Lloyd-Smith, Schreiber, Kopp & Getz 2005). That skew gives extinction probability $q = 0.76$ at $R\_0 = 3$, versus $q = 0.06$ under Poisson transmission ($k \to \infty$), making the average unrepresentative.

Second, hospital and testing queues choke nonlinearly near capacity. Under queue utilization $\rho \in (0, 1)$, waiting times diverge as $\rho/(1 - \rho)$ ([Layer 02](52-layer-meso-buffered-queue-order.md)), driving $\mathrm{TTR}\_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{I}\_t)$ past $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}}$ well before peak demand arrives.

Third, outbreak clusters correlate observations within households and wards. Clusters of mean size $\bar{m} \ge 1$ and size dispersion $\mathrm{CV}^2(m) \ge 0$ carry effective size $\tilde{m} = \bar{m}(1 + \mathrm{CV}^2(m))$. That clustering inflates variance by $1 + (\tilde{m} - 1)\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}} > 0$ (uniform: $1 + (m - 1)\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}}$; [Layer 09](59-layer-experimental-sampling-causal-order.md)) and reduces prevalence estimates to partial bounds. Without governance accountability, authorities discount early warnings ($\delta\_{\mathrm{status}} \to 1^-$) until the window closes.

## 3. The threshold is not a property of the pathogen

Guard site `T2Severity` tests whether textbook control thresholds—such as the classic herd-immunity fraction $1 - 1/R\_0$—actually suppress transmission once real-world differences in susceptibility are included.

Split a population into two equal halves with susceptibilities $s\_1 = 2a/(a+1) > s\_2 = 2/(a+1)$ for heterogeneity ratio $a > 1$. Under separable mixing and uniform infectiousness, the next-generation matrix $K = (K\_{ij})$ with $K\_{ij} = \frac{R\_0}{2}s\_i$ (Diekmann, Heesterbeek & Metz 1990; distinct from [Chapter 43](43-inquiry-openness-multiverse.md)'s contractual cap $K$) has spectral radius $\rho(K) = \mathrm{tr}(K) = R\_0$. Uniform vaccination ($v\_1 = v\_2 = p$) scales $K\_{\mathrm{vac}} = (1 - p)K$ and preserves $p\_c = 1 - 1/R\_0$, whereas targeted allocation or natural depletion breaks that rule.

Under separate group coverages $v\_1, v\_2 \in [0, 1]$, the post-vaccination spectral radius becomes $\rho(K\_{\mathrm{vac}}) = \frac{R\_0}{a+1}\bigl((1 - v\_1)a + (1 - v\_2)\bigr)$. When $R\_0 > 1 + 1/a$, vaccinating only the less-susceptible group ($v\_2 = 1, v\_1 = 0$) leaves $\rho(K\_{\mathrm{vac}}) = \frac{R\_0 a}{a+1} > 1$, so full coverage of the lower-risk half still fails to halt spread. Only for $1 < R\_0 \le 1 + 1/a$ can lower-risk coverage compensate; outbreak reporting must pair `(group allocation, spectral radius)`.

## 4. Case definitions, phylodynamics, and the archive's edge

Guard site `T3Archive` checks which infections actually enter official registries, test queues, and viral sequence databases before anyone treats reported numbers as population truth.

### Classification and selective recording

An outbreak archive only holds presentations that clear case definition $D$, reporting access, and administrative retention. Classification is organizational before it is descriptive (Bowker & Star 1999): shifting $D$ from clinical syndrome to lab confirmation alters reported cases $N(D)$ at constant transmission, and testing bottlenecks flatten curves while spread accelerates.

Write $Y = 1$ for a true infection and $R \in \lbrace 0, 1\rbrace$ for archival recording. Observed records give $P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1)$ rather than the population share:

$$P(Y = 1) = P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1)P(R = 1) + P(Y = 1 \mid R = 0)P(R = 0).$$

In plain words, total population prevalence mixes the observed rate among recorded people with the unobserved rate among everyone unrecorded, weighted by their population shares.

For interior coverage $P(R = 1) \in (0, 1)$, the selection covariance is $\mathrm{Cov}(R, Y) = P(R = 1)\bigl[P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1) - P(Y = 1)\bigr]$. Recorded prevalence matches true prevalence ($P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1) = P(Y = 1)$) only when selection correlation vanishes ($\rho\_{R,Y} = 0$). Even then, observed incidence $P(Y = 1, R = 1) = P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1)P(R = 1)$ scales with coverage. Because care-seeking tracks symptom severity, $\rho\_{R,Y} \ne 0$ inflates apparent severity and forces Manski (2003) partial-identification bounds.

### Two-stream capture–recapture and genomic trees

Across two surveillance streams capturing $n\_1$ and $n\_2$ cases with $m > 0$ overlapping cases, independence yields Lincoln–Petersen $\hat{N}\_{\mathrm{tot}} = n\_1 n\_2/m$ and unseen count $\hat{n}\_{00,\mathrm{LP}} = (n\_1 - m)(n\_2 - m)/m$. Positively dependent severity streams inflate overlap $m$ and bias $\hat{N}\_{\mathrm{tot}}$ downward. Under symmetric heterogeneous catchability ($M\_h$), Chao (1987), Chao (1989), and Chao & Colwell (2017) give the two-list lower bound $\hat{n}\_{00,\mathrm{Chao2}}(2) = f\_1^2/(4m)$ using single-list counts $f\_1 = n\_1 + n\_2 - 2m$. Yet inter-list dependence, list asymmetry ($M\_t$), and zero-catchability mild strata ($\pi\_i = 0$) still evade both without community sampling.

Genomic phylodynamics hits the exact same boundary (Volz, Koelle & Bedford 2013). At constant population size and unit mean offspring ($\mu = 1$), coalescent effective population size $N\_e = N/\sigma^2$ ($\sigma^2 > 0$) shrinks sharply under SARS-like superspreading ($k = 0.16$). In growing or declining SIR epidemics ($\mu = R\_t \ne 1$), coalescent rate and $N\_e$ depend jointly on incidence $2\beta S(t)I(t)$, prevalence $I(t)$, and depletion $S(t)$. Distinct epidemics can therefore map onto identical phylogenetic trees while hospital-biased sequencing ($\rho\_{R,Y} \ne 0$) and upload lags distort estimated $R\_0$ and migration (Volz, Koelle & Bedford 2013).

### Empirical evidence from arboviruses and nowcasting

Across 50 Americas jurisdictions from 2013 to 2023, de Souza et al. (2024) documented 3,684,554 chikungunya cases (3.9 times Zika's 949,567; 51% vs. 20% symptomatic). Even so, 40.5% of Brazilian municipalities logged zero cases despite 2%–70.5% local seroprevalence (29.5%, 114/387, in Puente de Ixtla, Mexico). Meanwhile, 1,614 genomes (~44 per 100,000 cases) revealed up to nine months of cryptic circulation, requiring One Health surveillance of *Aedes* vectors and peri-domestic primates along external reception perimeters ($\Theta\_{\mathrm{basin}}$; de Souza et al. 2024).

Archival retention also drifts across changing reporting vintages (Trouillot 1995). At frontier $\tau$, onset-to-display lag $\Delta$ right-truncates recent counts ($\Delta \le \tau - t\_{\mathrm{ev}}$; [Chapter 45](45-world-to-archive-big-data-paradox.md)), while recognizing an outbreak changes the reporting delay itself. In Lazio's 2017 chikungunya outbreak (414 notified; 412 with onset), Manica et al. (2023) found 189 cases (45.9%) had symptom onset before official detection on 6 September 2017 (earliest 72 days prior). Official recognition cut median reporting lag from 33 days (IQR 19–52) to 8 days post-detection (IQR 5–15; overall 15.5; Cox HR 3.45, 95% CI 2.80–4.25), yet only 3.6% of post-detection cases arrived within 24 h and 17% overall (0.9% vs. 30.7%) within the 5–8-day infectious window (Manica et al. 2023).

Nowcasting corrects both right-truncation ($\Delta > \tau - t\_{\mathrm{ev}}$) and missing onset dates. In Madrid (March–April 2020), De Salazar et al. (2022) found missing onsets in 53% of 32,723 early and 16% of 56,057 late SARS-CoV-2 reports (Murcia: 25% of 831 and 11% of 1,602). Imputation paired with `NobBS` recovered 16 March peaks ahead of 25–26 March reports and $R\_t < 1$ a week earlier (vs. 6–9 days early for unadjusted onsets), contingent on MAR onsets and stable ascertainment $f(t)$ (De Salazar et al. 2022). Generalized additive nowcasts (`GAM`) adjust line-lists for delay, location, and weekday ahead of lagging reports while remaining vulnerable to shifting prevalence and testing (Tang et al. 2025). Bitemporal custody logs $(t\_{\mathrm{ev}}, t\_{\mathrm{disp}})$, $(N(D), d/T)$, and conditional nowcasts together.

Write reported cases as $N(t) = f(t)I(t)$ with positive ascertainment fraction $f(t) > 0$ and true incidence $I(t) > 0$. Taking the log-time derivative separates epidemic growth from testing drift:

$$\frac{d\ln N}{dt} = \frac{d\ln I}{dt} + \frac{d\ln f}{dt},$$

Read aloud, the growth rate of reported cases equals true infection growth plus the growth rate of testing coverage. Thus expanding testing raises $d\ln N/dt$ even when transmission is flat. Stripped of case definition $D$ and $f(t)$, that metric travels by hiding local judgment (Porter 1995), leaving true growth $d\ln I/dt$ inside Manski (2003) bounds absent community surveys.

## 5. The two clocks as one ratio

Guard site `T5Temporal` compares how fast isolation and contact tracing remove infectious people against how fast the pathogen spreads from person to person.

### Generation intervals versus serial intervals

Let $T > 0$ be the **mean generation interval** ($G\_{ij} > 0$, infection-to-infection). Its observed proxy, the symptom-to-symptom serial interval $S\_{ij} = G\_{ij} + U\_j - U\_i$, matches $T$ in expectation when incubation times balance ($\mathbb{E}[U\_j] = \mathbb{E}[U\_i]$), and can turn negative ($S\_{ij} < 0$) under pre-symptomatic spread ($U\_i > G\_{ij} + U\_j$).

Under independent i.i.d. incubation with positive variance $\mathrm{Var}(U) > 0$, serial variance exceeds generation variance ($\mathrm{Var}(S) = \mathrm{Var}(G) + 2\mathrm{Var}(U) > \mathrm{Var}(G)$). Plugging serial intervals into the Euler–Lotka equation ($1/R\_0 = \int\_0^\infty e^{-r\tau}g(\tau)\mkern3mu d\tau$ for $R\_0 > 0$) therefore underestimates $R\_0$ whenever $r > 0$ (Wallinga & Lipsitch 2007).

By Jensen's inequality, $R\_0 \le e^{rT}$, reaching equality only in the deterministic limit $\tau \equiv T$ where $r = (\ln R\_0)/T$ and $N(t + d) = N(t)\mkern3mu e^{rd} = N(t)\mkern3mu R\_0^{d/T}$. For any positive-variance generation law at $r > 0$—such as uniform on $[0, 2T]$ giving $R\_0 = 2rT/(1 - e^{-2rT}) < e^{rT}$ or §1's Markovian limit $R\_0 = 1 + rT$—actual growth satisfies $r > (\ln R\_0)/T$. Consequently, $N(t)\mkern3mu R\_0^{d/T}$ acts strictly as a conservative lower bound on $N(t)\mkern3mu e^{rd}$.

### Symptomatic isolation versus contact tracing

Containment separates **symptomatic isolation alone** from **isolation plus contact tracing** along post-onset delay $d = d\_{\mathrm{detect}} + d\_{\mathrm{interview}} + d\_{\mathrm{trace}} \ge 0$. Under symptomatic isolation alone with efficacy $\varepsilon\_I \in [0, 1]$ and pre-symptomatic transmission share $\theta \in [0, 1]$ (Fraser, Riley, Anderson & Ferguson 2004), even instant isolation leaves $R\_I = R\_0(1 - \varepsilon\_I + \varepsilon\_I\theta) \ge \theta R\_0$. Over infectious post-onset window $T\_{\mathrm{post}} > 0$ under uniform post-onset infectiousness with finite delay $d\_{\mathrm{iso}} = d\_{\mathrm{detect}} + d\_{\mathrm{act}} \ge 0$, effective reproduction reads $R\_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{iso}} = R\_0\bigl(\theta + (1 - \theta)\min(1, d\_{\mathrm{iso}}/T\_{\mathrm{post}})\bigr) \ge \theta R\_0$, failing whenever $\theta R\_0 > 1$.

Strictly asymptomatic carriers evade symptom-indexed isolation and tracing altogether without active community screening. Contact tracing ($\varepsilon\_T > 0$) breaks through the $\theta R\_0$ ceiling—delivering up to a fourfold further reduction under efficient isolation (Fraser et al. 2004)—whenever $d/T$ quarantines exposed contacts before they transmit. Because $d$ starts at symptom onset, unmeasured detection delay $d\_{\mathrm{detect}}$ (Manica et al. 2023) and missing onset dates (De Salazar et al. 2022) stall $d/T$ even if interview times $d\_{\mathrm{interview}}$ drop; sound policy requires reporting $(N(D), d/T)$ together.
