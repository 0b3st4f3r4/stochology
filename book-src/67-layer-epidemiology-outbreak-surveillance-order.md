## 1. Two clocks and the ratio between them

While $R\_t = \beta S/\gamma$ stays above one, the count grows at the rate $r = \beta S - \gamma$, so the survival
clock is $\mathrm{TTS}\_{\mathrm{survival}} = \ln 2/r$. Double the growth rate and the clock halves. A report that quotes a
doubling time has therefore quoted no horizon at all. Flattening the curve and ending the outbreak
are two different statements, and a report promising the second while delivering the first has published a division
by a number tending to zero.

## 2. What the reported count cannot carry

Between the series and the clock stand three failures, none of them a measurement error. Transmissions are overdispersed, so a dispersion well below one
leaves large outbreaks explosive. Capacity is a queue, so waiting time rises as $\rho/(1-\rho)$ and recovery
passes the survival clock. And the archive is not a random sample: selection into it is non-ignorable,
clusters inflate variance by $1 + (m-1)\rho\_{\mathrm{cl}}$, prevalence is a
partial-identification bound. With no governance site in
the triple, authorities discount early clinical warnings and spend the window the clock had already priced.

## 3. The threshold is not a property of the pathogen

Both standard uses of $1 - 1/R\_0$ fail once susceptibility is heterogeneous. Take two equal groups whose
susceptibilities differ by a factor, normalised so that the mean equals the same $R\_0$ as the homogeneous case. The
next-generation matrix has equal entries within each row, so it is rank one and its spectral radius is its trace,
which is the very $R\_0$ the homogeneous formula assumes. Immunise the susceptible half and the outbreak goes out; apply the same coverage to the other half and
the radius stays above one, demanding a much larger share of that
group. For one pathogen the homogeneous formula returns a third answer. What the threshold is a function
of is the radius, not the coverage, so report the threshold as the pair (coverage, spectral radius) and not as coverage alone.

## 4. The case definition is the archive's edge

A surveillance archive does not hold cases. It holds records, and a record exists only where three
decisions coincide: that the presentation counted as a case, that the case reached a reporting site, and
that what the site entered survived into the series a reader can obtain. The case definition is the first
of the three, and it is the edge of the archive in the strict sense. Everything on one side of the line
is counted, everything on the other side is not, and the line itself is drawn in a document rather than
found in a patient. Bowker & Star (1999) argue that a classification is an
organizational achievement before it is a descriptive one, and that the residual category a scheme creates
is where the unclassified work disappears. Change the definition — admit a presentation only on a
confirmed laboratory result, or admit it on clinical grounds alone — and the count moves while nothing
in the world does. The archive publishes $N(D)$ for a definition $D$. A count published
without $D$ is a number whose denominator is a decision.

The definition also imports an instrument, and the instrument has a ceiling. Admit a
presentation only on a confirmed laboratory result and the count becomes a function of laboratory throughput: the
series saturates at whatever the laboratories can process in a week. Past that ceiling the count stops
growing for a reason that has nothing to do with transmission, and in the series alone the flat segment is
indistinguishable from a flat segment produced by control. This is the most
expensive form of the archival defect, because it arrives when the epidemic is largest and because
it flatters: the curve bends while the world does not. A report that draws its horizon from such a series
has read the counter's limit as the pathogen's.

The archival site has its own vocabulary for what such a count carries, and it is not the vocabulary of
transmission. Call *coverage* the share of events that generate a record at all, *retention* the share of
records that persist into the series, and $R$ the indicator that an event was recorded. What the archive
estimates is $P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1)$, the prevalence among records; what a reader needs is $P(Y = 1)$, the
prevalence among events. The two are related by

$$P(Y = 1) = P(Y = 1 \mid R = 1)P(R = 1) + P(Y = 1 \mid R = 0)P(R = 0),$$

and the second summand is unobserved. Hence Manski (2003) supplies the honest form of the statement
as a bound rather than an estimate. Let $\rho\_{R,Y}$ be the correlation between the recording indicator
and the outcome. At $\rho\_{R,Y} = 0$ the recorded share is a scaled copy of the world, biased only by the
unknown factor $P(R = 1)$. At $\rho\_{R,Y} \ne 0$ the scaling has a direction, and in surveillance the
direction is systematic, because a severe presentation reaches a reporting site and a mild one does not.
The archive tilts toward severity and away from the mild cases that carry most of the
transmission, so its severity is not the epidemic's.

Retention adds a second edge behind the first, and the archival vocabulary insists on it
because it acts after publication. A record that enters the series can be revised,
reclassified or removed: a death certificate re-coded when the autopsy returns, a case struck when a
second test is negative, a backlog entered in one week against onsets spread over the previous month. What
a reader obtains is therefore not a photograph of a count but the current state of a document that
is still being written, and a reported figure is an estimate of a fixed past produced by a moving archive.
Retention matters most where the archive is thinnest: a correction that moves a negligible share of
the series is noise, and one that moves a material share of it is the count. Trouillot (1995) separates
the silences that the making of sources produces from the silences that the making of narratives adds,
and the distinction is exact here. The definitional edge is a silence of the first kind. A revision that
overwrites an earlier figure without publishing both is a silence of the second.

A level shift would be the least of it, because coverage moves. Write the series as $N(t) = f(t)I(t)$, the
reporting fraction times the incidence, and take logarithms:

$$\frac{d\ln N}{dt} = \frac{d\ln I}{dt} + \frac{d\ln f}{dt}.$$

The growth rate read off the archive is the epidemic's rate plus the growth rate of attention. A testing
programme that expands, a definition that widens, a reporting channel that becomes mandatory — each
raises $f$, and each raises the apparent $r$ with no change in the pathogen. Read the
epidemiological threshold against the record, then, and not against the pathogen. The quantity
$1 - 1/R\_0$ has a pathogen in it, but the $R\_0$ a series supplies has the recording process in it too,
and the series alone cannot separate them. Porter (1995) gives the mechanism by which the
unseparated number nevertheless travels: a figure is trusted across sites precisely because it arrives
stripped of the local judgement that produced it. A count without its definition is not a weaker claim
than a count with one. It is a claim whose support has been removed in transit, and its portability is
what the removal buys.

The second series that would separate the two rates is what the corpus does not supply. Decomposing
$d\ln N/dt$ into the epidemic's growth and the growth of attention requires $f$ measured against something
other than the archive, and a survey that visits households independently of whether they reported is
exactly the instrument a surveillance system does not run, because its cost is the cost of counting the
same population twice. Absent that series the two summands are not identified from the record. The
honest statement is the one Manski (2003) licenses: a bound on $r$ whose width is the range of plausible
attention growth, not a point estimate of the pathogen's rate.

## 5. The two clocks as one ratio

Two parameters travel in every report. The serial interval, written $T$, is the mean time between successive infections in a chain of
transmission. The tracing delay, written $d$, is the time from a case being detected to its contacts being
reached and isolated. One is epidemiological, one operational, and neither is a governance object on its own.
They are one quantity. Suppose infectiousness is spread evenly
across the serial interval, so that a case transmits at the rate $1/T$ until time $T$. By time $d$ it has
spent the share $d/T$ of its transmission potential, and isolation at that moment leaves that
share realized and prevents the rest:

$$R\_{\mathrm{eff}} = R\min\left(1,\mkern3mu \frac{d}{T}\right).$$

Only the ratio enters. Double both clocks and $R\_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is unchanged, so two jurisdictions with
different serial intervals and different delays can be identical in control and opposite in report. The
same ratio governs how far the epidemic has run while the delay elapses. With $r = \ln R\_0/T$ the growth
rate implied by the generation interval, the case count at the moment contacts are reached is

$$N(t + d) = N(t)\mkern3mu e^{rd} = N(t)\mkern3mu R\_0^{d/T},$$

so $d/T$ is an exponent, not a comparison: the number of case-generations that pass during the delay,
and the factor by which the epidemic has multiplied over them. A delay equal to the serial interval leaves
the case's realized transmission at the unmitigated reproduction number, which is the statement that
tracing that slow has prevented nothing.

The numerator is where the two sites meet, because $d$ is not an operational constant either. It decomposes
into the delay from onset to detection, the delay from detection to interview, and the delay from interview
to the contacts being reached. The first of those is archival: it is the time it takes for
an event to become a record. So the ratio carries the archive in its numerator and the pathogen in its
denominator, and the defect of §4 and the ratio above are one defect seen
from two sides. A system that shortens interviews while its detection delay grows has improved the part of $d$
it manages and left the ratio where it was, and the improvement is reportable.

The quotient is also the only form in which the two clocks can be compared at all. $T$ and $d$ carry the
same unit, so their ratio is dimensionless: it means the same thing in a setting whose serial interval is
long and whose delay is proportionally long, and in one where both are short. Compare delays
across two settings and you compare two operational systems. Compare serial intervals and you compare two
pathogens. Neither comparison answers the question a minister asks, which is whether contact tracing
is working here. $d/T$ answers it. The ratio is the only governable quantity in the pair, and
the only one that policy can be held to.

The governance failure is a choice of which clock to report, and it does not look like a choice because both
numbers are true. $T$ is a property of the pathogen: it moves slowly, it is not the surveillance system's
to lose, and quoting it describes the world. $d$ is a queue, and §2 already priced the queue, so it moves
with load and quoting it describes the system. A bulletin that reports the serial interval through a surge
reads as reassurance while the ratio collapses, and every figure in it is accurate. With no governance
site in the local triple, no local site is charged with publishing the ratio: the laboratory owns $T$, the tracing
service owns $d$, and the quotient belongs to neither, so nobody publishes it without drawing on upstream governance orders. The governance axial exposure is not
a number that was suppressed. It is a number no local site had the standing to compute — which is what a
local governance axial exposure looks like when the epistemic and vital margins are both intact and the archive is
in order.

## Exercises

1. With overdispersed transmission and a positive household correlation, show why unadjusted symptomatic test positivity misestimates prevalence, and say which of the two archive defects carries the error.
2. Taking tracing utilisation to $\rho \to 1$, say what happens to containment when the recovery time passes the generation interval.
3. Show how the survival clock scales with the growth rate, and say which rate makes a one-week delay survivable (answer: the clock halves at each doubling of the rate, so only the slowest buys the week).
4. Explain why a ninety per cent effective measure arriving after four doublings has cost about fifteen times the cases it prevents (answer: four doublings accumulate $2^4 - 1 = 15$ times the starting count).
5. Take a two-group matrix of §3's form, immunise the less susceptible half instead, and compute the coverage the homogeneous formula would have accepted beside the coverage the radius demands.
