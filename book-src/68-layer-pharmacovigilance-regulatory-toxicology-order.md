## 1. The rule of three and the trial that cannot see the harm

The rule of three is what an exact bound becomes in the limit. A study that observes no event excludes any rate above
$-\ln(0.05)/n$ at ninety-five per cent confidence, so expecting one event at probability $p$ asks for about
$3/p$ subjects. A pre-market programme screens a few thousand subjects, enough for events far more common than the ones post-market surveillance exists to find, and no more. Authorization moves exposure to tens
of millions. The surveillance that replaces the trial is a different instrument on a different population.

## 2. Anytime validity, and what it does not buy

Spontaneous reports arrive continuously, and fixed-sample testing has no answer for that. Twenty weekly tests at a nominal five
per cent each leave a family-wise error of $1 - 0.95^{20}$, above one half. Anytime validity compares a
running likelihood ratio against $1/\alpha$ instead, so a stream with a fixed per-look ratio crosses after
$\lceil\ln(1/\alpha)/\ln(\text{per-look ratio})\rceil$ looks, at the same guarantee. That guarantee
says nothing about whether the archive is a random sample. Clinicians file only a fraction of adverse
reactions, and Chao's bound $f\_1^2/(2f\_2)$ puts unseen reaction classes in the tail.

## 3. Lucidity without a stop-work trigger

Layer 18 omits formalization and the survival clock, so its regime is the second collapse. A regulator
can hold a sequentially valid signal, an unambiguous archive and undisputed authority to suspend a licence, and
still watch exposure accumulate, because deliberation runs over years while millions of patients fill
prescriptions daily. The authority grants permission. It carries no clock.

## 4. The signal the archive licensed

The form licenses a sequence: detect with anytime validity, corroborate in the archive, act with statutory
authority. Rosiglitazone is a case in which every step was taken and the recommendation was wrong. A 2007
meta-analysis of the published record reported an odds ratio for myocardial infarction of 1.43 with an interval
of $[1.03, 1.98]$ pooled over trials designed for other endpoints (Nissen & Wolski 2007). On the log scale the
interval $[\ln 1.03,\mkern3mu \ln 1.98]$ has total width $\ln(1.98/1.03)$ and lower half-width $\ln(1.43/1.03)$, while
its lower endpoint clears the null $\ln 1 = 0$ by only $\ln(1.03)$, a small fraction of that half-width, so a modest
selection or reporting shift suffices to push the lower bound across unity.

A meta-analysis of published trials is a stream
whose inclusion rule is a function of the results, so the trigger must be conditioned on the provenance of the
archive. Two features of that provenance bound the correction. A report is not a report of an incidence:
under-reporting is the rule, not the exception, and its rate moves with the drug, the reaction and the
era (Hazell & Shakir 2006), so a count drawn from the archive carries a denominator nobody observed. And
corroboration is a coherence test, not a statistical one. Bradford Hill's criteria ask whether an
association coheres with evidence accumulated elsewhere, and a signal that passes coherence is still a
signal. That is why the last step of the sequence is a recall veto and not a further estimate.

## 5. The severity a report cannot carry

A spontaneous reporting system is not a sample of patients. It is a sample of decisions: a clinician
decides a reaction is worth a form, a pharmacist decides the form is worth forwarding, a company decides
the case is worth entering, and the archive is the residue of those decisions. The sampling mechanism is
the reporting indicator itself, and every quantity the archive yields is conditional on it.
Taking severity as $S$ and the reporting indicator as $R$, what the archive supplies is $E[S \mid R = 1]$,
the mean severity among reported cases; what a prescriber needs is $E[S]$. The relation between them
is the law of total expectation,

$$E[S] = E[S \mid R = 1]P(R = 1) + E[S \mid R = 0]P(R = 0),$$

in which the first factor of the second summand is unobserved and the second factor is unrecorded. The
conditional severity is all the archive holds, and it belongs to the decision to report before it belongs
to the drug.

Severity is coded before it is counted, and the code is a decision of the same order as the case
definition. A reaction term in a dictionary fixes how fine a description must be before it is entered. It
therefore decides whether one presentation is one case or two, and whether a presentation that resembles a known
reaction is recorded as that reaction or as a new one. Bowker & Star (1999) show that such schemes are
built to make some work visible and are indifferent to what falls between their categories. For severity that
indifference is not neutral: a scheme with a coarse term for a common mild event will absorb
the severe atypical case into the mild category, and the mean the archive reports will be the mean of a
population the coding produced. The limit here is not statistical either. Recoding the dictionary is not
a larger sample of the same variable; it is a different variable.

Nor can a constant correct the tilt, because its sign is not fixed across reactions. A dramatic
event is over-reported relative to its incidence, since it is memorable and it is not the disease being
treated. An event that resembles the underlying condition is under-reported to the point of invisibility,
because nothing distinguishes it from the reason the prescription was written. A stable reporting rate
would be a nuisance parameter and could be carried as one. A rate that moves with the drug, the reaction
and the era cannot, and Hazell & Shakir (2006) report precisely that movement across the published
estimates of under-reporting. The archive is not thereby unreliable. Its
severity estimate is an estimate of a different random variable than the one the decision needs.

The same substitution governs the rate. A rate requires a denominator of exposure, and the only
denominator a spontaneous archive holds is other reports. A disproportionality ratio therefore compares
the share of a reaction among reports for one drug with its share among reports for everything else,

$$\mathrm{PRR} = \frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)},$$

where $a$ counts reports of the reaction for the drug, $b$ the drug's other reports, and $c$ and $d$ the
same two counts over the rest of the archive. All four cells count decisions, so the ratio is invariant to
under-reporting only where under-reporting is equal in the two columns — the one condition the
evidence refuses. Patient-time of exposure would make the quantity a rate, and that is the
quantity no spontaneous system records. It is missing by design, not by oversight, because the
system was built to collect suspicions, not to enumerate the exposed. So the bound on severity among the
exposed does not narrow as reports accumulate: more reports sharpen the conditional distribution
and leave the conditioning untouched. The archive can carry a signal. It can never carry a rate.

## 6. The veto that has to fire before the signal is strong

The recall decision is not an inference. It is a liability act, and Layer 18 carries the liability site
with no survival clock, which fixes the shape of the choice. A regulator holds a veto. The archive supplies
a signal that is sequentially valid and uninterpretable as a rate. The question is not whether the
association is established but whether to act before it is. Two errors are available, and they are not
priced in the same currency.

Acting on a weak signal withdraws an exposure that was, for the patients who needed the drug, doing work.
That cost is paid once, at the moment of withdrawal, and it falls on the patients who lose the
indication and on the manufacturer whose product stops earning. Waiting pays per unit of exposure: the cost
accrues with every prescription filled while the review proceeds, and it falls on the people taking the
drug, who have the least standing to see the ledger. The first cost is visible in the archive,
because a withdrawal generates its own records and its own litigation. The second is not, because the harm
it produces enters the archive as exactly the kind of decision §5 described, and a harm that was never
filed is a harm the record cannot price.

The asymmetry is temporal, not evidential, and that is why the veto cannot wait for strength.
Write $f$ for the harmed fraction per unit of exposure and $E(t)$ for cumulative exposure. The cost of
waiting is proportional to $f\mkern3mu E(t)$ and grows for as long as the review runs; the cost of acting
is fixed at the moment of the act. Comparing them compares a growing quantity against a settled
one, and the growing quantity is the one the triple has no site for. With the vital margin absent, no
ledger in the architecture prices $E(t)$, so the comparison the decision requires is the comparison the
architecture cannot make. That is Proposition 2 in its exact form: the signal is valid, the authority is
undisputed, and the clock that would make waiting expensive is missing from the instrument.

Who bears each cost completes the geometry. The manufacturer bears the cost of acting, the patient bears
the cost of waiting, and the regulator bears neither while holding the veto. A decision maker who is not
the residual claimant of either error will choose the error that is cheap for the decision maker, and the
liability site is what converts the delayed harm into a claim afterwards — after the exposure has been
paid for by the people who could not file it. To require the veto to wait for a strong signal is therefore
to require the archive to carry a severity it cannot carry and a rate it cannot compute. It is to wait
for evidence that only the harm can produce.

The two sites are also coupled in the direction that matters for the veto, because the notification duty
makes a report an entry and a notice at once. Where filing a case is the act that discharges a duty, the
propensity to file is a function of anticipated liability rather than of clinical interest. The archive
thickens where legal exposure is highest and thins where the manufacturer has least to fear. So the severity
the veto must read is produced by the liability the veto exists to allocate, and the loop has no
fixed point inside the archive: a company that reports defensively raises the apparent severity of its own
product, a company that reports minimally lowers it, and the harmed fraction the asymmetry above
turns on stays where it was. The shape of the reporting system is itself a governance
question, and a triple that carries liability with no site for the exposure cannot audit the very
signal it acts on.

The historical record holds one ledger for each error. Neustadt & Fineberg (1983) reconstruct a mass
programme launched on a forecast and unwound when the forecast failed, and what that record prices is the
cost of acting on a weak signal, including the cost of having acted. Phillips, Bridgeman & Ferguson-Smith
(2000) inquire into a harm that accumulated while the official assessment judged the evidence insufficient
to act, and whatever else the inquiry establishes, it prices the second error for a record that had to be
commissioned after the exposure rather than before it. What the corpus does not fix, and what no site in
this architecture can compute, is the point at which the veto should fire. The strength at which acting
becomes cheaper than waiting is a function of the exposure the vital plane is missing, so the threshold is
stated here as a structural requirement, not as a value.
