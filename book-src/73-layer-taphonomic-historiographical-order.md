## 1. The record is a residue, not a population

Before taphonomy had a name, the fossil was read as a specimen of life: what the rock contained was taken as a diminished but faithful copy of what had lived. Efremov gave the passage its own object, treating the transition of remains from the living world into the buried one as a process with laws of its own, not as a loss of information about something else (Efremov 1940). The reclassification is the whole of this chapter. Once the passage is a process, the archive is its output, and an output is not a sample of the input.

Write the chain as four assemblages: what lived, what died, what was buried, what was found. A unit of the first reaches the fourth only by surviving every stage, so its probability of appearing in the record is a product

$$P(S \mid A) = P(D \mid A)\mkern3muP(B \mid D)\mkern3muP(S \mid B),$$

with $A$ the living assemblage, $D$ the death assemblage, $B$ the buried one and $S$ the sampled one. The structure carries the argument, not any particular value. A factor at zero is absorbing: a taxon whose hard parts dissolve in the deposit's chemistry is absent from $S$ however abundant it was in $A$, and no amount of further digging multiplies a zero by anything but zero.

Two failures are easily confused here, and the order exists because they differ. A collection defect is a departure of what was found from what was there to be found: unsystematic excavation, a screen with too coarse a mesh, a register that loses the provenience of the piece it records. That is a defect of the measurement, correctable in principle by measuring better, and it sits inside the archive as noise rather than as bias. The taphonomic filter is not that. It is a property of the ground, the chemistry, the transport that carried the grains, the institution that decided what was worth keeping. It does not make the record wrong. It makes the record a record of something other than the population, and no improvement in excavation touches it, because the units it removed were removed before the excavator arrived.

The consequence is a prohibition, not a technique. No unbiased estimator of the living assemblage is available from the archive alone. What the archive supports is a statement about the surviving corpus, and any statement about the past is that statement conditional on the filter. So the taphonomic site belongs to the epistemic axis, not to the axis of method: the filter is a fact about the world's admission policy, not about the archaeologist's diligence.

## 2. Deep time: the rate belongs to the window

Survival is attrition, and attrition compounds. If a fraction $p$ of a class survives one interval of deposition, the fraction surviving $n$ such intervals is $p^{n}$, because each interval applies the same conditional loss to whatever the previous one left. In continuous time, with a per-unit-time hazard $\lambda$, the same product becomes

$$S(T) = e^{-\lambda T},$$

and the quantity a site reports as a survival rate is this function evaluated at one particular $T$: the age of the deposit, not a constant of the material.

That is the deep-time site, and its content is an invariance failure. Take two classes with hazards $\lambda\_{1} < \lambda\_{2}$, so the first is the more durable. The ratio of their survivals over a window of length $T$ is

$$\frac{S\_{1}(T)}{S\_{2}(T)} = e^{(\lambda\_{2} - \lambda\_{1})T},$$

which tends to one as $T$ tends to zero and grows without bound as $T$ grows. Two conclusions follow, and both are ordinary errors of historiography rather than of statistics. First, a comparison of durability conducted on young deposits systematically understates the divergence that governs old ones: at a young site the durable and the perishable look nearly alike, because neither has had time to be sorted out. Second, the same class appears durable in a young archive and rare in an old one, and a historian who compares the two abundances without the exponent compares two different functions of two different windows as though they were one material property.

The window truncates at both ends. What a record can show of first and last appearance is bounded by the span it observes, so a class that existed before the record opens and after it closes will appear to have originated and vanished inside it. The truncation and its correction belong to Layer 15, where the width of the observed span and the number of dated intervals set the size of the adjustment. What this order adds is what the correction means for the historian's claim. A corrected end of a window is not a date at which something happened. It is the earliest date the record could have shown, and reporting it as an event converts the shape of the observation into a shape of the world.

The same arithmetic runs the other way through the deposit's chemistry, and there the order's first site and its second meet. The hazard is indexed by burial condition, so a single missingness correlation asserted for a whole civilisation is a category error. The correlation is a property of a site, and a stratum that averages over deposits averages over admission policies that have nothing in common. The variance of that average is not a sampling variance and does not shrink with more sites. It is a spread of regimes.

## 3. Liability: survival read as representativeness

The third site is where the first two become a claim someone signs. A corpus share is a statement about the record; a population share is a statement about the past; and the passage between them is an inference that has to be argued, not a reading that can be taken.

Let $n\_{1}$ be the count of a class in the surviving corpus of size $n$. The corpus share is available by division, $\hat{p}\_{\text{corpus}} = n\_{1}/n$, and it estimates the composition of the corpus with no assumption at all. The population share is not available by division, because the denominator that would make it one does not exist in the record. What exists instead is the survival probability of the class, and the population share is the corpus share reweighted by its reciprocal:

$$p\_{\text{assemblage}} \propto \frac{n\_{1}}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{P(S \mid \text{class } 1)}.$$

Everything the site requires is in that reciprocal, and nothing in the record supplies it. The weight is a hypothesis about the filter, of the same kind as the correlation the earlier chapter made the object of its correction: defined against a population the archive cannot see, and therefore declared rather than estimated. The order's discipline is to keep the two statements apart in the sentence that carries them: a share of what survived, or a share of what lived, with the filter that licenses the second printed beside it.

Three duties follow, and they are duties of the page, not of the conscience. The window must be printed: the age of the deposit and the interval the record actually observes, because a rate without its window is a number with no argument behind it. The direction of the filter must be printed: which classes the deposit favours (the durable, the abundant, the ones deposited where the chemistry preserves, the ones an institution had a reason to keep), because a bias whose direction is unnamed cannot be corrected by a reader and will be read as noise. And where no filter model is available, the share must be printed as a corpus share and left there, because a promotion performed silently is the defect the site names.

The liability is not blame for the ground. Nobody is at fault for the chemistry of a deposit, and the historian did not build the archive. What the historian does is sign the inference that reads survival as representativeness, and that signature is the object of the site. Its instrument is the seal: the claim carries its own inclusion rule, so a later reader can recompute the frame instead of inheriting it. A claim that prints its window and its filter can be corrected by the next generation. A claim that prints only the conclusion can only be repeated or rejected.

One case makes the passage no inference at all, and it must be distinguished rather than celebrated. Where the target of the claim is the archive itself — the history of the collection, the provenance of the fond, the composition of what the museum holds — the preserved fraction is one by definition, the estimand is the surviving corpus, and no reweighting is owed. That case is legitimate and narrow, and its failure mode is the promotion running backwards: a corpus share quietly read as a statement about the past because the corpus was the subject of the sentence. The null case is equally narrow. Where survival is independent of class, the corpus is a sample and the corpus share is the population share; but that independence is a result to be shown, and it is never the default.

## 4. One circuit, one guard per axis

The three sites are usually taught as three literatures — taphonomy in paleontology, stratigraphy in geology, source criticism in history — and the guard combination states that they are one circuit.

Which part of the world entered is the archival site. How the entering rate moves with the window is the temporal site. Who answers for reading the residue as the whole is the site of liability. Repair at one is undone at another. A perfect filter model applied to a corpus of unknown age corrects the wrong quantity. A correctly dated correction applied to a corpus whose filter direction is unnamed moves the estimate in an unknown direction. An honestly labelled corpus share, printed as a corpus share, is not yet a historical claim. It is the input such a claim would need, and the historian who stops there has answered a different question than the one the page poses.

The signature `(1, 1, 1)` says something the neighbouring orders of this stratum do not. One guard sits on each axis: no site is doubled, none is missing. That is why the order is the stratum's trans-triadic keystone — the only combination in which the epistemic, the vital and the governance axes each carry exactly one site, so the circuit is intact rather than merely connected. The neighbours with a `(2, 0, 1)` signature carry two epistemic sites and no vital one; they can be lucid and still have no clock. Here the clock is present as the window, and the governance site is present as the seal. So the order fails in three distinct ways rather than one: strip the archival site and the chapter becomes a census of a record pretending to be the history of a world; strip the temporal site and it becomes an undated correction, a durability claim that holds only at the age where it was measured; strip the liability site and the promotion is made with nobody answerable for it.

None of the three sites can be repaired by the other two, and that is the operational content of the signature. A better clock does not tell you which part of the world entered. A better filter model does not tell you who signs. And an impeccable seal on a claim that never printed its window certifies a number whose argument is missing.

## By the end of this chapter you can

1. Distinguish a taphonomic filter from a collection defect, and say why no improvement in excavation removes the bias the filter produces.
2. Derive the dependence of a survival rate on the observed window, and say what a durability comparison conducted on young deposits licenses and what it does not.
3. Keep a corpus share and a population share apart in the sentence that carries them, and state the three things a claim must print for a later reader to recompute its frame.
