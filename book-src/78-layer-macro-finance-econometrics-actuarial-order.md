## 1. Ruin under light and heavy tails

Lundberg (1903) and Cramér (1930) wrote the surplus of an insurer as $U(t) = u + ct - \sum\_{i=1}^{N(t)} X\_i$ with premium rate $c$, Poisson claims of intensity $\lambda$ and sizes $X\_i$, and under the net profit condition $c > \lambda\mathbb{E}[X]$ the adjustment coefficient $R > 0$ solves $\lambda(\mathbb{E}[e^{RX}] - 1) = cR$, giving $\psi(u) = \mathbb{P}(\inf\_{t\ge 0} U(t) < 0) \le e^{-Ru}$.

The bound is a theorem about light tails. Loss-severity archives in property and fire insurance are heavy-tailed, and extreme-value fits to them return a positive shape parameter rather than the zero a light-tailed law requires (McNeil 1997). Where the claim tail decays as a power $x^{-\alpha}$, every exponential moment diverges and the adjustment coefficient does not exist; for $\alpha > 1$ the subexponential result leaves $\psi(u) \propto u^{-(\alpha - 1)}$ in place of the exponential decay, the integrated tail of the claim law taking the place of the exponential (Embrechts, Klüppelberg & Mikosch 1997). Below $\alpha = 2$ the regime also changes what a sample can do. The variance is infinite, so the mean has no standard error at all; by the generalised central limit theorem its typical deviation shrinks as $n^{-(1 - 1/\alpha)}$ rather than $n^{-1/2}$, and halving it takes $2^{\alpha/(\alpha - 1)}$ times the data instead of four times, a factor that diverges as $\alpha$ falls toward one. A capital rule calibrated on a light-tailed model is under-provisioned not by a margin but by a category.

The exponent repays working by hand, since for one claim law the transcendental equation has a fraction for a root. With $X \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\mu)$ the equation reduces to $\lambda\mu R/(1 - \mu R) = cR$, so $R = \theta/((1+\theta)\mu)$ in the safety loading $\theta = c/(\lambda\mu) - 1$. The exponent rises with the loading and falls with the mean claim, so loading and reserve are substitutes chosen with a formula, and a thin-tail world turns either one into an exponential reduction in the probability of ruin. On the heavy-tailed side the exponent does not exist. With an index between one and two the Pareto rate makes ruin decay as a power of the reserve, so doubling the reserve multiplies the ruin probability by $2^{-(\alpha - 1)}$ rather than dividing it by an exponential: a hundred percent more capital buys a percentage reduction instead of orders of magnitude.

The same gap separates the average over accounts from the path of one account. For a price following $dS/S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, Itô's lemma gives $d\ln S = (\mu - \sigma^{2}/2)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, so the ensemble drift sits above the time-average growth by the variance drain $\sigma^{2}/2$. The growth rate of a multiplicative process is the mean of its log increments, so a positive arithmetic mean is compatible with a geometric growth rate reduced by half the variance, and an account sized on the ensemble average is sized on a quantity the account never experiences.

## 2. Coherent measures, endogenous risk, and the outlier the bound cannot price

Artzner, Delbaen, Eber and Heath (1999) call $\rho$ coherent when it is monotone, translation invariant, positively homogeneous and subadditive, $\rho(X + Y) \le \rho(X) + \rho(Y)$. Subadditivity is the algebraic form of diversification. Basel II anchored market-risk capital to Value-at-Risk, which under heavy tails or asymmetric dependence reverses that inequality and penalises prudential diversification, and the revised market-risk framework replaced it with Expected Shortfall at the $97.5$ percent level (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision 2019). Expected Shortfall is subadditive and coherent, and under finite data the Baker–McHale shrinkage factor $k\_{\mathrm{BM}}(n\_{\mathrm{eff}}) = 1/(1 + 1/n\_{\mathrm{eff}})$ keeps sizing from spending estimation noise.

The regulatory constraint makes the risk endogenous. Danielsson and Shin (2003) located the severe collapses inside strategic feedback between identical models: a price fall breaches a VaR limit, the breach forces simultaneous sales into an illiquid market, and the sales lower the price again (Brunnermeier & Pedersen 2009). MacKenzie and Millo (2003) showed the same loop running through Black–Scholes. Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) priced the backtest side of it, requiring $t \ge 3.0$ rather than $t \ge 1.96$ once hundreds of factors have been tried.

The outlier is the sharpest measurement in the layer. On 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell $22.6$ percent and the S&P 500 $20.4$ percent in one session (Carlson 2007). A Gaussian assigns to a reading $k$ standard deviations below the mean an upper-tail probability no larger than $\varphi(k)/k = e^{-k^{2}/2}/(k\sqrt{2\pi})$ — the bound is on the tail probability at that distance and not on the size of the fall, and $k$ here counts standard deviations, not the shrinkage factor $k\_{\mathrm{BM}}(n\_{\mathrm{eff}})$ defined above. That bound falls faster than any power of $k$, so whatever daily standard deviation is read off the years before the crash, a one-day fall of that size sits at a probability no record of finite length could be expected to deliver. The archive delivered it. The form applies, every input is measured, and the answer it returns is that the event which happened cannot happen.

The loop that produced it has a general form. Geanakoplos (2010) describes the leverage cycle: in calm periods lenders accept thin margins, so the buyers most optimistic about an asset can hold it with little capital and its price rises above what a less leveraged market would pay; when bad news arrives, the optimists lose their wealth and lenders raise margins at the same moment, so the marginal buyer becomes a more pessimistic holder and the price falls further than the news alone warrants. The margin is not a parameter of the market but a variable it sets, and a capital rule fitted on the calm phase of the cycle measures leverage at the point where it is about to be withdrawn.

## 3. The keystone, and where the thesis stops

Layer 28 is one of the keystones whose signature carries a site on every axis, so dropping any one site reproduces a collapse. Without $T\_2$ a non-severe backtest and a p-hacked factor model justify razor-thin reserves: Blind Undersizing. Without $T\_4$ a thin-tailed model meets a Pareto archive at leverage above the Baker–McHale fraction, time-average growth is negative, and capital reaches the absorbing barrier: Impotent Lucidity Before Ruin. Without $T\_6$ the conglomerate keeps the upside of leveraged trading and hands the tail spiral to a public balance sheet: Cynical Capture and Pillaging.

The thesis is that risk is a functional of the return law and can be provisioned before the loss. It expires when the parties whose compliance it governs produce the inputs. Marks, correlation matrix and quantile are all the institution's own, so the estimate is a decision variable inside the exposure rather than a measurement of it, and the complete `(1, 1, 1)` signature cannot notice, since no site of it establishes the estimator's independence. The symptom is a ratio that drifts one way: model-implied capital over the haircut a counterparty would demand in a forced sale, falling as the position grows. The procedure is to substitute a settlement bound at the point of decision, report it beside the model number rather than instead of it, and size on the larger of the two — what a real-time gate does when it refuses to let a self-reported quantity stand as its own audit.
