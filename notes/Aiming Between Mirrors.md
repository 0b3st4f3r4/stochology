# AIMING BETWEEN MIRRORS

---

**ABSTRACT**

This essay investigates the ecology of human understanding across three complementary scales: interpersonal reception in intimate life, the malicious corruption of the communicative medium through bad faith, and the ethics of cognitive influence in organizational leadership. In the first section, drawing on the Stoic distinction between *télos* and *skopós* in Antipater of Tarsus, the sociology of metrics in Goodhart and Strathern, Adorno's negative dialectics, and Canguilhem's philosophy of biology, it argues that attempting to control one's projected image in the eyes of others turns coexistence into surveillance and suppresses the alterity of the encounter. In the second section, grounded in Girard's scapegoat theory, Bateson's pragmatics of the double bind, and the ethical and political reflections of Arendt and Weil, it analyzes the figure of the manipulator who deliberately poisons collective perception, breaks prior pacts, and sacrifices third parties to conceal fraud. In the third section, articulating Simon's decision theory, Habermas's theory of communicative action, and the ethical formulations of Kant and Spinoza, it examines the boundary between the cognitive influence inherent to management and the deliberate choice of strategic manipulation. It concludes that the integrity of common life requires renouncing narcissistic control over intimate reception, relentlessly exposing the architecture of social betrayal, and preserving the transparency of pacts in institutional governance.

**Keywords:** philosophy of communication; interpersonal perception; cognitive manipulation; organizational ethics; alterity.

---

## 1 THE FREEDOM OF THE GAZE AND THE MARGIN OF THE ENCOUNTER

We often become trapped in the mirror of the gaze of others, trying to shape not only who we are, but also the image we reflect back to the world. Demanding that the projection others form of us correspond exactly to our desire is the worst punishment we can inflict upon ourselves, because being understood is altogether different from controlling how others understand us.

Antipater of Tarsus (Cicero, 1914) used the figure of the archer to separate two things that anxiety habitually conflates: the *télos*, the duty to do everything within our power to aim well, and the *skopós*, the external target, which remains subject to the winds once the arrow leaves the bow. In dialogue, clarity, presence, and vulnerability constitute our *télos*. The impression left upon the listener belongs to the *skopós*. Once a word or gesture is released into the world, it passes through the history, sensibility, and limits of another person. Demanding control over its reception is asking the arrow to keep obeying the hand after it has been loosed.

When we forget this boundary, coexistence turns into surveillance. Something akin to what the sociology of metrics (Strathern, 1997) and Goodhart's Law (1981) reveal in institutions occurs in intimate life: when a representation or measure becomes a target of control, it corrupts the living reality it was meant only to mediate. Spontaneous gesture and honest silence give way to the calculation of impression management. Demanding that the other serve as a noiseless reflection of our will is an attempt to erase what Adorno (1973 [1966]) called the Non-Identical—the alterity of reality and of the other that refuses to fit inside our schemes of control.

Canguilhem (1991 [1966]) observed that the health of an organism lies not in the rigidity of a static average, but in its capacity to maintain a margin of tolerance for the infidelities of the environment. In life with others, this margin consists in accepting the distance between who we are and how we are read, without despair and without giving up on speaking honestly. Renouncing the tyranny of unanimity is recognizing that genuine understanding is born from the encounter between two freedoms, not from the imposition of a policed portrait.

## 2 THE ADULTERATION OF THE MEDIUM AND THE FRAUD OF PACTS

Understanding oneself and restoring to the other the right to see from their own vantage point are not enough, however, when the communicative medium is corrupted by bad faith. There are situations in which the divergence between who we are and how we are judged stems not from the natural plurality of consciousnesses, but from the deliberate action of someone who manipulates the understanding of others and succeeds in convincing them to march toward a public lynching or self-sabotage. Whole persons can be manipulated into being thrown into the fire at the exact moment the abuser's dishonesty begins to leak out, even in the face of previously sealed agreements and pacts.

If in the Stoic image of the archer the wind that deflects the arrow belongs to the natural contingency of the world, in manipulation the very air is adulterated before the shot. René Girard (1986 [1982]) described the scapegoat mechanism by showing how crises of legitimacy are deflected through the fabrication of a surrogate target capable of absorbing collective violence. The manipulator operates within this cognitive and social fissure: upon realizing that their fraud is about to surface, they hijack the group's frames of perception and convert third parties into fuel, making the community believe it is administering justice when it is merely shielding the transgressor or marching toward its own ruin.

In the pragmatics of human communication studied by Gregory Bateson (2000 [1972]), this process takes the form of a destructive double bind. By falsifying the signals of reality while simulating fidelity to prior pacts, the abuser disorients the victims' system of reference until they collaborate in their own sabotage or in the destruction of the innocent. Breaking sealed agreements when one's own guilt threatens to come to light is dynamiting what Hannah Arendt (1958) called the islands of security of human promise—the only rational shelter erected against the unpredictability of the future. Confronted with a socially engineered betrayal that is finally laid bare, the ethical demand for truth described by Simone Weil (1952 [1949]) requires that no fraudster be left even room to breathe beneath the suffocation of their own exposed fraud.

## 3 COGNITIVE INFLUENCE AND MANIPULATION IN ORGANIZATIONAL LEADERSHIP

This tension between openness to dialogue and the hijacking of perception acquires even sharper contours when it moves from informal relations to the architecture of organizations. Anyone who occupies a position of leadership never acts within a cognitively neutral space, for their decisions and priorities shape the field of attention of everyone around them. Every manager is a cognitive influencer by obligation, but becomes a cognitive manipulator by choice.

In the sciences of decision-making and bounded rationality, Herbert Simon (1997 [1947]) demonstrated that organizational authority operates above all upon the premises of reasoning, defining which variables enter the team's visual field and how problems are structured. Coordinating requires exercising this cognitive influence in the open, sharing criteria, context, and limits with integrity. The choice of manipulation begins when the prerogative to frame reality is diverted to manufacture blind spots and artificial asymmetries of information. Those who execute the work are then forced to decide inside a labyrinth previously rigged at the top, preserving the illusion of autonomy where judgment has already been captured.

When this boundary is crossed, the coordination of work degenerates into the engineering of consent. The phenomenon reflects the distinction formulated by Habermas (1984 [1981]) between communicative action, oriented toward mutual understanding, and concealed strategic action, in which an agent instrumentalizes another's reason through systematic deception. The transparency of institutional pacts gives way to calculated ambiguity and the silent transfer of responsibility. Leading groups through cognitive traps violates the principle of autonomy formulated by Kant (1996 [1785]), treating the intelligence of others as a mere means to ends they can neither audit nor freely consent to.

Spinoza (2007 [1670]) affirmed that the true purpose of governing common life is neither to dominate through fear nor to convert rational beings into automata, but to ensure that mind and body may exercise their functions in safety and without fraud. The maturity of human and institutional coexistence rests, ultimately, upon three complementary refusals: renouncing the vanity of controlling the intimate reception of the other, denying oxygen to the cowardice of those who manufacture collective betrayals to cover up their own dishonesty, and never turning the legitimate duty of guiding consciousnesses into the deliberate choice of manipulating them. When we restore to the individual the freedom of their own gaze and demand from relationships and leadership the clarity of pacts, common life ceases to be a policed shadow theater and returns to being the space of encounter between whole freedoms.
