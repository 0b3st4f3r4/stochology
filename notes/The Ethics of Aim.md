# **The Ethics of Aim: Conjecture, Construction, and Rupture in the Knowledge of the Real**

*(Foundations of a Stochology)*

---

## **Abstract**

This essay presents the theoretical foundations of **Stochology**, conceived as the discipline—simultaneously epistemological, methodological, and ethical-institutional—that investigates the conditions of validity, the formal limits, and the practical responsibility of human judgment under structural uncertainty. It argues that knowing and deliberating when the outcome remains opaque requires examining, as an indissoluble unity, the complete circuit of mediation across three planes of experience: the material and historical world in which phenomena occur, the archive and model that attempt to represent them, and the institutional decision that intervenes in collective life. The discipline's method is organized as a **negative and material dialectic of conjecture**, structured across three mediating filters and seven constitutive tensions, through which it is shown how each achievement of analytic reason—theory, mathematical formalization, the accumulation of records, and the rule of governance—turns into a new source of blindness when absolutized. From this traversal emerge three interdependent safeguards—the **epistemic margin**, the **vital margin**, and **fully apt agency under institutional governance**—whose strict complementarity is demonstrated by three theoretical collapse propositions. In the final part, the practical resonances of this architecture are unfolded across multiple methodological traditions—from demography and administrative archives to experimental inference, critical infrastructure planning, algorithmic governance, and qualitative, historiographical, clinical, and ethnographic inquiry—while subjecting Stochology itself to the reflexive test of Goodhart's Law and to explicit conditions of refutability.

**Keywords:** Stochology; ethics of aim; negative dialectics; partial identification; non-ergodicity; performativity; institutional responsibility.

## **I. Introduction: The Genealogy of the Shot and the Disciplinary Program of Stochology**

Technical, linguistic, and institutional mediators carve up reality long before it reaches human deliberation. Economic indicators, forensic reports, medical records, demographic censuses, historical chronicles, and digital databases never offer events in the raw. They deliver a world already filtered, arranged according to specific categories to make certain ways of seeing, comparing, calculating, and governing possible. For this reason, knowing and deciding are never equivalent to the serene contemplation of a motionless landscape. They constitute a permanent friction between the human effort to order experience into concepts and the material resistance of that which, in historical and natural life, overflows the schema. When we are forced to act before the course of events is fully revealed—as happens to the clinician facing a severe presentation, the judge facing incomplete evidence, the public administrator facing a health crisis, or the historian facing a mutilated archive—the decisive question ceases to be the raw quantity of accumulated information. The central problem becomes the cognitive and moral integrity of judgment while the outcome of action remains open.

The history of Western thought preserved the memory of this problem in the very etymology of the word *stochastic*. When the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli (1713/2006), in Part IV, Chapter 2 of his posthumous work *Ars Conjectandi*, sought to name the science of measuring the probability of uncertain things, he defined it as *ars conjectandi sive stochastice*—the art of conjecturing, or stochastics. In doing so, Bernoulli did not invent a neutral term; he drew upon a Greek semantic matrix deeply bound to the practical experience of aiming through fog. In classical Greek, the noun **στόχος (*stókhos*)** originally designated the narrow stake set up as a mark for shooting and, by extension, the target, the aim, or the conjecture of the mind—a term the Romans rendered as *signum* or *scopus*. From the same root derive the archer (**τοξότης, *toxótēs***), the conjecturer (**στοχαστής, *stokhastēs***), and the verb **στοχάζεσθαι (*stokházesthai*)**: to aim with discernment, to cast thought toward a point that cannot be grasped in the hand.

Centuries before the creation of the calculus of probabilities, Plato had already granted this verb a central place in the classification of human knowledge. In the dialogue *Philebus* (55e–56c), examining the structure of the arts and crafts (*tékhnai*), Plato separates two great groups of crafts. On one side stand the arts supported by the rule, the lathe, the compass, the plumb line, and exact weighing, whose model is carpentry and building construction, in which docile matter submits directly to the measuring instrument. On the other side stand the arts which, dealing with living, fluid, or turbulent matter, possess no fixed template and must operate through the disciplined exercise of conjecture and aim (*stokházesthai*). The first example invoked by Plato is not a game of chance, but **music**, which finds the right measure of consonance through the sensitive training of ear and hand without relying on a mechanical gauge; it is followed by clinical medicine, agriculture, maritime navigation, and the art of strategic command.

The recognition that these crafts possess a rational rigor of their own—distinct from geometric exactness, yet in no way inferior to it—matured throughout Antiquity. Already in the fifth century BCE, texts of the *Corpus Hippocraticum* such as the treatise *On Ancient Medicine* maintained that the physician, facing the unrepeatable constitution of each sick organism, cannot apply a uniform arithmetic measure of food or medicine; the physician must read and weigh clues, symptoms, and traces (**σημεῖα, *sēmeîa***). In the following centuries, the controversies among Hellenistic and Roman medical schools, systematized by Alexander of Aphrodisias and by **Galen of Pergamon** (2nd century CE, *De sectis*; *Ars medica*), consecrated the technical category of **στοχαστικὴ τέχνη (*stokhastikē tékhnē*)**: a "conjectural art" or "art of aim," in which the method is entirely rational and open to teaching and criticism, even though material success remains subject to factors that escape the artisan's mastery. To steer the ship through the storm or intervene at the critical instant of illness (**καιρός, *kairós***), the pilot and the clinician exercise what the Hellenists Marcel Detienne and Jean-Pierre Vernant (1974) reconstructed under the concept of **μῆτις (*mêtis*)**: a plastic, oblique, and vigilant intelligence, capable of bending to the movement of unstable ground without losing its bearings.

It is important to state transparently the scope of this genealogy. The Hippocratic physicians, Plato, and Galen were thinking of a qualitative and interpretive prudence, not a mathematical calculus of frequencies, which would only be born in the seventeenth century. Evoking *stokhastikē tékhnē* and *mêtis* here is not intended to project modern statistics anachronistically onto the ancient world, nor to dress contemporary concepts in a classical veneer of authority. It is a **heuristic, open, and revisable genealogy**: its function is to remind any contemporary specialist—whether in the humanities, the natural sciences, law, health, or engineering—that the human dilemma of judging methodically without possessing certainty precedes modern mathematization and was not dissolved by it. When modernity reduced the ancient art of aim (*stókhos*) to the technique of stochastic calculation, it gained immense formal power, yet frequently forgot the ethical and epistemological question that gave it meaning: what does it mean to aim justly when the map is incomplete and another person will bear the impact of the arrow?

### **1.1. Definition of Stochology, the Three-Plane Circuit, and Its Theoretical Demarcation**

To recover the integrity of this problem in the age of formal models and large bureaucracies, **Stochology** (from *stókhos*, aim or conjecture, and *lógos*, critical reason or systematic study) is proposed here as the **discipline**—simultaneously epistemological, methodological, and ethical-institutional—that investigates the conditions of validity, the formal limits, and the practical responsibility of judgment under structural uncertainty.

What justifies speaking of a distinct **disciplinary program**, rather than merely a supplementary chapter of statistics or moral philosophy? In the history of the sciences, a discipline is defined not by the monopoly over a tool, but by the delimitation of a **formal object** that specialized fields habitually split into incommunicable fragments. Whereas traditional scientific methodology studies the relation between reality and data, decision theory analyzes the passage from the model to rational choice, and sociology or law examines the effects of institutions on society, Stochology takes as its indivisible unit of analysis the **complete circuit of mediation** across three planes of reality:

1. **The plane of the material and historical world:** the concrete terrain where events unfold in time that does not run backward, marked by extreme accidents, bonds of mutual dependence among individuals, inequalities of visibility, and human agents who react strategically when they perceive that they are being observed or governed;
2. **The plane of representation, model, and archive:** the symbolic and documentary space—whether a judicial record, a historical archive, a statistical survey, or an algorithm—in which the inexhaustible richness of the world is inevitably compressed into concepts, classifications, tables, and equations subject to simplifications and silences;
3. **The plane of deliberation and institutions:** the practical sphere in which individuals, committees, or organizations take those imperfect representations as the basis for acting upon the world, allocating scarce resources, imposing norms, and distributing across different social groups the benefits of success and the costs of error.

The founding thesis of Stochology is that grave error rarely arises within a single isolated plane; it arises in the **passages** from one plane to another. Three direct transits drive action: first, the passage **from the world to the archive**, where much of reality is lost through non-recording or biased selection; second, the passage **from the archive to the decision**, where simplifying summaries (such as the mean) conceal extreme hazards and overload the human capacity to judge; third, the passage **from the decision back to the world**, where the intervention itself alters people's behavior and falsifies the premises of the original model. To watch over these three passages, the discipline demands three reflexive movements in the reverse direction: requiring the model to make explicit how it conceives the functioning of the world before fitting numbers; requiring the institution to declare beforehand its hypotheses, its failed attempts, and who will pay for error before using the archive; and requiring the temporal scale of the evidence gathered in the world to be compatible with the urgency of practical decision.

To situate the theoretical place of Stochology with precision, it is helpful to demarcate it in relation to the major currents that confronted the problem of uncertainty over the past century:

- **In relation to the separation between risk and uncertainty and theories of ambiguity:** Already in 1921, independently, the economist Frank Knight (1921) and the philosopher and economist John Maynard Keynes (1921) warned that not all human doubt can be reduced to a numerical fraction. Knight distinguished **risk**—situations akin to a roulette wheel or a stable life table, in which we know all possibilities and their proportions in advance—from **genuine uncertainty**, in which the historical future is singular and we possess no objective basis for calculating exact probabilities. Decades later, Daniel Ellsberg (1961) demonstrated experimentally that perfectly rational people treat an urn whose proportion of balls they know (risk) differently from an urn whose composition they ignore completely (ambiguity). Leonard J. Savage (1954) himself, in constructing the axioms of subjective expected utility that dominate standard decision theory, had the intellectual care to restrict their validity to so-called **small worlds**—logically closed scenarios in which the decision-maker can list beforehand all possible states of nature and all consequences of their acts. It is important to note that Savage's logical concept of a "small world" (the completeness of the list of possibilities) is not to be confused with the physical concept of *ergodicity* (the equivalence between the average across many parallel systems and the trajectory of a single system over time), which we will examine below. To deal with open worlds where a single probability is artificial, Itzhak Gilboa and David Schmeidler (1989) proposed deciding by considering the worst-case scenario within a family of plausible probabilities, Peter Walley (1991) developed the logic of **imprecise probabilities** (expressed by intervals between a lower and an upper bound), Charles Manski (2003, 2013) founded **partial identification** in econometrics and policy evaluation, and Peter J. Huber (1964, 1981) built **robust statistics** to prevent a handful of outliers from ruining an estimate;
- **In relation to theories of local robustness and temporal accumulation:** In engineering and planning, Yakov Ben-Haim (2006) proposed *Info-Gap* theory, suggesting that when we do not know probabilities, we should choose the option that tolerates the largest margin of error around our reference model. However, the mathematician Moshe Sniedovich (2007, 2012) demonstrated a severe flaw in this approach: if the initial reference model is deeply mistaken, seeking robustness only in its immediate neighborhood creates a "No-Man's Land," approving policies that collapse catastrophically under larger deviations. Stochology embraces Sniedovich's demonstration and subordinates any local analysis to **Abraham Wald's (1945) global principle of maximum protection (*Maximin*)**, which requires guaranteeing a non-negotiable survival floor even in the most adverse scenario. In the study of time, meanwhile, the discipline integrates the discoveries of John L. Kelly Jr. (1956) and Leo Breiman (1961) on growth rates along repeated trajectories, articulating them with the critical caveats of Paul Samuelson (1971, 1979) and Robert C. Merton (1969, 1971) and the contemporary formulation of Ole Peters (2019);
- **The specific contribution of Stochology:** Each of these traditions brilliantly illuminated one piece of the puzzle: Manski addressed honesty about what is missing in the data; Wald and Gilboa addressed prudence in the choice rule; Kelly and Peters addressed survival over time; MacKenzie and Goodhart addressed social reaction to indicators; Sosa and Scanlon addressed the ethics of judgment and moral justification. Yet when applied in isolation, these solutions remain vulnerable to one another. It does no good for a demographer or econometrician to compute an unimpeachably honest uncertainty interval (on the plane of the archive) if the engineer or minister sizes reserves at the edge of exhaustion (on the temporal plane), or if the legal arrangement allows executives to capture immediate bonuses while shifting the cost of catastrophe onto citizens (on the institutional plane). The proper object of Stochology is precisely the **structural interdependence among these three links**.

### **1.2. The Nuclear Triad and the Three Theoretical Collapse Propositions**

So that the circuit between world, archive, and decision does not break, Stochology maintains that every responsible judgment under uncertainty must rest simultaneously on **three constitutive safeguards**:

1. **The epistemic margin (on the plane of knowledge and the archive):** the intellectual duty to refuse false exactness. Whenever data are incomplete, archives contain selective silences, or models are partial simplifications of reality, the specialist must refrain from proclaiming a single number as if it were the truth, openly declaring the ambiguity interval compatible with the evidence (Manski, 2003; Lee, 2009), the structural distance between the model and the real phenomenon (Kennedy & O'Hagan, 2001), and the permanent possibility of being mistaken (Lindley, 1985);
2. **The vital margin (on the material and temporal plane):** the practical duty to preserve physical, biological, financial, and temporal reserves against the irreversibility of harm. It translates into deliberately maintaining operational slack in essential services against queue strangulation (Kingman, 1961; Scarf, 1960), containing leverage to prevent a sequence of adverse fluctuations from leading to definitive ruin (Kelly, 1956; Peters, 2019), ensuring that the autonomous survival time of a community or infrastructure exceeds the time required to rescue or recover it (Simchi-Levi et al., 2014), and moderating interventions so as not to trigger destabilizing reactions in the social environment (Perdomo et al., 2020);
3. **Fully apt agency under institutional governance (on the ethical and political plane):** the duty to bind the act of judging to responsibility for its consequences. It requires that methodological competence include the reflexive wisdom to withhold the shot when the fog is excessive (Sosa, 2015), that this brake be institutionalized in collegiate bodies with independent auditing and automatic suspension clauses (Beer, 1972; List & Pettit, 2011), that communication respect the limits of human attention (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995; Zheng & Meister, 2025), and that organizational contracts protect vulnerable frontline operators while preventing top decision-makers from profiting from the fabrication of hidden risks (Holmström, 1979; Innes, 1990; Chouldechova, 2017).

These three safeguards do not function as substitute ingredients, where an excess of one could compensate for the absence of another. They operate according to **strict complementarity**—like a bridge supported by three piers in series, or, in economic language, a relation in which the overall outcome is bounded by whichever link is missing. Suppressing any one of the three margins provokes, by logical and practical necessity, a specific mode of ruin of the other two, which we can demonstrate in three theoretical propositions:

- **Proposition 1 (Collapse through the Absence of the Epistemic Margin — *Blind Undersizing*):** Imagine a public or private institution governed by honest leaders and fully disposed to maintain physical safety reserves (it therefore possesses ethical governance and the intention of a vital margin). Yet its specialists suppress the *epistemic margin*: they analyze a massive database without realizing that the most fragile populations were left out of the registry, or they calculate the risk of a dam, an epidemic, or a financial crisis using conventional averages that ignore extreme events. Because the database is gigantic, the apparent margin of error becomes tiny. Trusting that illusory certainty, the institution builds a levee, a budget, or a hospital bed reserve that looks generous relative to the biased estimate, but is tragically smaller than the true force of the shock. When the crisis arrives, the material reserve is swept away in an instant—not out of bad faith by the managers, but because the vital margin was sized from a blind map;
- **Proposition 2 (Collapse through the Absence of the Vital Margin — *Impotent Lucidity Before Ruin*):** Imagine now the converse: an intellectually scrupulous technical staff that declares with complete transparency the width of epistemic uncertainty, and an institution that follows transparent procedures of deliberation. However, in the name of "maximum efficiency" or under severe material constraint, the system operates without a *vital margin*: hospitals run at one hundred percent bed occupancy in ordinary times, the firm operates at maximum debt leverage, or the ecosystem is exploited right up to the edge of its regenerative capacity. An adverse fluctuation then strikes—a seasonal outbreak, a moderate drought, an economic contraction—whose magnitude was perfectly foreseen within the uncertainty interval that the analysts themselves had announced. Because there is neither physical slack nor a buffer of time to absorb the impact, the system crosses a point of no return (bankruptcy, widespread blackout, patient deaths) before any learning or course correction can take place. Without a vital margin, all epistemic honesty and procedural rigor are reduced to the capacity to watch, with complete diagnostic lucidity, one's own destruction;
- **Proposition 3 (Collapse through the Absence of Agency and Governance — *Cynical Capture of Doubt and Pillaging of the Reserve*):** Suppose, finally, that an organization possesses both scientists capable of honestly measuring uncertainty intervals (*epistemic margin*) and material contingency funds and stocks (*vital margin*), but lacks *symmetric institutional governance*: its executives are evaluated and rewarded solely by ultra-short-term financial or political results, without independent auditing, without clawback obligations to return gains in the event of future disaster, and without automatic circuit breakers. In this environment of moral hazard (Holmström, 1979), the first two safeguards are rapidly perverted. On one side, the *epistemic margin* is hijacked as a rhetorical alibi for inaction: facing grave environmental or public-health warnings, power invokes the existence of scientific uncertainty to postpone protective measures indefinitely ("the studies still show wide margins; we must wait for absolute certainty"). On the other side, the *vital margin*—buffer stocks, maintenance crews, reserve funds—is labeled on quarterly balance sheets as "idle cost" and liquidated to generate immediate profits or political dividends, leaving the structure hollow when the shock inevitably strikes.

## **II. The Method: Negative Dialectics of Conjecture and the Topology of the Seven Tensions**

Why does human knowledge fall repeatedly into these traps? Is it merely the fruit of technical ignorance or individual moral weakness? Stochology advances a deeper hypothesis: the disasters of judgment under uncertainty stem from the very internal movement of rationalization when it loses awareness of its limits. The architecture of our method is therefore organized according to a **negative dialectics of conjecture**, driven by what the philosophical tradition calls **determinate negations**.

Understanding this movement is straightforward when we observe the history of scientific and administrative practices. To correct an evident defect of common sense, human reason constructs a disciplining instrument; yet the moment that instrument triumphs and comes to be treated as absolute and self-sufficient, it generates its own specific blindness—more sophisticated and harder to detect than the original error it came to cure:

- To overcome arbitrary guesswork and disorderly opinion, science demands **structured theory**; yet theory, when enamored of its own internal coherence, begins to select the facts that confirm it and invent excuses for what refutes it;
- To overcome the ambiguity of words and rhetorical passions, thought turns to **mathematical formalization**; yet the mathematical model, seduced by the elegance of its equations, begins to amputate from reality everything that does not fit into a well-behaved average;
- To correct the fragility of small samples and local impressions, institutions build **monumental archives and massive censuses**; yet the gigantic archive creates the illusion of omniscience while hiding, beneath millions of numbers, the fact that the most vulnerable groups were never recorded;
- To avoid remaining in passive contemplation, knowledge transforms itself into a **rational rule of intervention and a target of governance**; yet the target imposed from above causes the human terrain to react strategically, corrupting the indicator and consuming in irreversible time the reserves that sustained the system.

So that the exposition of this movement does not betray itself, its philosophical status must be made clear. In *Negative Dialectics*, Theodor W. Adorno (1966/2009) formulated a decisive warning against closed philosophical systems. Every concept, Adorno reminds us, operates by identification: in classifying a tree, a patient, a worker, or a crisis under a general category, thought equates what is different. Epistemic violence begins when the conceptual system forgets that it left something out and proclaims that the concept has exhausted the thing—devouring what Adorno calls the **Non-Identical**: the material, historical, and singular residue of the object that resists classification. If Stochology were to present its filters and tensions as a closed and definitive table of all possible categories of the human mind, it would fall into the exact totalizing dogmatism criticized by Adorno.

Consequently, the architecture of **three mediating filters and seven constitutive tensions** presented in this essay (and summarized in Table 1) must be read strictly as a **heuristic, open, and negative topology**. It is not a mold into which to force the world, but a set of **critical self-limiting locks**: each tension functions as an institutional and methodological reminder that compels the concept, the number, and the norm to declare what they do not know, to measure the cost of their simplifications, and to preserve room for the Non-Identical.

Along this path, Stochology interweaves three critical lineages of modern and contemporary thought:

1. **The epistemological lineage (respect for the Non-Identical and declaration of discrepancy):** uniting Adorno's (1966/2009) philosophical critique with methodological reflection on imperfect models (Kennedy & O'Hagan, 2001) and incomplete data (Manski, 2003), it establishes that the distance between representation and the real never closes and must remain visible in the form of an *epistemic margin*;
2. **The material, biological, and thermodynamic lineage (the reactivity of the medium and the arrow of time):** uniting Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen's (1971) critique of economic mechanism, Georges Canguilhem's (1943/2009) philosophy of life, Crawford Holling's (1973) ecology of resilience, and Donald MacKenzie's (2006) sociology of performativity, it recognizes that every decision acts upon a living, reactive medium subject to irreversible wear, demanding a *vital margin*;
3. **The ethical and political-institutional lineage (from the care of aim to the social contract):** uniting the Stoic ethics of the archer (Antipater of Tarsus; Cicero; Plutarch), reflection on moral luck (Nagel, 1979; Williams, 1981), virtue epistemology (Sosa, 2007, 2015), the contractualist philosophy of risk (Scanlon, 1998; Hansson, 2013), and the theory of institutions and incentives (List & Pettit, 2011; Holmström, 1979; Chouldechova, 2017), it demonstrates that methodological honesty requires a just distribution of power and of liability for error.

## **III. The First Filter: When Interest Conceals and Theory Insulates Itself**

### **3.1. Tension 1 — Situational Interest versus Intellectual Rectitude**

The first mediation occurs before any data are gathered or any equation is written. It resides in the social, economic, and institutional position occupied by the questioner: **interest selects what deserves to be seen and conceals what is convenient to ignore**. No researcher, magistrate, physician, public administrator, or corporate board deliberates outside the world; all operate immersed in structures of funding, career, prestige, and power. When project approval, budget renewal, a company's share price, or a government's political survival depends on a given conclusion, a silent gravitational field inclines attention. Clues favoring the desired result are received with immediate benevolence, while contrary signs are subjected to relentless skepticism or simply left outside the search field.

The most insidious aspect of this first distortion is that it rarely needs to resort to explicit fraud. Altering a document or fabricating a measurement is a crude and risky offense; far safer and more routine is shaping the prior architecture of inquiry. One chooses a narrow definition of the problem that excludes its gravest collateral effects; one slices the historical series at the exact year in which the trend looks favorable; one runs twenty preliminary trials or models behind the scenes and publishes only the single one that produced the convenient number, keeping silent about the nineteen that failed.

In contemporary epistemology, Quassim Cassam (2019), studying so-called *epistemic vices*—dispositions of individual or institutional character that systematically obstruct the attainment of truth—highlighted two particularly grave forms of this pathology: **epistemic malevolence** and **epistemic insouciance**. Epistemic malevolence consists in the deliberate, organized production of doubt to prevent society from acting—as the public-relations departments of the tobacco, asbestos, and fossil-fuel industries did for decades by funding studies whose sole purpose was to repeat that "the science is still uncertain." Epistemic insouciance is subtler and pervades the daily life of modern bureaucracies. It takes up the classic diagnosis of the philosopher Harry Frankfurt (2005) in his essay *On Bullshit*: whereas the liar still maintains a tense relation with the truth (since one must know it in order to hide it from the interlocutor), the producer of *bullshit* has severed all commitment to factual reality. It does not matter to them whether what they say is true or false, so long as the discourse sounds technical, satisfies the form's requirement, and produces the political effect of projecting competence.

To prevent inquiry from being born corrupted by indifference to truth or by the furtive selection of results, Stochology establishes three theoretical requirements at the origin of any conjecture:

1. **Antidogmatic reserve (Cromwell's Rule):** In 1650, facing the intransigence of the Scottish churchmen, Oliver Cromwell addressed to them a sentence that echoed across the centuries: *"I beseech you, in the bowels of Christ, think it possible you may be mistaken."* The British statistician Dennis Lindley (1985) baptized this warning **Cromwell's Rule** and showed its deep logical meaning: in human reasoning under uncertainty, assigning to any empirical statement about the world absolute certainty (one hundred percent) or absolute impossibility (zero percent) is equivalent to destroying the capacity to learn from experience. If a judge, a clinician, or a scientist starts from the dogmatic premise that a hypothesis has strictly zero possibility of being true, no mountain of future evidence will ever move them from their initial prejudice. The ethics of aim requires, as a first gesture of intellectual rectitude, always keeping open a formal slit for one's own error;
2. **Bitemporal integrity of prior commitment and the separation between principle and parameter:** If human interest is a master at reinterpreting the past to justify the present, the only effective defense is to separate two moments in time: the instant at which we declare what we are going to look for and how we will judge success, and the later instant at which we open the results of reality. Without this verifiable prior commitment, the analyst acts like the dishonest archer who first shoots the arrow at a blank wall and then paints the target rings around the hole where the shaft landed. At the same time, this temporal discipline teaches an essential theoretical distinction: we must not confuse the **invariant structural principles of method**—such as the duty to declare the rules before the game, not to hide failed attempts, and to check whether the conclusion holds when secondary assumptions are varied—with the **conventional and contingent parameters** adopted in a specific study (such as choosing a tolerance margin of five percent or one percent). Parameters are tools adjustable to practical context; the principle of not manipulating the rule after knowing the outcome is non-negotiable;
3. **Mandatory memory of discarded attempts, the specification curve, and prevention of Type III Error:** When a researcher or institution explores dozens of possible analytical paths, the probability of finding an impressive coincidence by pure chance approaches certainty. As Ruodu Wang and Aaditya Ramdas (2022) demonstrated theoretically in analyzing false-discovery control when multiple tests depend on one another—and as Sara Steegen et al. (2016) show in **multiverse analysis (*specification curve*)**—the validity of a finding can be judged only if we know how many doors were opened before finding it and whether the conclusion remains stable when conventional windowing and preprocessing choices are varied. At the same time, intellectual rectitude at the origin demands avoiding what Allyn Kimball (1957), Howard Raiffa (1968), and Ian Mitroff (1974) termed **Type III Error**: the error of giving a mathematically precise answer to the wrong framing of the problem, optimizing a narrow target while excluding severe collateral externalities from the ledger, or hiding the framing conflict between the standpoint of the institution and the standpoint of those who pay the price of error (**strong objectivity**, Longino, 1990; Harding, 1991).

### **3.2. Tension 2 — Accommodating Confirmation versus Fruitful Refutation and the Theoretical Bridge Between Mayo and Manski**

Once the barrier of immediate interests is overcome, a second difficulty arises, even deeper because it is inscribed in the logical structure of human thought itself: **when reality contradicts a cherished theory, reason's natural tendency is to patch the theory to save it from refutation**.

Ever since David Hume demonstrated that no finite number of past observations logically guarantees that the sun will rise tomorrow under the same laws, the philosophy of science has understood that our theories are always conjectures. Karl Popper (1934/2002) drew from this his celebrated criterion of demarcation: what distinguishes genuine science from myth or ideology is not the accumulation of favorable examples—for any astrological or political doctrine easily finds cases that appear to confirm it—but the deliberate willingness to formulate risky claims, specifying in advance which observable events would suffice to refute them.

Yet Popper's solution ran into a formidable obstacle, revealed by the physicist and historian Pierre Duhem (1906/1954) and radicalized by the philosopher Willard Van Orman Quine (1951), known as the **Duhem–Quine thesis**. A theoretical hypothesis never stands alone before the tribunal of experience. To test an astronomical law or a theory of unemployment, for example, we must combine it with dozens of auxiliary assumptions: that the telescope suffered no optical distortion, that the survey questionnaires were understood by respondents, that no external shock interfered during the period. When the prediction fails, deductive logic tells us only that *some piece* of that vast bundle is false, but remains silent about *which* piece should be discarded. The scientist or ruler attached to their doctrine always has a logical escape hatch: preserve the core of the theory intact and shift all the blame for failure onto an auxiliary condition ("the economic model was perfect, but failed because of the weather or public misunderstanding").

How, then, can we distinguish legitimate scientific revision from an opportunistic excuse? The philosopher of science Imre Lakatos (1970) answered this question by showing that we must judge not an isolated hypothesis at a static instant, but the historical trajectory of a research program over time. When an anomaly occurs, two opposite paths open. In a **progressive problemshift (*fruitful adjustment*)**, the auxiliary hypothesis introduced to explain the deviation does not serve merely to plug the hole; it takes the risk of predicting entirely novel, previously unsuspected facts that can be verified independently. The classic example is Urbain Le Verrier in 1846: confronted with the fact that the observed orbit of the planet Uranus violated Newton's gravitational calculations, Le Verrier neither discarded Newton nor invented a vague excuse; he calculated that the anomaly could be explained only if an unknown planet existed at a specific coordinate in the sky—and when the Berlin Observatory pointed its telescope at that spot, it discovered Neptune. In a **degenerating problemshift (*ad hoc patching*)**, by contrast, every empirical failure is met with a linguistic restriction or an exception clause tailor-made solely to neutralize the counterexample, shrinking the reach of the theory step by step until it turns into an unfalsifiable dogma.

In the contemporary world, this temptation toward accommodating adjustment has acquired industrial speed through computational power. The philosopher of statistics Deborah Mayo (2018) confronted this landscape by formulating the **severity principle (*severe testing*)**. Mayo's intuition is luminously clear and applies to every field of knowledge: **data that agree with a hypothesis count as genuine evidence in its favor only if the method used would have had a very high probability of detecting the error had the hypothesis been false**. Imagine an inspector who declares a city's water free of a toxic metal after examining it with the naked eye in a clear glass. The result of the observation ("clear water") is perfectly compatible with the hypothesis of purity; yet because that procedure would never detect the colorless poison if it were present, the test lacks all severity and the approval is a sham. Similarly, when a researcher watches data arrive day after day and decides to stop the study on the exact afternoon when the numbers momentarily cross the favorable line, they destroy the test's severity. To neutralize this temporal vulnerability, recent developments in sequential evidence theory—led by Glenn Shafer (2021), Peter Grünwald, Rianne de Heide, and Wouter Koolen (2024), and Aaditya Ramdas et al. (2023)—have reformulated statistical proof as a continuous accumulative process (akin to an honest bet against chance), whose validation thresholds remain rigorous regardless of the moment at which the observer consults the archive.

Here we reach a theoretical crossroads that habitually divides methodologists of different traditions and that Stochology sets out to resolve: **how is it possible to reconcile Deborah Mayo's (2018) philosophy of severe testing, which demands sharp claims capable of being refuted by facts, with Charles Manski's (2003, 2013) theory of partial identification, which holds that social data are almost always insufficient to pinpoint a single value and compel us to work with wide intervals of doubt?**

To a hasty glance, Mayo and Manski appear irreconcilable: one demands a sharp cutting edge; the other insists on the persistence of fog. Stochology demonstrates, however, that these two demands form an indissoluble theoretical alliance once we understand the intellectual division of labor between them:

1. **Manski's moment (honesty about what the world allowed us to see):** When we evaluate the effect of a medical therapy, an educational reform, or an economic policy, we never observe the same person living two lives simultaneously (one with the treatment and one without it), and almost always a fraction of people disappears from follow-up. Manski demonstrates that, in order to produce a single point number ("the program raised income by exactly seven percent"), the analyst is forced to introduce invisible and unverifiable assumptions about the people who vanished. Partial identification refuses this fiction and honestly delimits the **identification region**: the interval stretching from the most pessimistic scenario to the most optimistic scenario compatible with the data actually recorded;
2. **Mayo's moment (severe testing applied to the decision boundary):** Once that honest interval of doubt has been delimited, Mayo's severe test enters the stage. What we subject to rigorous refutation testing is no longer an imaginary point number in the middle of the road, but the decisive practical question: **does even the most conservative lower bound of that interval—that is, the scenario in which all omissions of the archive work against our hypothesis—still outweigh the cost and risk of intervening?**

We thus understand why there is no contradiction between the two: **recognizing Manski's identification bounds is the indispensable precondition for a test to be genuinely severe in Mayo's sense**. When a study ignores selective data missingness and applies a conventional statistical test to a biased point mean, it acts exactly like the inspector looking at water against the light: its test has zero severity against the hidden bias. Genuine severity exists only when the test bears upon the honest margin of our ignorance.

## **IV. The Second Filter: When the Model's Norm Narrates and the Archive Omits**

### **4.1. Tension 3 — Domesticating Formalization versus Reflexive Formalization**

If the first filter concerns the investigator's intentions and the structure of theories, the second filter takes hold in the instruments with which we translate the world into signs: **formal models and documentary archives**. Historically, the passage from ordinary language to mathematical and classificatory formalization represented an extraordinary achievement. It made it possible to render explicit premises previously hidden in rhetoric, compare distant phenomena, and subject reasoning to public scrutiny. Yet from this very virtue springs the third dialectical inversion: **the internal grammar of the model begins to legislate over what has the right to exist in reality**.

Theodor Adorno (1966/2009) diagnosed the philosophical root of this phenomenon by showing how classificatory reason replaces the living experience of the object with the manipulation of the conceptual labels pasted over it: everything qualitative, asymmetric, or recalcitrant to uniform measurement is discarded as irrelevant "noise." In the epistemology of the social and natural sciences, Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (1971) gave this critique a complementary formulation by distinguishing two types of concepts. There are **arithmomorphic concepts**, which possess perfectly sharp, motionless boundaries like integers: the number two is absolutely separated from the number three, with no gray zone between them. And there are **dialectical concepts**, which describe almost the entirety of biological, psychological, historical, and political processes—such as "health," "democracy," "justice," "development," or "ecological stability"—which are surrounded by a *constitutive penumbra* of transition and qualitative change. The error of uncritical formalism lies not in using mathematics to illuminate aspects of these phenomena, but in forgetting the penumbra and treating the arithmomorphic schema as if it were the very structure of being.

For this reason, Stochology rejects both the irrationalism that despises calculation and the technicism that idolizes it, proposing the distinction between **domesticating formalization** and **reflexive formalization**. Domesticating formalization chooses certain equations—symmetric curves, linear relations, simple averages—merely because they are easy to solve on paper, artificially domesticating the wildness of the world. **Reflexive formalization**, by contrast, uses the power of formal thought itself to expose the illusions of simplification across four dimensions accessible to every field of knowledge:

1. **The convex asymmetry of harm and the fallacy of planning for the average (Jensen, 1906):** At the beginning of the twentieth century, the Danish mathematician Johan Jensen (1906) proved a theorem on curved (convex) functions whose practical consequence for human and social life is immense. We say that a harm or cost is *convex* when each additional unit of stress causes greater damage than the preceding one. Think of the human body: our normal internal temperature hovers around thirty-seven degrees Celsius; spending one hour at twenty-seven degrees and another hour at forty-seven degrees has an "average" of exactly thirty-seven degrees, yet the organism dies of hyperthermia in the second hour. Think of agriculture: five months of absolute drought followed by one month of torrential deluge may yield the year's "historical average rainfall," yet destroy the entire harvest. Think of a hospital or a highway: when the flow of patients or vehicles rises from eighty to ninety-nine percent of capacity, congestion does not grow by ten or twenty percent—it explodes. Jensen's inequality proves mathematically that whenever harm accelerates nonlinearly, **the actual suffering or cost produced by the oscillations of the world is inevitably greater than the cost calculated on the average scenario**. This difference—**Jensen's Gap**—explains why public policies, budgets, and infrastructures designed exclusively "for the average" fail systematically in practice. The lone mean erases the pain of fluctuations;
2. **Worlds of wild extremes and the vanishing of standard variance (Hill, 1975):** In school, we learn to view the variety of the world through the bell curve (the Gaussian or normal distribution), whose textbook example is human height: there are shorter and taller people, but no one stands twenty meters tall; if we take a thousand people, the tallest individual adds a tiny fraction to the total sum, and deviation around the mean stabilizes quickly. Yet as Bruce M. Hill (1975) studied in the statistical analysis of so-called *heavy tails*, much of nature and human history obeys not the logic of height, but the logic of earthquakes, pandemics, wildfires, wars, class-action lawsuits, and financial crises. In these domains, a single extreme event in the tail of the distribution can be thousands of times larger than the sum of all ordinary preceding events. When this disproportion crosses a critical threshold, something occurs that shocks common intuition: **the population variance ceases to exist as a finite number**. This means that traditional measures of "standard deviation" calculated during calm periods are mirages that drastically underestimate the reality of major shocks;
3. **Explicit recognition of the discrepancy between model and reality (Kennedy & O'Hagan, 2001):** Every model—whether a climate simulation, an actuarial projection, or a sociological theory—leaves out real mechanisms of the world. Marc Kennedy and Anthony O'Hagan (2001) showed what happens when a scientist calibrates a model while pretending that its theoretical structure is perfect: to force the model's curves to pass right through the points observed in the past, the internal parameters are twisted into unrealistic values, silently absorbing the theory's defects. The model appears to fit the past wonderfully, yet loses the ability to predict the future under new conditions. Reflexive formalization requires explicitly including a **structural discrepancy** term—an honest measure of what the model ignores—widening uncertainty whenever we step outside familiar terrain;
4. **The difference between speaking with numerical precision, respecting the scale of measurement, and being calibrated with the real (Stevens, 1946; Krantz et al., 1971; Dimitriadis, Gneiting & Jordan, 2021):** Finally, assigning numbers to uncertainties or qualitative evaluations guarantees no rationality by itself. As Stanley Smith Stevens (1946) and David Krantz, R. Duncan Luce, Patrick Suppes, and Amos Tversky (1971) demonstrated in representational measurement theory, computing arithmetic means over purely ordinal scales is a formal abuse whose conclusion can flip sign under a simple monotone relabeling of the steps, requiring instead the use of quantiles and ordinal stochastic dominance. And in probabilistic judgment, as Timo Dimitriadis, Tilmann Gneiting, and Alexander Jordan (2021) show, evaluation must be judged by two distinct virtues: **calibration** (if the specialist or system states that an event has an eighty percent chance, that event should actually occur in roughly eight out of ten similar occasions) and **discriminative resolution** (the capacity to genuinely separate high-risk cases from low-risk cases, without hiding behind a vague average). Without this dual verification outside the data used to build the model, mathematical language degenerates into a rhetoric of intimidation.

### **4.2. Tension 4 — Sample Volume versus Evidential Weight, the Silence of the Archive, and the Epistemic Margin**

When the fragility of models becomes visible, the most common reaction of our time is to bet on the brute force of documentary accumulation: it is believed that if we gather millions or billions of digital records (*Big Data*), the sheer immensity of numbers will wash away the defects of observation and hand us the naked truth. Here the fourth dialectical inversion takes hold: **the gigantism of the archive replaces the quality of evidence and turns invisible omission into arrogant certainty**.

In 1921, in Chapter 6 of *A Treatise on Probability*, John Maynard Keynes formulated a philosophical distinction that remains the most lucid antidote to this illusion: the difference between the **probability** of a conclusion and the **weight of argument** that supports it. Imagine two physicians or two magistrates who must assess the plausibility of a hypothesis. The first has read only a single two-line summary note, in which one clue speaks in favor and another speaks against; they conclude that the chance is fifty-fifty. The second has spent weeks examining dozens of laboratory tests, hearing contradictory witnesses, inspecting the site, and checking historical records, finding a complex balance of evidence for and against; they too conclude that the chance is fifty-fifty. The probability number is identical in both cases, yet the cognitive density—the **evidential weight**—is incomparable. The weight of argument measures not which side is winning on the scales of evidence, but **how much relevant reality has already been placed upon the pans of the scale compared with the vastness of what we still ignore**.

Nearly a hundred years after Keynes, the statistician Xiao-Li Meng (2018) proved mathematically how contempt for weight and collection quality gives rise to the **Big Data paradox**. Studying why gigantic surveys with millions of digital responses fail spectacularly where small, careful samples succeed, Meng proved an exact identity: the error between the mean we see in our archive and the true mean of the entire population is the product of three elements: (1) **representative recording quality** (that is, the degree of biased association between the fact that someone enters the database and the characteristic being measured), (2) **relative coverage proportion** (the ratio between how many were left out of the archive and how many entered), and (3) **the natural diversity of the population**.

From this relation follow three theoretical consequences that every scholar—in any field—must retain:

1. **What matters is the proportion reached, not the isolated raw count:** having one million records in a country of two hundred million inhabitants without control over who entered confers, by itself, no informational superiority about the whole;
2. **Selection bias does not shrink when we pile up more data collected in the same biased way:** if a historical archive, a digital app, or a service registry preferentially records the more educated, wealthier, or better-connected citizens, doubling or multiplying by a hundred the number of registered users under the same logic reduces the defect of representativeness by nothing at all. Meanwhile, the traditional "margin of error" of statistics—which was designed solely to measure the fluctuation of a pure random draw—shrinks rapidly toward zero as the raw number of rows grows;
3. **The perverse effect of maximum certainty in error:** combine the persistence of bias with the shrinking of the apparent margin of error and the trap is complete. A small sample humbly acknowledges that its uncertainty band is wide; a monumental, biased database draws a hair-thin uncertainty band around the wrong place. **The larger the biased archive, the closer to zero becomes the chance that its official confidence interval contains the truth.** Meng demonstrated that a biased registry of millions of people can contain less reliable information about society than a genuinely randomized sample of four hundred people.

Two additional phenomena deepen this opacity of the archive in the transit between world and representation. The first is the **echo or clustering effect**, demonstrated by Cathy Campbell (1979) and Brent Moulton (1986). In real life, observations are rarely independent of one another like successive tosses of a coin: patients treated in the same hospital, students in the same school, residents of the same watershed, or transactions occurring on the same day of financial panic experience the same shared influences. When we treat ten thousand clustered records as if they were ten thousand independent witnesses, we forget that many are merely repeating the same common shock; the number of **genuinely independent units** is drastically smaller than the total number of pages in the file, especially when we try to evaluate extreme events.

The second and deepest limit is the **political and material nature of omission**. The statistical theory of missing data, founded by Donald Rubin (1976) and systematized by Roderick Little and Donald Rubin (1987/2002), distinguishes three situations when information is missing from the archive: the absence may be purely accidental (like a page lost through a mechanical printing defect); it may depend only on characteristics already visible in the registry itself (allowing partial compensation); or it may be **structurally non-ignorable**—when the probability that the record disappears depends on the exact invisible condition we need to know. In the humanities, medicine, and public administration, silence is almost never innocent: the most destitute are precisely those who lack documents to enter the poverty census; workers subjected to the worst coercion are those who do not file complaints in labor registries; the most underserved patients die without a diagnosis; the vanquished of history rarely write the official archives.

It is in the face of this non-ignorable silence that Charles Manski's (2003, 2013) theory of **partial identification** stands, together with the fundamental distinction established by Guido Imbens and Charles Manski (2004) between **sampling uncertainty** and **identification ambiguity**. Sampling uncertainty comes merely from having observed a limited portion of available records, and would shrink if we collected more similar data; **identification ambiguity**, by contrast, comes from the fact that part of reality remains structurally hidden or depends on counterfactual scenarios that never occurred—and therefore **it does not vanish even if the database becomes infinite**. In real archives where Meng's selection bias and Moulton's unequal clustering operate together, the effective sample size contracts compoundedly; where certain human strata have near-zero probability of inclusion in the registry (positivity violations that blow up Horvitz & Thompson, 1952, weights and collapse Kish's, 1965, effective sample size), where the size of the omitted contingent itself must be estimated by cross-referencing multiple sources (*capture-recapture* and Chao's, 1987, lower bound), or where silence occurs through selective exit over time (*informative censoring* bounded by Peterson's, 1976, bounds), the explicit declaration of Manski bounds replaces illusory extrapolation with evidentiary transparency.

When a government, a court, or a scientific team demands that the specialist hide this ambiguity and present a single number ("state the exact rate or the exact impact"), it forces the specialist to fill the silence of the archive with invented assumptions. The **epistemic margin** of Stochology refuses this imposition: it openly declares the interval between the smallest and largest values compatible with what we actually know (complemented by David S. Lee's, 2009, trimming bounds when part of the participants selectively drop out of a study). And it shows that declaring an interval does not paralyze human decision; it simply changes the rule of prudence:

- When probabilities are well identified and calibrated, one decides by transparently comparing the ratio between the **cost of the protective measure** and the **magnitude of the avoidable harm**, always respecting temporal scale parity (one never decides the daily shift of an emergency ward on the basis of an annual average);
- When, on the contrary, we are under **partial identification and severe uncertainty**, we abandon the fiction of maximizing an unknown average and decide by Manski's (2003, 2013) **minimax regret** rule or by seeking robustness subordinated to **Abraham Wald's (1945) and Moshe Sniedovich's (2007, 2012) global protection floor (*Maximin*)**—choosing the policy that prevents catastrophe even if the invisible portion of the archive contains the worst possible scenario.

## **V. The Third Filter: When the Truth of the World Tears Under Action and Time**

### **5.1. Tension 5 — Passive Observation versus Performative Intervention**

Let us trace the path traveled thus far. We have supposed an investigator who watched over their own interests, subjected their conjectures to severe tests, refused the fetish of the lone mean, and honestly declared the intervals imposed by the silences of the archive. Could they at last rest easy when their conclusion becomes a statute, a clinical protocol, a public policy, or an economic directive? Stochology's answer is negative, for here the third mediating filter opens: **the instant a representation is used to govern the world, the living terrain reacts to the intervention and tears the map that was meant to guide it**.

The first reason for this rupture is logical and ontological, systematized by Judea Pearl (2009; Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018) in his **Ladder of Causation**. Pearl demonstrates that human knowledge about cause-and-effect relations is divided into **three strictly hierarchical rungs**, and that no amount of data gathered on the lower rung allows climbing to the next without a structural understanding of how the world works:

1. **The first rung is Association (the act of seeing or observing passively):** it consists in noticing that two phenomena tend to appear together. We observe, for example, that people who sleep with their shoes on tend to wake up with a headache, or that hospitals receiving a certain piece of equipment record more deaths. Passive observation shows correlation, but is blind to structure: whoever slept with their shoes on probably did so because they drank too much the night before, and the hospital with advanced equipment records more deaths precisely because it receives the sickest patients in the region;
2. **The second rung is Intervention (the act of doing or materially altering the world):** it consists in asking what will happen if we step into the system and change an element by our own action—compelling a sober person to sleep with their shoes on or installing the equipment in an ordinary hospital. When we intervene, we sever the old links that explained the passive correlation and inaugurate a new dynamic;
3. **The third rung is the Counterfactual (the act of imagining alternative worlds and judging retrospectively):** it consists in asking what *would have happened* in a singular case that has already taken place had the action been different ("would this specific patient have survived if they had been treated two hours earlier?"; "would this region have avoided flooding if the forest reserve had not been cleared?"). The counterfactual rung is the condition of possibility both for transporting scientific knowledge from one historical context to another and for attributing moral and legal responsibility in social life.

In the humanities, economics, law, and public administration, the passage from the first rung (seeing) to the second (intervening) undergoes an even more dramatic complication: **the atoms of the social world think, interpret the rules, and change strategy**. In classical physics, predicting a comet's trajectory does not cause the comet to change course out of whim or interest; in social life, however, announcing a forecast or turning a statistical indicator into a criterion of reward and punishment immediately alters people's conduct.

This discovery was formulated in economics by Robert Lucas (1976)—by demonstrating that historical correlations between inflation and employment dissolve as soon as economic agents adjust their expectations to a new government policy—and received its most celebrated expression in the law formulated by the economist **Charles Goodhart (1975)**. Analyzing British monetary policy, Goodhart warned that any statistical regularity observed in the past tends to collapse **once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes**—a fundamental clause of political and administrative pressure, later synthesized in the anthropology of organizations by Marilyn Strathern (1997) in the maxim that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

The sociologist Donald MacKenzie (2006), studying the history of mathematical models in financial markets, coined the definitive image for this phenomenon: a model applied to social life is not **a camera** that passively records the world out there, but **an engine** that enters reality and manufactures it. In extreme cases—which MacKenzie calls **counterperformativity**—the punitive use of the indicator destroys the very reality the indicator sought to measure. If a department of education begins rewarding schools and punishing principals exclusively by students' mean score on a standardized test, many schools begin obstructing the enrollment of struggling learners or drilling children solely to memorize the test key: the official score rises brilliantly on government tables while real education is devastated. If a public-safety system evaluates police precincts solely by the drop in recorded crime reports, a silent incentive is created to discourage victims from filing complaints: statistical crime "vanishes" on paper while real violence grows in the streets.

Recent research on **performative prediction**, developed by Juan Perdomo, Tijana Zrnic, Celestine Mendler-Dünner, and Moritz Hardt (2020), together with the classification of the four forms of Goodhart's Law collapse proposed by David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant (2018) and the search for invariant causal relations across distinct environments (Peters, Bühlmann & Meinshausen, 2016), makes it possible to grasp with theoretical clarity what happens in these processes. When an institution deploys an evaluation rule and the population reacts to it, two distinct dangers open:

- The first danger is **chronic instability**: if society's strategic reaction to each change of the rule is too intense, the system never finds rest; governors and governed enter a cat-and-mouse game of perpetual oscillations;
- The second danger, far subtler and more deceptive, is confusing **performative stability** with **performative optimality** (Perdomo et al., 2020). Often, an institution notices that its predictive model "hits the mark" more and more over time and celebrates the apparent scientific success, without realizing that it was the institutional decision itself that pushed the population into that outcome. If a bank or development agency decides, on the basis of a preliminary model, that a given poor neighborhood has a high default risk and therefore cuts off its credit and investment, the small businesses of that neighborhood will indeed fail; the following year, the bank will look at the bankruptcy data and say: *"See how right our model was!"*. That is merely **performative stability**—a self-fulfilling prophecy (*Barnesian performativity*, MacKenzie, 2006) in which power molds the world in the image of its prejudice. **Performative optimality**, demanded by Stochology, compels the decision-maker to evaluate the model not by the comfort of confirming its own intervention, but by the substantive quality of the world that its rule helps produce: continuously checking whether the indicator is being corrupted by extreme noise (*regressional collapse*), by shifting outside historical experience (*extremal collapse*), by cosmetic action on symptoms rather than genuine self-improvement in root causes (*causal collapse*, Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2020; Miller, Milli & Hardt, 2020), or by direct strategic manipulation (*adversarial collapse*), measuring spillover and congestion effects when scaling from a pilot study to the entire population (Hudgens & Halloran, 2008), and bounding, on the third counterfactual rung of the singular case, the exact probabilities of necessity and sufficiency (Tian & Pearl, 2000).

### **5.2. Tension 6 — The Average of Parallel Universes versus the Trajectory of a Single Life in Irreversible Time**

The second rupture of the third filter concerns the very nature of **time**. As Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (1971) demonstrated in his critique of the foundations of modern economics, much of formal science adopted as its model the **reversible mechanical time** of classical physics: in the equations of motion of a planet or an ideal frictionless billiard ball, if we reverse the sign of time, the film runs backward without anything being lost. Yet living organisms, ecosystems, families, firms, and political institutions do not inhabit the mechanical time of clocks; they inhabit **irreversible thermodynamic and historical time**, governed by entropy, in which burned fuel does not turn back into firewood, broken trust does not mend without a scar, and death or bankruptcy admits no return.

When we ignore the irreversibility of time, we fall into the sixth constitutive tension: the confusion between the **ensemble average** and the **time average**. So that any reader—even without the slightest interest in mathematical formulas—can grasp the vital gravity of this distinction, a simple image suffices.

Imagine two completely different ways of crossing a river covered by a thin sheet of ice, where there is a ten percent chance that the ice will break and the traveler will drown, and a ninety percent chance of reaching the other bank safe and sound, receiving a large reward in gold there:

- In the **first way (the ensemble average, in parallel space)**, we gather **one hundred different travelers** on the riverbank and have all of them cross simultaneously, each along their own strip of ice, a single time. By late afternoon, ten travelers will have fallen into the freezing water, while ninety will have reached the other side wealthy. If we look at the "group average" on that single day, the expedition will appear an extraordinary economic success;
- In the **second way (the temporal trajectory, in irreversible history)**, there is only **a single traveler**—a real person, a family, an institution, or a biological species—who must cross that same river **one hundred days in a row**, carrying in their backpack everything accumulated on previous days. What will happen to this historical traveler? Even if they succeed on the first day, the second, and the fifth, the practical certainty is that on one of the hundred days the ice will give way beneath their feet. And on the day they drown, all previous successful crossings and all gains that would have come on subsequent days cease to exist instantly, because a dead traveler does not keep walking the next day.

The average of the hundred parallel travelers on the same day is positive and radiant; the trajectory of the single traveler across a hundred successive days ends almost certainly in ruin. The theoretical assumption that pretends these two things are equal is called the **ergodicity hypothesis**.

To treat this theme with maximum intellectual and historical honesty, Stochology carefully separates the **mathematical theorem** from the **economic and philosophical interpretations** built upon it:

1. **The foundation in stochastic calculus (Itô, 1944, 1951):** In the 1940s and early 1950s, the Japanese mathematician Kiyosi Itô (1944, 1951) discovered the exact rule governing continuous processes subject to random fluctuations. Applied to any resource that grows or shrinks in proportion to what is already held (multiplicative processes), Itô's result reveals a relentless asymmetry: **a fifty percent percentage loss followed by a fifty percent percentage gain never returns anyone to their starting point** (someone who had one hundred and loses half is left with fifty; when they gain half of fifty, they reach seventy-five, accumulating a net loss of twenty-five percent, even though the "simple average" between minus fifty and plus fifty looks like zero!). Because of this asymmetry, the agitation or volatility of the path itself acts as an **invisible drain** that pulls down the growth rate over time;
2. **The economic and decision-theoretic tradition (Kelly, Breiman, Merton, Samuelson, and Peters):** In 1956, the physicist John L. Kelly Jr. (1956), working at Bell Labs, and shortly thereafter the mathematician Leo Breiman (1961) demonstrated how to choose the prudent fraction of exposure to maximize growth over time without falling into ruin. In the following decades, Robert C. Merton (1969, 1971) incorporated Itô calculus into financial theory, while **Paul Samuelson (1971, 1979)** formulated an important critical caveat: he reminded us that human beings do not live for an infinite time and that, over finite horizons and for choices that do not threaten survival, Kelly's logarithmic rule does not replace all legitimate forms of human preference. More recently, the physicist **Ole Peters (2019)** synthesized this long debate under the name of *ergodicity economics*, showing how a substantial part of traditional economic theory treated irreversible historical decisions as if they were averages across parallel universes.

Faithful to conceptual rigor, Stochology avoids exaggeration: **not everything in the world is non-ergodic**. When a person or institution makes small everyday additive choices whose occasional losses are tiny and never approach a point of no return, traditional averages work as good approximations. **Non-ergodicity becomes a theoretical and practical matter of life and death** precisely when two conditions come together: **(a) gains and losses compound upon one another along the path** and **(b) absorbing barriers exist**—physical, biological, ecological, or legal thresholds at which the game ends for good (clinical death, insolvent bankruptcy, irreversible soil desertification, dam rupture). Before an absorbing barrier, governing by the average of parallel universes is a fatal imprudence.

### **5.3. The Two Meanings of Survival and the Biological and Ecological Grounding of the Vital Margin**

The central importance of absorbing barriers compels us to clarify immediately the word **survival**, lest anyone confuse the stochological defense of life with some form of social Darwinism in which "surviving" would justify any means. Here two symmetrical confusions must be dispelled:

- The first occurs in the transit **from the world to the archive** and constitutes **statistical survivorship bias**, discovered by **Abraham Wald (1943)**. During the Second World War, the U.S. military command examined bombers returning from missions over Europe and noticed that the wings and tail were riddled with bullet holes, while the engines and cockpit returned almost intact. The generals concluded that armor should be reinforced on the wings. Wald prevented this blunder by pointing out the obvious fact hidden by the archive: bullet holes in the engines did not appear on the spreadsheets precisely because planes hit in the engine fell into the sea and never returned to be counted! Looking only at survivors blinds the observer to the true cause of ruin;
- The second occurs in the transit **from action to history** and constitutes the **normative fallacy of survival**: the moral and sociological error of imagining that everyone who survived, grew rich, or remained in power did so because they were wiser, more virtuous, or more efficient. In a world crossed by chance and unequal power, reckless gamblers frequently survive for long seasons thanks to favorable winds, and deeply unjust or polluting institutions perpetuate themselves because they manage to shift the bill for their recklessness onto unprotected populations or future generations.

For this reason, Stochology distinguishes three unmistakable planes: (1) **material survival**, which is not the ultimate meaning of justice, but the *indispensable physical floor* so that subjects and collectivities can continue to exist and deliberate; (2) **epistemic survival**, which belongs to theories that have withstood severe tests; and (3) **legitimate institutional survival**, which is won without parasitizing or destroying the vital margin of others.

Where, then, should we seek the positive concept of this protective reserve? Stochology finds its most fruitful inspiration in the philosophy of medicine of **Georges Canguilhem (1943/2009)** and the ecology of **Crawford S. Holling (1973)**. In his classic work *The Normal and the Pathological*, written in the heat of the French Resistance, the physician and philosopher Georges Canguilhem rejected the positivist idea that health is conformity to a "normal statistical average." An organism that can live only if temperature, nutrition, and exertion remain strictly constant at the mean is, in truth, already deeply ill. For Canguilhem, health is a creative power—*biological normativity*—defined as **"a margin of tolerance for the inconsistencies of the environment"** (*une marge de tolérance des infidélités du milieu*, Canguilhem, 1943/2009). The environment and history are inevitably "unfaithful": they bring unexpected winters, novel viruses, sudden crises, accidents. To be healthy is to possess internal slack—pulmonary, cardiac, immunological, psychic—that makes it possible to absorb the medium's betrayal without collapsing, instituting new norms of life.

Thirty years later, in systems ecology, Crawford S. Holling (1973) arrived at a sister distinction by separating two concepts of stability in nature. **Engineering resilience** is concerned solely with local efficiency: how fast a system returns to its exact equilibrium point after a small deviation. **Ecological resilience**, by contrast, asks something far deeper: how large a shock can a forest, a lake, or an institution withstand before crossing an invisible boundary and plunging into an irreversible degraded state? Holling showed that policies obsessed with eliminating every natural fluctuation in order to maximize immediate efficiency (such as suppressing every small natural brushfire or exploiting a fishery at its maximum limit) end up destroying the ecological resilience of the whole, preparing an unprecedented catastrophe.

The **vital margin** of Stochology is the methodological and institutional translation of this lesson from Canguilhem and Holling. It teaches any planner, jurist, physician, or economist three practical principles of preservation:

1. **Deliberate containment of exposure under epistemic uncertainty:** even before promising projects, never commit the entirety of one's forces or capital at a level where a sequence of setbacks could destroy the capacity to continue existing (Kelly, 1956; Breiman, 1961), prudently shrinking the exposed fraction when the edge itself is estimated with an epistemic margin and over a finite horizon (Samuelson, 1971, 1979; Baker & McHale, 2013);
2. **Defense of capacity slack against the tyranny of "zero idleness" and accumulated allostatic wear (Kingman, 1961; Scarf, 1960; McEwen, 1998; Scheffer et al., 2009):** in the theory of queueing systems proved by John F. C. Kingman (1961), when a hospital, a court, an electrical grid, or a logistics network is forced to operate near one hundred percent occupancy on ordinary days to "cut idle costs," any tiny fluctuation in case arrivals causes waiting times to grow explosively until total strangulation. Moreover, chronic overload without rest imposes an **allostatic load** (McEwen, 1998) that silently erodes the capacity ceiling and narrows the resilience basin, announcing the approach of collapse through the *critical slowing down* of recovery (Scheffer et al., 2009). Maintaining vacant reserve beds, regulatory buffer stocks of medicine and food (Scarf, 1960), and teams with time to breathe and think is not administrative inefficiency; it is the material price of collective health;
3. **Guaranteeing breathing time in critical networks (Simchi-Levi et al., 2014):** in any infrastructure upon which human life depends, the local reserve available to keep the system functioning after a supply disruption (*Time-to-Survive*) must always exceed the time required to repair the failure or activate an alternative source (*Time-to-Recover*).

## **VI. The Architecture of Judgment Under Turbulence: *Télos*, Moral Luck, Apt Agency, and Institutional Contract**

### **6.1. Tension 7 (First Face) — The Stoic Archer (*Télos* and *Skopós*), Moral Luck, and Why the Margin Is a Moral Duty**

We reach the last and most delicate link of the circuit. Even if we have understood the filters of theory, model, archive, performative reaction, and irreversible time, one truth remains unshakable: **no human caution completely eliminates chance**. The most zealous and up-to-date physician may lose a patient to a fulminant complication; a civil defense agency that evacuated a city on the basis of every meteorological signal may watch the cyclone veer off at the last hour; a reckless manager who ignored every safety warning may go years without suffering a single accident by sheer luck. There arises, then, the seventh constitutive tension: **how can we justly evaluate the merit and responsibility of those who decide under uncertainty, neither worshiping the accidental success of the reckless nor offering a safe-conduct for the negligence of the powerful?**

Ancient philosophy confronted this problem through a luminous distinction formulated within Stoicism, whose historical reconstruction deserves care. In Book III of his treatise *On the Ends of Goods and Evils* (*De Finibus Bonorum et Malorum*, III, 22), **Cicero** sets forth in Latin the famous image of the archer or spear-thrower: the archer must do everything within the reach of his art to aim straight (what Cicero calls the *ultimum* or *extremum bonorum*, the supreme good internal to conduct), even though the material fact of the arrow striking the external target (*propositum*) does not depend on him alone, since a sudden gust of wind may deflect the shaft after it has left the bow. Although Cicero does not cite the Greek author of the image there, the Greek testimonies of **Plutarch** (*On Common Conceptions Against the Stoics*, 1070f–1071e) and **Stobaeus** (*Eclogae*, II, 76, 3–15; Long & Sedley, 1987, 64C–F) preserved the authorship and original technical vocabulary: the distinction belongs to the Stoic philosophers **Antipater of Tarsus** and Panaetius, who separated the **τέλος (*télos*, the internal end—that is, the integral and consummate exercise of the art of aiming itself)** from the **σκοπός (*skopós*, the external target one seeks to hit)**. In Stoic ethics, hitting the *skopós* belongs to the class of so-called **preferred indifferents** (**προηγμένα, *proēgmena***): the serious archer rationally wants and selects hitting the target—anyone who shoots without caring whether he hits is a fraud—yet the moral dignity of the agent resides in the vigilant perfection of preparation and aim (*télos*), which no external gust can steal from him.

In the twentieth century, two great moral philosophers, **Thomas Nagel (1979)** and **Bernard Williams (1981)**, reopened this wound at the heart of modern ethics under the name of **moral luck**. We would like to believe, Nagel and Williams observe, that moral responsibility bears only upon what lay within our control. Yet in the real practice of courts, politics, and everyday life, our judgment is traversed by four forms of luck: *constitutive luck* (the temperament and social conditions in which we were formed), *circumstantial luck* (the fact of living in a season of quiet peace or being placed before a tragic crisis), *causal luck* (the antecedents of our actions), and above all **resultant luck**—the way the external outcome alters the moral weight we assign to the act. Two drivers drive drunk with the exact same degree of recklessness; the first finds the corner empty and receives only a minor traffic fine; the second encounters a pedestrian crossing the street and becomes a killer. In the psychology of everyday judgment, Jonathan Baron and John C. Hershey (1988) experimentally demonstrated how this tendency produces **outcome bias**: when people know the final outcome was good, they judge that the initial decision was wise, even if it was a senseless gamble; when they know the outcome was bad, they condemn the decision-maker, even if he made the most lucid and prudent decision possible with the information available at the time.

How does Stochology resolve the dilemma between process (*télos*) and outcome (*skopós*)? It refuses both vicious extremes: it accepts neither the **ethics of mere subjective intention** (according to which it would suffice for a ruler to say "I meant well" to excuse himself from studying the data and preserving reserves) nor the **tyranny of the brute outcome** (which applauds the lucky speculator and punishes prudence). The theoretical synthesis is found in the virtue epistemology developed by **Ernest Sosa (2007, 2015)**, which distinguishes three rungs in the quality of any human action or judgment:

1. A shot is **accurate** when it simply hits the target, even if by a stroke of blind luck;
2. It is **adroit** when executed with genuine competence, rigorous method, honest consultation of sources, and care;
3. It is **apt** when it achieves accuracy *because* it was adroit—and it reaches the highest plane of **fully apt performance** when the archer possesses a second-order reflective wisdom: the capacity to assess the lighting of the field, the force of the wind, and the limits of his own eyesight in order to decide **when he must lower the bow and abstain from shooting**. Under severe uncertainty, the greatest proof of competence in a scientist, physician, judge, or ruler is knowing how to recognize when the evidence does not yet authorize an irreversible intervention.

There remains, however, a fundamental philosophical question to answer: **why is honestly declaring the epistemic margin and preserving the vital margin a *moral* obligation of justice, rather than merely technical advice for good administration?**

In traditional epistemology—from William Kingdon Clifford's (1877) celebrated essay *The Ethics of Belief* to the contemporary discussions of Jonathan Adler (2002)—the duty to proportion our beliefs to the evidence is usually viewed as a rule of internal intellectual hygiene. If a solitary collector believes, on the basis of a bungled count, that he owns exactly five thousand stamps in an album locked in his bedroom, he commits an arithmetical error, but he practices no immorality against his neighbor. Why, then, is omitting a margin of uncertainty in a technical report or emptying a hospital reserve a properly *moral* wrong?

Stochology's answer rests on the convergence of three contemporary theories: the thesis of **moral and pragmatic encroachment** on knowledge (Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath, 2009), the **moral contractualism of Thomas M. Scanlon (1998)**, and the **ethics of risk of Sven Ove Hansson (2013)**. Fantl and McGrath demonstrated that the level of proof required for someone to have the right to assert *"I know this is safe"* is not an abstract laboratory constant: it rises in proportion to the gravity of the harm that error may cause to practical life. For his part, Scanlon (1998) showed that acting morally is being guided by principles that other affected persons could not reasonably reject, and Hansson (2013) made clear that moral theory cannot treat risk imposed on third parties as a simple mathematical average, because **the person who decides to run the risk is almost never the same person who will suffer the harm if the gamble goes wrong**.

When an expert, an economist, or an algorithm designer hides the width of doubt—presenting an uncertain estimate as if it were an indisputable single number—or when a manager eliminates the safety slack of a public service in the name of average efficiency, he **confiscates from the affected citizens the right to know what danger they are being exposed to**. Hiding the imprecision of the map when someone else is going to walk across the minefield defrauds the minimum conditions of reciprocal respect and democratic deliberation. In Stochology, therefore, the epistemic margin and the vital margin are not footnote technicalities: they are moral guarantees against the arbitrary imposition of risk upon the vulnerable.

### **6.2. Tension 7 (Second Face) — From Individual Conscience to Institutional Design and Contractual Justice**

There is, however, an indispensable step still to take. Aristotle, the Stoics, and Ernest Sosa conceived virtue starting from the figure of the individual. Yet in the modern world, the agent who decides interest rates, a pandemic protocol, a civil defense budget, or a court's triage algorithm is not an isolated individual: it is **collective institutions and organizations**. Relying solely on the moral heroism of an individual civil servant or scientist inside a badly designed institution is sociological naïveté, for perverse structures punish those who speak uncomfortable truths and reward those who produce docile numbers.

To build the bridge between individual ethics and institutional architecture, Stochology draws upon the theory of **group agency** of **Christian List and Philip Pettit (2011)** and the studies of **Miranda Fricker (2007, 2010, 2020)** on **testimonial epistemic injustice** and **institutional epistemic vices**. List and Pettit demonstrate, through the **discursive dilemma**, that a technical board voting by simple majority on the final approval of an operation can approve an unsafe project even when a majority of specialists recognizes the violation of at least one essential safeguard—which is why a rational collective agent requires an **internal constitution** that subordinates the conjunctive decision to safety premises, with a clear division of functions, protected documentary memory, and constitutional veto power for technical audit over political majorities. At the same time, Miranda Fricker (2007) shows how **testimonial injustice**—the systematic credibility discount imposed on the word of frontline operational workers, patients, affected residents, or vulnerable minorities due to status prejudice—is not merely a moral offense, but a **fatal cybernetic blindness**: by silencing or discounting the alarm coming from the ground, the institution delays recognition of collapse beyond the physical time available for recovery.

This means that the personal virtue of Sosa's archer—knowing when to lower the bow when fog prevents a safe shot—must be translated into **constitutional mechanisms inside the organization**. In the organizational cybernetics created by **Stafford Beer (1972)**, no complex organization remains viable and ethical if it depends solely on reports climbing obediently up the normal hierarchical ladder, because each bureaucratic rung tends to filter out bad news in order to please the immediate superior. Beer showed the necessity of two structural devices that Stochology adopts in full: first, the **strict separation between those who build the plan and those who audit it**, through an independent verification channel able to inspect reality on the ground without asking management's permission; second, an **emergency pain or alarm channel (the algedonic signal)** and **automatic suspension clauses (*institutional circuit breakers*)**, capable of immediately halting a policy or automated model as soon as its real errors exceed previously declared safety bounds.

At the same time, we must confront a gravely distorting politico-institutional pathology: the **technocratic alibi**. Time and again, high-ranking officials invoke the fact that they "followed the technical protocol" or "consulted the official model" to wash their hands before predictable tragedies, keeping their offices, wealth, and bonuses intact while the population bears the disaster. The essayist Nassim Nicholas Taleb (2018) rightly denounced this asymmetry under the maxim of **"skin in the game"**—the ancient rule that the builder of a bridge must sleep beneath it. Yet formulated merely as a sweeping slogan, the idea of "skin in the game" runs a grave risk of injustice if we fail to distinguish those who hold power over the structure from those who stand on the operational frontline. If we apply blind punishment to an overworked on-call physician, a public school teacher, or a smallholder farmer when an adverse outcome occurs, we crush precisely the weakest link who did not design the system, while incentivizing frontline professionals to flee from the hardest cases.

To give just and rigorous form to institutional responsibility, Stochology replaces punitive Darwinism with the **economic theory of contracts under moral hazard, limited liability, and multitasking**, developed by **Bengt Holmström (1979)**, **Bengt Holmström and Paul Milgrom (1991)**, and **Robert D. Innes (1990)**, articulating it with the distributive justice of **Alexandra Chouldechova (2017)** and the cognitive protection of the human decision-maker:

1. **Collective protection at the base and an end to the "free option on volatility" at the top (Holmström, 1979; Innes, 1990):** The contract analysis of Holmström (1979) and Innes (1990) allows us to draw a clear dividing line. At the **base of society and organizations**—where operational workers and ordinary citizens stand, possessing limited wealth and no power to decide financial leverage or system capacity margins—justice and efficiency demand **solidaristic insurance, social protection, and limited liability**. At the **decision-making top**—where chief executives, financial managers, and regulatory authorities decide the level of structural risk and the cutting of reserves—there occurs today a scandalous contractual failure proved by Robert Innes (1990): when a top executive receives multimillion-dollar bonuses in the years when a risky gamble pays off and, in the year when the gamble bankrupts the institution, suffers at most dismissal without returning what he gained in previous years, he holds a **free option to gamble on instability**. For him, manufacturing hidden tail risks and destroying the organization's vital margin is highly profitable, because the gain is private and immediate while future ruin is public. Stochological governance demands inverting this logic at the top: tying the compensation and prestige of top decision-makers to long windows of system survival and health, with mandatory holding of rewards (*clawback* escrow) and patrimonial restitution should it emerge that apparent success was obtained at the expense of hollowing out safety margins;
2. **Protecting invisible care against the dictatorship of the single metric (Holmström & Milgrom, 1991):** In their classic study on multitask contracts, Bengt Holmström and Paul Milgrom (1991) provided the exact theoretical explanation for the everyday degradation of hospitals, universities, courts, and public services. Nearly all skilled human work consists of two parts: a part easy to count on spreadsheets (how many ten-minute consultations were performed, how many papers were published, how many rulings were signed) and an essential part that is exceedingly difficult to measure in numbers (the attentive listening that uncovers a rare disease, the ethical formation of the student, prudence in analyzing complex judicial evidence). Holmström and Milgrom proved that when administration creates strong financial incentives or punitive threats tied solely to the easily counted indicator, it rationally destroys dedication to invisible quality. Protecting fully apt agency therefore requires containing the quantifying fury of single targets and preserving the professional autonomy of care;
3. **Political transparency before the Fairness Trilemma (Chouldechova, 2017):** When institutions use statistical models or standardized criteria to classify people across social groups marked by historical inequalities—for instance, in granting pretrial release, allocating credit, or prioritizing social assistance—it is routinely promised that the model will be "technically neutral and fair in every sense." In 2017, the statistician Alexandra Chouldechova proved mathematically why this promise is impossible. Whenever two groups in the population have different real base rates of incidence at the starting line (whether because one group suffers greater economic vulnerability or because it is more heavily policed), **it is algebraically impossible for any decision rule to equalize simultaneously three legitimate criteria of justice**: (a) that a risk score carry the same meaning in both groups (*calibration / predictive parity*); (b) that innocent members of both groups suffer the same proportion of false alarms (*false positive rate balance*); and (c) that true cases in both groups go unassisted or undetected in the same proportion (*false negative rate balance*). Improving one of these criteria mathematically compels sacrificing another. The ethical consequence for Stochology is direct: **there is no purely technical decision under historical inequality**. Every institution has the moral duty to declare publicly which type of error it has chosen to minimize and who is paying the price of that choice;
4. **Defending the human mind against informational drowning (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995; Zheng & Meister, 2025):** Finally, no institutional responsibility is real if the human being charged with deciding on the frontline is cognitively crushed by the technical instrument. Contemporary neurobiological research led by Jieyu Zheng and Markus Meister (2025) shows that although our senses receive millions of stimuli per second, conscious, deliberative human thought operates at an extraordinarily slow and precious biological pace—roughly ten bits of information per second. When a computerized hospital or industrial system fires hundreds of low-reliability sirens and alerts every shift—as Andrew Wong et al. (2021) documented in *JAMA Internal Medicine* when revealing that, in one of the commercial sepsis prediction systems most widely used in U.S. hospitals, nearly ninety percent of triggered alerts were false alarms—the human brain defends itself by shutting down attention. The ethics of aim demands that technical systems serve human judgment rather than sabotage it: rigorously filtering noisy signals and presenting uncertainties in **clear natural frequencies** (for example: *"out of every one hundred cases with this profile, between ten and fifteen develop the complication"*, as demonstrated by Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage, 1995), a format that human intelligence grasps effortlessly and without illusion.

## **VII. Practical Resonances Across Multiple Methods and Reflexive Self-Application**

### **7.1. Stochology as a Transversal Grammar: Dialogue with Five Methodological Families**

Because it is bound neither to a single technical software package nor to a particular database, Stochology offers a **common language of critical dialogue** among specialists who rarely converse across university departments and state agencies. Whether an investigator works with medieval archives, street ethnography, clinical vaccine trials, dam engineering, econometrics, or artificial intelligence, he always traverses the same circuit between world, record, and decision. Let us see how the stochological triad—epistemic margin, vital margin, and institutionally responsible agency—resonates practically across five major methodological families:

1. **In survey sampling, demography, censuses, and large digital archives (observational *Big Data*):**
   - *The habitual trap:* believing that replacing traditional probability surveys with gigantic digital registries (social networks, electronic health records, tax registries, or mobile apps) has eliminated statistical uncertainty.
   - *The stochological transformation:* drawing on Keynes's (1921) distinction regarding evidential weight and Meng's (2018) proof of the Big Data Paradox, the demographer, quantitative sociologist, and public statistician treat the raw size of the database with methodical distrust. Before computing any average, the central question becomes: **who was left out of this archive and why?** The method demands discounting the echo effect of groups and territories (Campbell, 1979; Moulton, 1986) and, whenever there is reason to suspect that social or economic vulnerability itself prevented registration (Rubin, 1976; Little & Rubin, 1987/2002), refusing to publish a falsely exact single number, publishing instead Manski's (2003, 2013) partial identification intervals that clearly show society the size of the shadow the official registry fails to reach;
2. **In experimental, quasi-experimental, and clinical and public-policy evaluation methods (clinical trials, difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, and regression discontinuities):**
   - *The habitual trap:* stopping a study as soon as a favorable result appears, ignoring how participants who drop out midstream alter the meaning of the comparison (the classic problem of social program evaluation exposed by Robert LaLonde, 1986), and assuming that a project that worked in a small controlled sample will produce the exact same effect when enacted as national law.
   - *The stochological transformation:* the policy evaluator or clinical researcher incorporates three practical safeguards. First, he seals his analysis plan in advance and uses continuous sequential validation methods (Shafer, 2021; Grünwald, de Heide & Koolen, 2024; Ramdas et al., 2023) that make it possible to monitor data over time without manufacturing false positives, declaring all tested variables (Wang & Ramdas, 2022). Second, faced with the inevitable loss of participants over the course of the study, he unites Manski (2003) and Mayo (2018): he computes effect bounds accounting for selective attrition (Lee, 2009) and applies severe testing to the most conservative boundary of that interval, asking whether the guaranteed minimum benefit still outweighs the cost of the intervention. Third, when moving from pilot study to public scale, he investigates whether the policy itself will trigger strategic market or behavioral reactions (Lucas, 1976; Pearl, 2009; Peters, Bühlmann & Meinshausen, 2016; Perdomo et al., 2020) that nullify the initial achievement;
3. **In operations research, infrastructure planning, ecology, and extreme-risk management:**
   - *The habitual trap:* sizing hospitals, power grids, dams, food reserves, financial portfolios, or environmental harvesting quotas by the "historical average," viewing any idle reserve as inefficiency to be cut.
   - *The stochological transformation:* the planner and the engineer abandon the dictatorship of the single average. Recognizing the curved asymmetry of harm (Jensen, 1906), the presence of extremes that invalidate ordinary variance (Hill, 1975), and the inevitable discrepancy between simulator and terrain (Kennedy & O'Hagan, 2001), they explicitly design the **vital margin** (Canguilhem, 1943/2009; Holling, 1973). This means maintaining deliberate idle capacity to prevent queue explosions (Kingman, 1961), conserving regulatory buffer stocks sized for severe crises (Scarf, 1960), ensuring that every critical node holds local reserves to survive longer than relief takes to arrive (Simchi-Levi et al., 2014), limiting leverage so that temporal volatility does not lead to irreversible ruin (Itô, 1951; Kelly, 1956; Peters, 2019), and subjecting every choice to the global safety floor of Wald (1945) and Sniedovich (2007, 2012);
4. **In predictive modeling, machine learning, and algorithmic systems governance (medical, judicial, labor, and administrative decision support):**
   - *The habitual trap:* judging an algorithm solely by its abstract accuracy rate on a frozen test set, deploying it as a supposedly neutral black box that floods human operators with alerts and reproduces historical inequalities.
   - *The stochological transformation:* developing and auditing algorithms ceases to be a purely computational task and is governed instead as a sociotechnical intervention. The model is required to prove out-of-sample its genuine calibration and discriminative resolution (Dimitriadis, Gneiting & Jordan, 2021), separate self-fulfilling prophecy from genuine social improvement (MacKenzie, 2006; Perdomo et al., 2020), make explicit and open to public debate the unavoidable conflict in error distribution across unequal groups (Chouldechova, 2017), filter its alerts to respect the pace of human consciousness (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995; Wong et al., 2021; Zheng & Meister, 2025), and operate under independent oversight endowed with the authority to shut it down as soon as its premises degrade;
5. **In qualitative, historiographical, clinical, legal, and ethnographic methods (*documentary source criticism*, *process tracing*, *case studies*, and *institutional ethnography*):**
   - *The habitual trap:* imagining that rigor under uncertainty is the monopoly of numbers—leaving the humanities and clinical practice hostages either to an inferiority complex before statistics or to impressionistic narratives that always find a rhetorical way to confirm themselves.
   - *The stochological transformation:* Stochology demonstrates that qualitative and interpretive methods play an irreplaceable role of equal theoretical dignity at every stage of the circuit, precisely because they are the guardians of what numbers cannot reach—Adorno's (1966/2009) **Non-Identical** and Georgescu-Roegen's (1971) **dialectical concepts**:
     - *In the passage from world to archive:* before any statistician can compute a bound for missing data, it is the **historian**, the **anthropologist**, the **field sociologist**, or the **social worker** who discovers that the silence exists. Historical source criticism and ethnography are the instruments that reveal how official archives were constructed, which voices were silenced, and which sufferings were never given a name on state spreadsheets;
     - *In the passage between hypothesis and proof:* the method of **process tracing** in political science and history, legal investigation, and **clinical reasoning** in medicine (direct heir to the Hippocratic and Galenic *sēmeîa* and the *mêtis* studied by Detienne and Vernant, 1974) do not work by counting thousands of superficial cases, but by assessing **Keynes's (1921) evidential weight** in depth. A single authentic historical document proving a secret order, or a single pathognomonic clinical sign observed at the bedside, possesses greater logical force to decide between two rival explanations than a million vague correlations. Conversely, Stochology also applies to qualitative research the demand of Lakatos (1970) and Mayo (2018): the qualitative researcher must honestly declare in advance what kind of documentary trace, testimony, or field observation would suffice to refute his thesis, refusing the temptation to select only those quotations and episodes that illustrate his prior opinion;
     - *In the passage from decision back to social life:* only the **ethnography of institutions** and qualitative listening to workers and citizens can see from the inside how the pressure of arithmetical targets corrupts the meaning of work (Goodhart, 1975; Strathern, 1997; MacKenzie, 2006), how obsession with indicators destroys invisible care in multitask distortion (Holmström & Milgrom, 1991), and how bureaucratic indifference (*insouciance*, Cassam, 2019; Fricker, 2020) hides behind impeccable statistical reports.

### **7.2. Reflexive Self-Application: Stochology Under Goodhart's Law and Its Conditions of Refutability**

An essay that denounces how theories armor themselves and how institutional metrics degenerate under control pressure would fail in its own duty of coherence if it ended by presenting Stochology as a recipe immune to deviation. A final exercise in **critical reflexivity** is therefore mandatory: what would happen if an institution turned the three pillars of Stochology itself into isolated bureaucratic targets, subjecting them to the pressure of Goodhart's Law? And what facts or demonstrations would refute the theses of this essay?

Let us observe first how each of the three stochological safeguards can degenerate if detached from the other two, and what internal brake prevents that corruption:

1. **Degeneration of the Epistemic Margin — *Skeptical Inflation and the Alibi of Indecision*:** Imagine that a technical agency begins rewarding its analysts solely for "never missing a forecast"—that is, for guaranteeing that the real outcome always falls inside the announced interval. The immediate opportunistic response will be to widen the margin of uncertainty artificially until it covers almost everything that could possibly happen (for instance, forecasting that inflation or hospital demand will lie "between zero and one hundred"). Such a margin will never be refuted by the facts, yet it is completely useless for guiding action and serves only to shield the bureaucracy from accountability ("we warned that anything was possible"). **Stochology's internal brake:** in the theory of judgment evaluation (Dimitriadis, Gneiting & Jordan, 2021), calibration honesty is inseparable from **discriminative resolution**. A lazily inflated interval destroys the informative resolution of the study. The ethics of aim does not ask for maximum doubt, but for *just doubt*: the most informative interval that the evidence permits sustaining without inventing certainties;
2. **Degeneration of the Vital Margin — *Regressive Hoarding and Bureaucratic Selfishness*:** Imagine that preserving financial reserves, buffer stocks, or operational slack is turned into an absolute dogma, blind to immediate human needs. A ministry or public fund might deny urgent relief to starving families or refuse treatment to present patients under the accounting pretext of "not touching the future safety cushion," or a corporation might use the discourse of "vital slack" to accumulate idle privileges at the top. **Stochology's internal brake:** the vital margin exists to serve life, never the reverse. As Scarf's (1960) inventory theory and Samuelson's (1971, 1979) intertemporal analysis demonstrate, every reserve has an upper bound dictated by **social opportunity cost**: withholding resources beyond the boundary necessary for systemic safety while real people suffer irreversible deprivation today frontally violates the very reason for being of the vital margin;
3. **Degeneration of Agency and Governance — *Procedural Theater and Paralysis in Emergency*:** Imagine, finally, that the requirement of pre-registration, independent audit committees, and documentary verification turns into a slow, formalistic paper labyrinth. Faced with an imminent flood or an explosive epidemic, officials might fold their arms waiting for months of bureaucratic paperwork, fulfilling the paper ritual to perfection while losing the vital instant of action (*kairós*). **Stochology's internal brake:** institutional procedure is always subordinated to the physical condition of survival in time (Simchi-Levi et al., 2014). When the time required to complete a detailed audit threatens to exceed the time the community or patient has left to survive, the reflective wisdom of fully apt agency itself (Sosa, 2015) commands suspending ordinary ritual and immediately triggering a simple, direct rule of maximum protection for the vulnerable (Wald's *Maximin*, 1945) until the immediate danger subsides.

Finally, in strict respect for Karl Popper's (1934/2002) principle of refutability and Deborah Mayo's (2018) severity criterion, every theory that claims substantive content must declare the conditions under which it would accept having been refuted. The fundamental theses of Stochology would be refuted if four conditions were demonstrated:

- **First refutability condition (on the plane of the archive):** the stochological argument regarding the epistemic margin would be refuted if it were proved that merely increasing the quantity of data in non-probabilistic, biased archives makes error disappear by itself, without any need to correct the selection mechanism or declare partial identification intervals when missingness depends on the very vulnerability under study;
- **Second refutability condition (on the plane of the model):** the prohibition against planning by the isolated average would be refuted if, in systems subject to increasing (convex) harm or heavy-tailed extreme events, sizing services and reserves by the simple arithmetic mean produced, over time and out of the initial sample, the same safety and the same human cost as sizing attentive to fluctuations and tails;
- **Third refutability condition (on the plane of action and time):** the distinction between performative stability and social optimality, as well as the defense of the vital margin against non-ergodicity, would be refuted if the repeated application of indicators under punitive pressure ceased to provoke strategic distortions in human behavior, or if agents subjected to cumulative risks before irreversible barriers of bankruptcy or death could survive in the long run while guided solely by the abstract average of parallel universes;
- **Fourth refutability condition (the duty of public memory of one's own errors):** in the concrete exercise of any science or institution, Stochology refutes itself the instant it hides its own failed conjectures. For this reason, the method demands always separating the phase of constructing an idea from the phase of testing it on virgin ground, maintaining a **public registry of hypotheses that failed confirmation**. Recognizing openly and on the record when a risk forecast, a reserve rule, or a causal hypothesis was defeated by experience is not a weakness of the discipline, but living proof that it refuses *ad hoc* patching and remains faithful to reality.

## **VIII. Conclusion: The Seven Tensions and the Integrity of Aim**

Throughout this essay, we have sought to reconstruct and ground **Stochology** as a discipline capable of being read, understood, and practiced by specialists from any province of knowledge—from the humanities, law, and social sciences to the medical, natural, economic, and mathematical sciences. Starting from the ancient Greek image of conjectural aim (*stókhos*), we saw that the challenge of judging and acting when the target is shrouded in fog demands viewing as a single circuit the passages between the **historical and material world**, the **archive and model** that represent it, and the **institution** that decides and distributes risks.

Without promising a magic formula that would erase the irreducible distance between our concepts and the rebellious richness of things (Adorno's *Non-Identical*), we traversed **three filters and seven constitutive tensions**: we saw how situational interest demands Cromwell's anti-dogmatic openness and the memory of failed attempts; how the tendency of theories toward self-protection demands uniting Mayo's severe testing with Manski's honest intervals; how the simplifying violence of averages demands recognizing Jensen's convexity of harm and Hill's extremes; how the illusion of gigantic archives demands recovering Keynes's evidential weight and Meng's diagnosis of the silence of the excluded; how society's living reaction to control targets demands distinguishing performative accommodation from genuine substantive justice; how the irreversible arrow of Georgescu-Roegen's entropic time and barriers of no return demand the vital margin of Canguilhem and Holling; and how the abyss between the care of the art (*télos*) and the caprice of moral luck demands institutions endowed with independent brakes, respect for the limits of human attention, and contracts that protect the vulnerable at the base while holding decision-makers at the top accountable.

The three safeguards that emerge from this crossing sustain one another like the stones of a single arch. Where the **epistemic margin** is lacking, material reserves are built blindly upon an illusory certainty; where the **vital margin** is lacking, the most brilliant intellectual lucidity watches helplessly as collapse strikes at the first severe shock along the way; where **fully apt agency under institutional governance** is lacking, both prudent doubt and the common reserve are pillaged by short-term opportunism and converted into suffering for those who had no voice in the decision. To declare honestly the width of what we still do not know, to preserve generously the material reserves that life needs in order to withstand the inconsistencies of the environment, and to answer in solidarity for the impact of the arrow once released: such is the proper task of Stochology and the human meaning of the ethics of aim.
